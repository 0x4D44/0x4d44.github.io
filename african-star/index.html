    A map from another era. The game dates from 1951 and retains several historical place names and a treasure-hunt premise shaped by European adventure fiction. This edition uses original, non-caricatured art, replaces the old “robber” drawing with a leopard, and names the Bight of Benin while explaining the original rule in context.

    How to play

    1

    Search

    Start in Tangier or Cairo with £300. Everyone throws first; play proceeds from highest roll to lowest, with tied expeditions re-rolling for their places.

    2

    Turn a disk

    On arrival, pay £100 to turn the disk immediately or wait. From your next turn there, a 4–6 turns it for free; you may still pay or travel on.

    3

    Race home

    The finder carries the Star. Once it is found, any later horseshoe also becomes a race piece. First to either starting city wins.

    RoadFreeRoll and move. You may stop early at a city.
    Ship£100Pay on boarding, roll and stop at the next port. With no cash, move exactly 2 sea spaces without rolling.
    Flight£300Move directly to the next linked flight city; no die roll.

    Places with special rules

    Cape Town: first expedition to arrive receives £500. Gold Coast: any gem found there pays double. Sahara & Saint Helena: land on a ringed trap and, on later turns, roll for a 1 or 2. A successful escape uses that turn; movement resumes on the next.

    Kari Mannerla designed Afrikan tähti as a nineteen-year-old in Finland. First published in 1951, its exceptionally simple loop — choose a route, roll, reveal — turned it into a family fixture across the Nordic countries.

    The board is also unmistakably a product of its time. Its geography mixes cities, regions and colonial-era names; its fantasy of extracting gems from a continent was inherited from European adventure stories, not from African perspectives.

    This browser edition treats the object as both game and artefact. The route structure, economy and 30-disk mix remain recognisable. The illustration is entirely new, human caricatures have been omitted, the old coast label is not printed on the board, and a short note is kept visible rather than pretending the context is not there.

    ABOUT THIS EDITION

    A board game, rebuilt as a living map.

    This is an unofficial, non-commercial adaptation made for the 0x4D44 Almanac. It uses a deterministic, tested rules core, local-only saving, synthesised Web Audio and an original mid-century travel-poster board drawn entirely in SVG and CSS.

    No commercial scans, logos or illustrations are included. The historical note is part of the design, not an afterthought.
