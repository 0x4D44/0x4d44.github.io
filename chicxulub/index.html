A morning in the late Cretaceous

An ordinary Tuesday, and then it wasn't

It is spring in the Northern Hemisphere, and the world is warm and green. Flowering plants have spread across the continents; Triceratops browses the coastal forests of what will one day be Montana; Tyrannosaurus rex is the apex predator of the age. In the seas swim ammonites, mosasaurs and great marine reptiles. This world is not decaying or exhausted. It is thriving. It has been the age of dinosaurs for over 165 million years.

Somewhere out beyond the orbit of Mars, a rock roughly ten to fourteen kilometres across has been falling toward the inner Solar System for a very long time. It is a fragment of the early Solar System — a piece of the same rubble that built the planets — and it is now on a collision course with the Earth. It is moving at around twenty kilometres every second: sixty times faster than a rifle bullet.

When it reaches the top of the atmosphere it will cross the whole sky in a fraction of a second. There is no warning. There is no defence. In the geological record, the boundary it leaves behind is a line just a few centimetres thick — and on one side of that line is the Mesozoic, and on the other side is everything that came after, including us.

This page is an interactive almanac of that impact — the Chicxulub event that closed the Cretaceous. How big is a "mountain-sized" rock?

The scale of the thing

We say "asteroid" and picture a pebble. The Chicxulub impactor was somewhere between 10 and 14 km across — big enough that when its leading edge struck the sea floor, its trailing edge was still up at the cruising altitude of an airliner. Drag the slider to see it against things you know.

The years the sun went out

Impact winter

The crater lay in sulfur-rich rock. The impact flung billions of tonnes of soot from global wildfires, pulverised rock, and sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, where they lingered for years. Sunlight at the surface collapsed; photosynthesis all but stopped; the land froze. This is the killer that reached everywhere, long after the fires died. How we know · reading the rock

The line in the cliff

For a century the extinction was a mystery. Then in 1980 the physicist Luis Alvarez and his geologist son Walter found something strange in a clay layer that turns up at the boundary all over the world: a spike of iridium, a metal rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. The spike was the fingerprint of an impact. Eleven years later the crater itself was confirmed, buried under the Yucatán. Drag down through the rock to read it.

Below boundary

The boundary carries more than iridium. It holds shocked quartz, grains fractured by pressures only an impact can produce; tiny glass spherules, droplets of rock melted and flung skyward that rained back down; and a layer of soot from a world on fire. Above the line, the fossils change completely. It is the same story on every continent, and at the bottom of every ocean.

The reckoning · who died, who lived

The sorting

Roughly three-quarters of all species vanished. But the extinction was not random. To live, it helped to be small, to be able to hide, to eat detritus or seeds rather than fresh leaves, and to breed fast. Tap a group to see how it fared, and why.

What grew back

A world made empty, and then remade

For a few thousand years the recovering world was ruled by ferns — the first green things to colonise scorched ground — and by the small, the burrowing and the buried. The great reptilian dynasties were gone, and with them the ceiling that had held mammals small and nocturnal for a hundred million years. Into that empty world, over the millions of years that followed, mammals radiated: into the whales, the bats, the horses, the primates. One branch of those primates is reading this sentence.

The asteroid did not just end a world. By clearing the stage so completely, it made ours possible. We are, quite literally, the descendants of the survivors — the shrew-like things that were small enough, and lucky enough, to still be breathing when the sky finally cleared.

The crater is still there. It is called Chicxulub, after the town near its centre, and it is about 180 kilometres wide, buried under a kilometre of younger rock and the shallow waters of the Gulf. You cannot see it from the ground. But a ring of sinkholes — cenotes — traces its buried rim across the Yucatán, water finding the fractured stone. Sixty-six million years on, the wound has not entirely closed.
