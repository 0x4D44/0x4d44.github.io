Forced induction: stuffing more air in than the engine can breathe.

An engine's power is limited by how much air it can swallow — burn more fuel and you need more oxygen to burn it with. Forced induction cheats the limit: it pumps dense air into the cylinders, so a small engine breathes like a big one. The turbocharger does it almost for free, by stealing energy from the exhaust — but nothing is truly free, and the bill is paid in lag.

The air limit

An engine is an air pump that happens to make power.

Every power stroke burns the fuel that the trapped air can oxidise — no more. The chemically correct (stoichiometric) ratio for petrol is about 14.7 kg of air to 1 kg of fuel. So the real limit on power is the mass of air you can pack into the cylinder each cycle. A naturally aspirated engine is capped by atmospheric pressure and its own breathing. There are only two ways past: make the engine bigger, or make the air denser.

Brake power, in terms of air P ≈ ( ṁair / AFR ) · Qfuel · ηbrake   →   double the air density, you can roughly double the fuel — and the power

Forced induction raises ρair, the intake charge density. A 2-litre engine at 1 bar of boost ingests roughly the air of a 4-litre engine — "downsizing" with a turbo is exactly this trade: a small, light, low-friction engine that breathes like a large one when you ask it to.

The free lunch (almost)

The turbocharger: two wheels on one shaft.

A turbocharger is two impellers on a common shaft. Hot, fast exhaust gas — normally thrown away — spins the turbine wheel on its way out; the shaft drives the compressor wheel on the intake side, which crams dense air into the engine. It recovers energy that would otherwise be wasted as noise and heat, so it costs the crankshaft almost nothing. The catch is that it only works once there's enough exhaust flow to spin it — and that takes a moment.

Turbo lag: the boost arrives late.

Stab the throttle and the turbo doesn't respond instantly. The turbine has to accelerate its own rotating inertia up to speed before it makes meaningful boost, and the only thing accelerating it is exhaust flow — which is itself weak until the engine is already making more power. It is a chicken-and-egg delay: turbo lag. Press throttle and watch boost chase demand; shrink the turbo or add a supercharger to close the gap.

Taming boost

Wastegate, variable geometry & blow-off.

Wastegate

Left alone, a turbine spins faster the harder you drive until boost destroys the engine. The wastegate is a valve that bleeds exhaust around the turbine once target boost is reached, capping shaft speed. It is the throttle on the turbine side.

Variable geometry (VGT)

Moving vanes in the turbine housing change the gas angle and effective area: closed-down at low speed to spool fast, opened-up at high speed to flow freely. A VGT gives one turbo the manners of a small one and a big one — common on diesels, where exhaust is cooler.

Blow-off / recirc valve

Lift off the throttle and the compressor suddenly pushes against a closed butterfly — the flow stalls and reverses, hammering the wheel (surge). A blow-off valve vents that pressure spike (the classic "psssh"), protecting the turbo and keeping it spinning for the next squeeze.

More than one

Sequential, parallel & twin-scroll.

If one turbo can't be both fast-spooling and high-flowing, use more than one — or feed one more cleverly.

ArrangementHow it worksBuys you

The modern answer to lag

The trend is small twin turbos with twin-scroll housings and, increasingly, an electric pre-spool. Each attacks the inertia problem from a different side — less mass to accelerate, cleaner pulses to accelerate it with, or an electric motor that doesn't wait for exhaust at all. Cooling the charge

Why boost needs an intercooler.

Compressing air heats it (the same adiabatic law that heats the diesel's charge in Ch. 02). Hot air is less dense, which fights the very thing the turbo is for — and hotter intake charge brings a petrol engine closer to knock. An intercooler (charge-air cooler) sits between compressor and engine and sheds that heat, recovering density and detonation margin. On a hard-working engine it can be worth a significant slice of the boost's benefit.

Density gained by intercooling ρ = p / (R · T) → drop charge temperature from 120 °C to 50 °C at the same pressure and you regain ≈ 18% density (and the fuel to match)
The other pump

Superchargers & turbo-compounding.

Supercharger — driven by the crank

A supercharger is a compressor driven mechanically off the crankshaft (belt or gear), so it makes boost instantly and in exact proportion to engine speed — no lag at all. The price is that it consumes crankshaft power to run, so it is less efficient than a turbo. Roots and twin-screw blowers are the classic drag-strip and muscle-car choice; centrifugal types resemble a belt-driven turbo compressor.

Turbo-compounding — give the exhaust back

A power turbine downstream of the main turbo geared back to the crankshaft recovers still more exhaust energy as shaft work, rather than boost. The Wright R-3350 Turbo-Compound and modern heavy-truck and marine diesels use it to claw back a few percent of efficiency — the same idea as F1's MGU-H, which turns that recovered energy into electricity.
