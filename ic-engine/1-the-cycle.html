The cycle: four events, in one bore.

Every piston engine is a loop. Draw in a charge, squeeze it, burn it, throw it out — and the only stroke that pays is the third. Everything clever in engine design is about getting that one stroke to feed the other three smoothly.

The problem

Fire is fast and violent. A wheel wants steady and slow.

Burning a petrol–air mixture releases its energy in a few milliseconds, as a sharp rise in pressure. A road wheel or a propeller, by contrast, wants a smooth, continuous torque. The engine's job is to act as a buffer and a converter between these two worlds: to catch each violent expansion, harvest it through a piston and crank, and store enough of it to carry the mechanism through to the next one.

It does this by running the same trapped mass of gas around a repeating thermodynamic cycle. In the four-stroke (Otto/Diesel) cycle, the crank turns twice for each firing; in the two-stroke (Clerk) cycle, once. The price of admission for every cycle is the same: you must first get fresh charge in, and last get burnt gas out — the "breathing" that limits every engine ever built.

A definition worth fixing

A stroke is one full travel of the piston, top to bottom or bottom to top — exactly 180° of crank rotation. TDC (top dead centre) and BDC (bottom dead centre) are the turning points where the piston momentarily stops. The whole study comes back to what is happening at, and between, these two points.

The four-stroke cycle

Two turns of the crank, one payday.

0° → 180°

1 · Intake (induction)

Piston descends from TDC; the inlet valve is open. The growing volume drops cylinder pressure below atmospheric and fresh charge — air, or air plus fuel — is pushed in by the outside air. Breathing here sets the engine's volumetric efficiency, and is exactly what a turbocharger later attacks.

180° → 360°

2 · Compression

Both valves shut; the rising piston squeezes the charge into the clearance volume. Temperature and pressure climb steeply (T ∝ V1−γ). The compression ratio set here fixes the cycle's thermal efficiency — and, in a petrol engine, how close you dare run to knock.

360° → 540°

3 · Power (expansion)

Just before TDC the charge is ignited — by spark, or by the heat of compression in a diesel. Peak pressure (80–200 bar) drives the piston down: this is the only stroke that does net work. The expanding gas cools as it gives up its energy to the crank.

540° → 720°

4 · Exhaust

The exhaust valve opens; the rising piston sweeps the spent gas out. A well-designed engine times the valves so that the outgoing exhaust helps drag in the next intake charge — the overlap around TDC.

Net cycle work — the indicator loop Wnet = ∮ p dV = (work of power + intake strokes) − (work of compression + exhaust strokes)

The three non-firing strokes are paid for out of the energy banked from the last firing — temporarily, by the flywheel (Ch. 04). A single-cylinder engine therefore delivers torque in violent gulps; adding cylinders, phased around the cycle, is how you fill in the gaps.

Breathing

Valve timing: the cycle doesn't start and stop on the dot.

Gas has inertia. By the time the piston reaches BDC the incoming charge is still moving, so the inlet valve is held open well after BDC to keep packing it in (ram effect). Likewise the exhaust valve opens before BDC, while there is still pressure to help blow the cylinder clear. Around TDC between exhaust and intake, both valves are briefly open together — the valve overlap — letting the departing exhaust scavenge the chamber and pull in fresh charge.

Why variable valve timing exists

Fixed valve timing is always a compromise: lots of overlap helps top-end power but spoils a smooth idle and low-speed torque. Variable valve timing (and lift) lets a modern engine have a clean, narrow-overlap idle and a wide-open, free-breathing top end from the same hardware — a recurring theme in Ch. 02's ignition and Ch. 06's boosting.

The two-stroke cycle

One power stroke every revolution — at a price.

The two-stroke folds intake and exhaust into the ends of the power and compression strokes, doing away with separate breathing strokes — and often with the valve gear entirely. As the piston nears BDC it uncovers an exhaust port and a transfer port in the cylinder wall; fresh charge, pre-pressurised in the crankcase below the piston, rushes up through the transfer port and scavenges the burnt gas out. The piston rises, covers the ports, compresses, and fires again. Toggle the model above to two-stroke to watch the ports do the work the valves used to.

What you gain

Twice the firing frequency for a given speed → high power-to-weight; mechanical simplicity (no valve train in the classic crankcase-scavenged design); and it works in any orientation — hence chainsaws and outboards. Large uniflow two-stroke diesels are the most thermally efficient engines ever built.

What you pay

Scavenging is imperfect: fresh charge and exhaust briefly share the cylinder, so in a carburetted petrol two-stroke some unburnt fuel escapes — poor economy and dirty emissions. Crankcase scavenging means oil is mixed with the fuel. The cylinder also runs hotter, with less time to cool.

The two-stroke is not a crude version of the four-stroke — it is a different bargain. At one extreme it powers a disposable strimmer; at the other, the 14-cylinder Wärtsilä-Sulzer marine engines that move container ships are uniflow two-stroke diesels, and among the most efficient heat engines on Earth.
