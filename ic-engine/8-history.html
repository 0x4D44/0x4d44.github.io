History: a century and a half of refining fire.

The internal combustion engine was not invented once. It was argued into existence over decades — Otto's four-stroke, Diesel's compression ignition, the turbo, electronic injection — each step trading a problem solved for a new one created. The brain catches up to the machine

Modern ignition & injection strategy.

For most of its life the engine was controlled by mechanical guesswork — a distributor's centrifugal weights guessing ignition advance, a carburettor's venturi guessing fuelling. The microprocessor replaced guessing with measurement. A modern engine control unit (ECU) decides, for every single firing, exactly when to inject, how much, and when to light it — reading a dozen sensors hundreds of times a second.

Mapped & knock-corrected spark

Ignition timing is looked up from a 3-D map (load × rpm) and then trimmed in real time: a knock sensor listens for detonation and retards the spark a degree at a time until it stops, letting the engine run as close to the efficiency edge as conditions allow.

Direct injection & multiple events

High-pressure direct injection sprays fuel straight into the cylinder, and can split one charge into several injections per cycle — a small pilot to soften diesel combustion noise, a main event, and a late post-injection to heat the catalyst or burn off soot.

Closed-loop everything

An oxygen (lambda) sensor in the exhaust lets the ECU hold the mixture exactly at stoichiometric for the catalytic converter; variable valve timing, boost, and EGR are all servo-controlled the same way — measure, correct, repeat.

Why this matters

Everything earlier in this study — compression ratio limits, knock, balance, breathing, boost, even oil temperature — is now actively managed by software many times per second. The modern engine makes more power, burns less fuel and emits a tiny fraction of the pollution of its 1970s ancestor from the same thermodynamic cycle, almost entirely because of control.

A definition people get wrong

Torque, power, and why they're not the same thing.

Torque is the twisting effort at the crankshaft — force times the radius it acts at. Power is the rate of doing work: torque times rotational speed. An engine can make big torque slowly (a ship's diesel) or modest torque very fast (an F1 V10) and arrive at similar power. The relationship is exact and worth carrying for life:

Featured engine · I

Sulzer 12LDA28C — the heart of the Class 47.

If one engine represents the workhorse era of British diesel traction, it is this one. The Sulzer 12LDA28C powered the BR Class 47 — at 512 built, the most numerous mainline diesel locomotive in British history, and a class still earning its keep some sixty years on. It is a wonderful example of the twin-bank layout (Ch. 05): essentially two straight-sixes sharing one crankcase, with two parallel crankshafts geared together to a single output.

ConfigurationTwin-bank, 12-cyl (2 × inline-6)
CycleFour-stroke diesel, turbocharged + intercooled
Bore × stroke280 mm × 360 mm
Swept volume~22 L/cyl → ~264 L total
Output2,750 bhp @ 800 rpm
Derated to2,580 bhp (from mid-1960s)
Lineage12LDA28A (2,300) → B (2,500) → C
Built under licenceVickers, Barrow-in-Furness

The "C" was wrung from the earlier "B" by fitting strengthened connecting rods and pistons, larger intercoolers, and raising engine speed from 750 to 800 rpm. That last 10% is also where the trouble lay: at the highest rating the cyclic, shock-laden life of rail service (very different from the steady marine duty the engine descended from) cracked crankcases on around a hundred locomotives. BR's fix was the most instructive decision in the whole class's history — they derated it to 2,580 bhp. Reducing the stress bought reliability with almost no loss of useful performance, and turned a troubled engine into a sixty-year success. It is Ch. 04's lesson about stress and Ch. 07's about fatigue, written in locomotives.

Featured engine · II

Napier Deltic — the triangle that howled.

We met the Napier Deltic's extraordinary geometry in Ch. 05; here is the engine in service. Conceived for fast naval craft — where its power-to-weight and the ability to be lifted out and replaced as a unit mattered more than fuel economy — it found lasting fame in the BR Class 55 "Deltic", which used two of these 18-cylinder opposed-piston two-stroke diesels to produce 3,300 bhp and haul the East Coast Main Line expresses for two decades.

Type (D18-25)18-cyl opposed-piston 2-stroke diesel
LayoutTriangular — 3 banks, 3 crankshafts
Pistons36 (two per cylinder, no head)
Output (per engine)~1,650 bhp @ 1,500 rpm
Class 55 loco2 engines → 3,300 bhp
AlsoNavy MTBs, minesweepers; "Baby Deltic" T9-29

Why two smaller screaming engines instead of one big slow one? Power density and serviceability. A Deltic could be unbolted and swapped in hours, keeping the locomotive in traffic while the engine went to the works — a maintenance philosophy from its naval roots. The price was a thirsty, complex, highly-stressed engine with a fearsome appetite for skilled attention, and an unforgettable hard, whistling exhaust note from firing 18 cylinders every revolution. Only 22 Class 55s were ever built, yet they are among the most beloved locomotives in British history — the clearest case in this study of a layout chosen for power-to-weight over all else.

Featured engine · III

The Formula 1 V10 — the cycle taken to its limit.

At the opposite extreme from a locomotive diesel sits the engine many consider the most extreme expression of the reciprocating IC engine ever raced: the 3.0-litre Formula 1 V10 of roughly 1995–2005. Where the Sulzer makes its power from sheer size at 800 rpm, the V10 makes comparable power from almost nothing — three litres — by doing the cycle astonishingly fast.

Configuration90° V10, naturally aspirated
Displacement3.0 L (~300 cc/cyl)
Peak speed~18,000–19,000 rpm
Power~900+ bhp (~300 bhp/litre)
Mass~95 kg
Piston accel.~10,000 g at the crown

Every theme of this study is at its breaking point here. The reciprocating forces of Ch. 04 reach ~10,000 g, demanding exotic pneumatic valve springs because steel ones cannot follow the cam. Breathing (Ch. 01) is everything, so the engine lives at wide-open valve overlap and screams. Balance: ten cylinders give an inherently good primary balance and a sound no other count makes. The V10 was the perfect compromise of the era between the smoothness and power of a V12 and the lighter weight and lower friction of a V8 — which is exactly why the rules eventually mandated it, then replaced it with a 2.4 V8, then a turbo-hybrid V6, each time trading spectacle for efficiency. The V10's wail is the sound of the Otto cycle at its absolute limit.

The long view

Where it goes next.

After 150 years the IC engine is no longer the obvious future of personal transport — the electric motor of its sibling study is quieter, simpler and cleaner at the point of use. But the engine is far from finished. It is being pushed to extraordinary efficiencies as a hybrid range-extender running at one optimal point; it is learning to burn hydrogen and carbon-neutral synthetic and bio-fuels; and in the places where energy density is everything — ships, heavy haulage, aviation, standby power, and the locomotives this study keeps returning to — it will be turning cranks for a long time yet.

The thread through all eight chapters

From the single cylinder of the contents page to the F1 V10, every engine in this study is the same idea under different pressures: catch a controlled explosion, harvest it through a piston and crank, and smooth it into useful rotating power — while fighting the forces, the heat and the wear that the explosion itself creates. Everything else is engineering judgement about which compromise to accept.
