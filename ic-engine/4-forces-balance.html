Forces & balance: the war against your own moving parts.

Every piston is a hammer thrown up and down hundreds of times a second. The gas does useful work — but the dead weight of the reciprocating parts shakes the engine, and the firing pulses arrive in lumps. This chapter is about turning a bag of hammer-blows into smooth, quiet torque.

The root of all vibration

Gas does the work. Inertia sends the bill.

Two quite different forces act through the crank. The first is the gas force — useful, the thing you bought the engine for. The second is the inertia force of accelerating the piston and rod up and down: every stroke they are flung from rest at a dead-centre to full speed and back to rest again, and that acceleration needs force — force the engine structure has to react against.

The piston's acceleration isn't a clean sine wave, because the connecting rod is finite in length. The exact result, with crank radius r, rod length L, and ratio λ = r/L, is:

Piston acceleration (≈ to 2nd order) a ≈ r ω² ( cos θ + λ cos 2θ )

That tidy expression hides the whole story of engine balance. The cos θ term repeats once per revolution — the primary. The λ cos 2θ term repeats twice per revolution — the secondary. The reciprocating inertia force is just mass × this acceleration, and it is the enemy we must arrange to cancel.

Two harmonics

Primary and secondary forces.

1st order

Primary force — once per revolution

F1 = m·r·ω²·cos θ. Large, and at engine frequency. In multi-cylinder engines it can often be cancelled by arranging the cranks symmetrically, or absorbed by counterweights on the crankshaft. A single cylinder cannot escape it.

2nd order

Secondary force — twice per revolution

F2 = m·r·ω²·λ·cos 2θ. Smaller (λ ≈ 0.25–0.35) but at twice the frequency, so it is harder to cancel with crank arrangement alone. It is the famous buzz of the inline-four, and the reason that engine often needs a pair of balance shafts spinning at 2× crank speed.

An engine layout is "balanced" to a given order if the vector sum of all cylinders' forces (and their moments along the crank) cancels at that order. The art of configuration — Ch. 05 — is largely the art of making these sums vanish.

The famous case

Why the straight-six is "inherently balanced".

The inline-six is the engine enthusiasts call perfectly balanced, and the figures above show why. Its six throws are arranged at 0–120–240–240–120–0°, which makes it behave like two inline-threes back to back, mirror-imaged. Every primary and secondary force has an equal and opposite partner along the crank, and so do their moments (the rocking couples that an inline-three and inline-four can't escape). The result: primary forces, secondary forces, primary couples and secondary couples all cancel — a rare clean sweep.

The smoothing element

The flywheel: a bank account for torque.

No matter how many cylinders, torque still rises and falls through the cycle. The flywheel — a heavy disc on the crankshaft — bridges the gaps. During a power stroke, surplus torque speeds it up slightly and energy is stored as rotational kinetic energy; during the idle strokes it gives that energy back, keeping the crank turning smoothly through to the next firing.

Energy stored in the flywheel E = ½ I ω²   →   fluctuation handled: ΔE = I ω² Cs  (Cs = coefficient of speed fluctuation)

The flywheel also does double duty: it carries the starter ring gear, presents the friction face for the clutch, and on many engines its rim is where the crank-position sensor reads. A dual-mass flywheel splits it into two spring-coupled halves to isolate the gearbox from torsional pulses — the same lumpiness of Fig. 4.2, attacked downstream.

Cancelling what's left

Counterweights and balance shafts.

Crankshaft counterweights

Lobes cast opposite each throw cancel the rotating mass of the crank pin and the big-end, and a chosen fraction of the reciprocating primary. They also reduce the bending and the main-bearing loads — but add rotating inertia, blunting throttle response.

Balance shafts

For the secondary force an inline-four can't cancel, Lanchester balance shafts — a pair counter-rotating at twice crank speed — generate an equal, opposite 2nd-order force. They smooth the engine at the cost of friction and complexity, which is why small, cheap fours often go without and simply buzz.

The whole chapter in one sentence

Balance is about cancelling forces and couples at the 1st and 2nd order; smoothness of delivery is about overlapping power strokes and storing the surplus in a flywheel. Configuration (Ch. 05) is how engineers buy both at once.
