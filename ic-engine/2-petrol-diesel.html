Petrol & Diesel: spark versus squeeze.

Two ways to light the fire. One brings the spark to a ready mixture; the other compresses air so hard that fuel ignites the moment it arrives. That single choice ripples through efficiency, fuelling, cold starting and the very shape of the cycle.

The fork in the road

Premix then ignite, or compress then inject.

Spark ignition

The petrol (Otto) engine

Air and fuel are mixed before they reach the cylinder (or during early intake, in direct injection) to a near-stoichiometric ratio. The charge is compressed modestly — typically 9:1 to 13:1, limited by knock — then a spark lights it at a precisely chosen instant. Combustion is fast and roughly constant-volume, near TDC.

Compression ignition

The diesel engine

Only air is drawn in and compressed hard — 14:1 to 24:1 — until it reaches 700–900 °C. Fuel is then sprayed in at high pressure; it ignites on contact and burns as it injects, giving a more drawn-out, roughly constant-pressure combustion. There is no throttle: power is set purely by how much fuel you inject.

That last point matters more than it looks. A petrol engine controls power by throttling the air, which costs pumping work at part load. A diesel always breathes freely and simply meters fuel — one reason it sips at light load, and idles so frugally.

The thermodynamics

Why the diesel is the more efficient engine.

Air-standard efficiency depends almost entirely on how much you compress the charge:

Otto (constant-volume heat addition) ηOtto = 1 − 1 / r(γ−1)
Diesel (constant-pressure heat addition, cutoff ratio rc) ηDiesel = 1 − (1 / rγ−1) · [ (rcγ − 1) / ( γ (rc − 1) ) ]

The bracketed term in the diesel expression is always greater than one, so at the same compression ratio Otto is more efficient. The diesel's advantage is that it has no knock limit — it compresses pure air, so it can run ratios of 18–24:1 where a petrol engine would detonate. The much higher r more than overcomes the cutoff penalty. The result, in real engines: ~25–35% brake thermal efficiency for petrol, up to ~45% for road diesels and 50%+ for large marine two-strokes.

The other half of the story — part load

Petrol engines throttle the intake to reduce power, creating a partial vacuum the pistons must pump against — pumping loss. Diesels never throttle; they just inject less fuel. In stop-start, light-load driving this gap widens dramatically, which is why diesels dominate trucks, ships and (historically) economy cars.

The limits of the fuel

Knock and octane; ignition delay and cetane.

Petrol problem

Knock (detonation)

In a spark engine the flame should travel smoothly across the chamber. If the unburnt end-gas ahead of the flame gets hot enough to auto-ignite, it explodes all at once — a sharp pressure spike heard as a metallic "pinking". Sustained knock hammers bearings and can hole a piston. Octane number rates a fuel's resistance to it; higher octane lets you run more compression or advance.

Diesel problem

Ignition delay & cetane

A diesel wants the fuel to auto-ignite — but not too slowly. The gap between injection and ignition is the ignition delay; if it is long, too much fuel piles up and burns at once, giving the diesel "knock"/clatter and high NOₓ. Cetane number rates how readily a fuel ignites — high cetane = short delay = smoother, quieter combustion.

Octane and cetane pull in opposite directions: a fuel that resists auto-ignition (good petrol) is a poor diesel, and vice versa. They are, in effect, mirror-image quality scales for the two combustion philosophies.

Starting cold

Glow plugs: helping a diesel light when the air is cold.

A diesel relies on the heat of compression to ignite its fuel. On a cold morning, the cylinder walls and the incoming air are cold enough to drain that heat away before ignition can occur — the compressed air simply doesn't get hot enough. The fix is a glow plug: an electrically heated element projecting into each cylinder (or the pre-chamber) that glows red-hot for a few seconds before and during cranking, providing a local hot-spot to start combustion.

Pre-glow

You wait for the dashboard coil lamp. The plugs heat to ~1000 °C in 2–5 s on modern ceramic units, far faster than the old metal-sheath type.

Crank & start

The hot element ignites the first injections reliably even with cold, heat-sapping cylinder walls.

After-glow

Plugs stay on for up to a minute after starting to smooth running and cut white smoke and noise while the engine warms.

Petrol engines need no such help to ignite — the spark does that regardless of temperature — but they do run a richer mixture and higher idle when cold, and rely on the same warm-up discipline for lubrication, which we take up in Ch. 07.

Beyond the distributor

Modern ignition & injection strategy.

Early engines fired a single spark at a fixed-ish advance set by a mechanical distributor and centrifugal weights. A modern engine's ECU instead computes the optimum spark and injection timing tens of thousands of times a minute, from a live picture of load, speed, temperature and even individual-cylinder behaviour.

The trend

The line between the two engines is blurring. Petrol engines now use diesel-style high-pressure direct injection; experimental HCCI engines deliberately auto-ignite a premixed charge like a diesel, chasing diesel efficiency with petrol-clean combustion. Ignition is no longer a spark — it is a control problem.
