Wear & upkeep: how an engine ages, and how to read it.

An engine is thousands of hard parts sliding against each other, hundreds of times a second, in a bath of hot acid and grit, for years. That it lasts at all is a triumph of lubrication. This chapter is about how that truce breaks down — slowly as wear, suddenly as failure — and how engineers diagnose an engine without taking it apart.

The whole game

Everything rides on a film microns thick.

A crankshaft does not actually touch its bearings. At speed it floats on a wedge of pressurised oil a few micrometres thick — thinner than a human hair — dragged in by the journal's own rotation. This is hydrodynamic lubrication, and while the film holds, wear is almost zero: the metal surfaces never meet. The art of engine longevity is keeping that film intact through every condition the engine sees.

Patience

Why you let the oil come to temperature before loading it.

Cold oil is thick. That sounds protective, but it is the opposite of what you want at the bearings: thick oil is slow to be pumped to the far corners of the engine, slow to form its hydrodynamic wedge, and it shears and pumps badly. Worse, the engine's parts are at uneven temperatures — the piston heats and expands far faster than the cold cylinder around it, so clearances are wrong. Loading an engine hard before the oil (not just the coolant) is warm forces the bearings to carry full firing loads while the protective film is still marginal.

Coolant warm ≠ oil warm

The temperature gauge on the dash usually reads coolant, which warms in a few minutes. The oil takes far longer — often two to three times as long — to reach full temperature and shed condensed water and fuel. A racing or hard-driven engine is best kept gentle until oil temperature, not coolant, is up.

The cold-start ritual

Start, let oil pressure establish, drive gently. This is why diesel and aero engines specify a warm-up, and why turbocharged engines also benefit from a brief cool-down: the turbo's bearing is oil-fed and red-hot, and shutting down instantly can coke the oil in it.

Slow death

How an engine wears.

Even perfectly maintained, an engine wears. The classic pattern follows the "bathtub": rapid run-in wear when new as surfaces bed together, a long flat plateau of slow steady wear, then an accelerating climb as clearances open, oil control fails, and damage feeds on itself.

Cylinder bore & rings

The ring sliding on the bore is the hardest-worked seal in the engine. As it wears, the bore goes oval and tapers, compression leaks past, oil is burned, and "blow-by" of combustion gas into the crankcase rises. A ridge at the top of the bore is the classic tell.

Main & big-end bearings

The plain bearings on the crank wear if the oil film fails — from dirty oil, low pressure, or sustained overload. Wear opens the clearance, the oil pressure drops further, and a knock develops. Left unchecked, this is the road to catastrophic failure.

Valve train

Cam lobes, followers, valve guides and seats wear and recede; clearances drift, sealing degrades, and the engine loses breathing and compression. Hydraulic lifters hide it for a while; eventually it shows as lost power and hard starting.

The dramatic failure

"Putting a leg out of bed."

It is one of the great pieces of engineman's slang — vivid, grim, and instantly clear once you've seen the aftermath. To "put a leg out of bed" (or "throw a rod") is the catastrophic failure in which a connecting rod breaks free and punches through the side of the crankcase. The "bed" is the crankcase — the housing the rods live in; the "leg" is a connecting rod. When one comes out of its bed, it comes out through the block.

Reading the engine's blood

Oil analysis: a diagnosis without a teardown.

Engine oil is a continuously circulating sample of the engine's internal health. Send a small bottle to a lab — spectrometric oil analysis — and the trace metals dissolved and suspended in it reveal which parts are wearing and how fast, long before anything is audible. Fleet operators, railways, ships and aircraft live by it: it turns maintenance from "fix it when it breaks" into "replace it just before it would have." Each wear metal points to a specific component.

Trends matter more than numbers

A single reading means little; the power is in the trend. A steady iron level is healthy wear; an iron level that doubles between samples is an engine telling you something is starting to fail. This is condition-based maintenance — and it is why the same Sulzer and Deltic locomotive engines of Ch. 08 were sampled at every depot visit.

Cold starting

Glow plugs: helping a cold diesel catch.

A diesel has no spark — it relies on the heat of compression to ignite its fuel (Ch. 02). But a cold engine steals that heat into its cold metal walls before the charge gets hot enough to fire, so a cold diesel may not start at all. The glow plug is a small electric heating element screwed into each cylinder; switched on for a few seconds before cranking, it warms the combustion chamber (or the pre-chamber) enough to guarantee ignition on the first compression strokes. The dashboard "wait-to-start" coil light is the glow plugs doing their job.

Glow plug (diesel)

Heats the chamber so compression ignition will catch when cold. Modern ceramic plugs heat in under two seconds and may stay on after starting (post-glow) to smooth warm-up and cut white smoke. Indirect-injection diesels lean on them heavily; large direct-injection engines may use intake grid heaters instead.

Not to be confused with…

A spark plug (petrol) fires every cycle to ignite the mixture; a glow plug (diesel) only assists starting and then is no longer needed. Model aircraft "glow engines" use a third, different thing — a catalytic glow filament — to keep a methanol engine firing. Same word, three jobs.

In practice

What actually keeps an engine alive.

Clean oil, changed in time

Oil wears out — additives deplete, acids and soot accumulate, the film weakens. Regular changes with the right grade are, by an enormous margin, the single biggest factor in engine life.

Clean air

The silicon line in the oil report is a reminder: a tired air filter feeds the engine grit, and grit is sandpaper on the bores. The cheapest part in the engine protects the most expensive ones.

Gentle warm-up, honest cool-down

Let the oil reach temperature before loading; let a hot turbo idle a moment before shutdown. Both keep the film where the Stribeck curve wants it — on the right-hand, safe side.
