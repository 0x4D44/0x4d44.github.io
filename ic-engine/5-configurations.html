Configurations: how you arrange the cylinders is the engine.

Take the same cylinder and the same cycle and lay the cylinders out differently, and you get a delivery van, a supercar, a fighter, or a locomotive. Layout decides balance, length, height, centre of gravity, cost and sound — often more than any other single choice.

Single bank

The inline (straight) engine.

All cylinders in one row, sharing one crankshaft and one cylinder head — the simplest possible multi-cylinder engine. Cheap to make, easy to maintain, and in six-cylinder form (Ch. 04) inherently balanced. Its weakness is geometry: a straight-six is long, which makes it awkward across a small car's engine bay and gives a tall four a high centre of gravity. The inline-four is the default engine of the world for one reason — it is the cheapest adequate answer — and lives with its 2nd-order buzz, sometimes tamed by balance shafts.

Why builders love it

One head, one cam bank, one exhaust manifold, a stiff simple crankcase. Half the part count of a V of the same cylinder count.

Where it runs out

Beyond six cylinders an inline becomes too long and its crankshaft too whippy (torsional vibration). That is the door through which the V walks.

Two banks, shared crank

The V engine: folding the engine in half.

Split the cylinders into two banks on a common crankshaft, set at an included angle, and a long engine becomes short and stiff. A V8 is no longer than an inline-four but has twice the cylinders; a V12 is effectively two straight-sixes sharing a crank — and inherits the straight-six's perfect balance, which is why it has always been the connoisseur's engine.

The bank angle is chosen for balance and packaging: 60° suits a V6 or V12, 90° suits a V8 (and lets the inlet "vee" sit neatly between the banks), 72° appears in some V10s. Get the angle wrong and you reintroduce the very shaking forces the layout was meant to avoid; that is why a 90° V6 (made by lopping two cylinders off a V8 tooling) needs a split crankpin or a balance shaft to fire evenly.

Cross-plane vs flat-plane V8

A cross-plane V8 crank (throws at 90°) gives the smooth, off-beat burble of an American V8 and a muscle-car idle, but needs heavy counterweights. A flat-plane crank (throws at 180°, like two inline-fours) is lighter and revs harder with a flat, hard wail — the choice of Ferrari and most F1 V8s — at the price of more vibration. Same layout, two completely different characters.

Banks at 180°

Flat & boxer engines.

Open the V right out to 180° and the banks lie flat. Two distinct things share this description. A true boxer gives each piston its own crankpin directly opposite its partner, so the two pistons "box" in and out together — primary forces cancel beautifully and the engine is very smooth and very low. A 180° V (flat but sharing crankpins, like some Ferrari "flat-12s") behaves differently and isn't truly boxer-balanced.

What the flat buys

The lowest possible centre of gravity — the engine sits below the axle line. Superb for a sports car's handling (Porsche 911, Subaru) and for a low cabin floor. Inherently good balance in 4- and 6-cylinder boxer form.

What it costs

Width. A flat engine is broad, crowding the front wheels and steering. Two cylinder heads, two exhaust banks, and awkward access make it more expensive than an inline of the same count.

Cylinders in a circle

The radial: the shape of early flight.

Arrange an odd number of cylinders like spokes around a single crank throw, all their rods ganged onto one master rod, and you get the radial — short, light, and beautifully air-cooled because every cylinder sits in the slipstream. It dominated aviation from the 1920s to the 1950s. The odd cylinder count (5, 7, 9…) is essential: it lets every other cylinder fire in turn for even firing in a four-stroke.

Strengths

Excellent power-to-weight, superb cooling, short and robust, tolerant of battle damage — a radial could lose a cylinder and fly home.

Quirks

Large frontal area (drag), and oil pools in the lower cylinders when stopped — they must be turned by hand before starting to avoid hydraulic lock.

Scaling up

Need more power? Stack two rows (twin-row radial) — the Pratt & Whitney R-2800 packed 18 cylinders into two rows of nine.

A featured engine

The Napier Deltic: three crankshafts in a triangle.

The most extraordinary layout in this study. The Napier Deltic is an opposed-piston two-stroke diesel arranged as an equilateral triangle: three cylinder banks form the sides, with a crankshaft at each of the three corners. Each cylinder holds two pistons that rush toward each other and back — there is no cylinder head at all; the combustion space is the gap between the two piston crowns mid-cylinder.

No pistons at all

The Wankel rotary: the cycle without a reciprocating part.

Felix Wankel's engine dispenses with pistons entirely. A triangular rotor orbits inside a figure-eight (epitrochoid) housing; the three chambers between rotor and housing each expand and contract as the rotor turns, performing intake, compression, power and exhaust in different places around the housing at once. There are no valves — ports do the breathing — and almost nothing reciprocates, so it is extraordinarily smooth and revs freely.
