01 / The problem

Halve the set.
Not the instructions.

Fertilisation combines two nuclear genomes. Meiosis prevents that combination from doubling chromosome number in every generation.

Imagine a species whose ordinary diploid nuclei contain four chromosomes. If two reproductive cells each contributed four, their offspring would start with eight. Repeat the process and the next generation would have sixteen. Instead, each parent makes haploid reproductive nuclei with two chromosomes. At fertilisation, two plus two restores four.

n + n = 2n

One set from each gamete → two sets in the zygote.

But “take half” is not a sufficient instruction. Our model has a long chromosome pair and a short chromosome pair. Giving a gamete both long chromosomes and no short one would leave some genetic instructions duplicated and others missing. A normal product needs one long and one short: one representative of every chromosome type.

This is the problem meiosis solves. First it recognises matching chromosomes. Then it distributes them in an organised sequence of two nuclear divisions. The products contain fewer homologous sets, without normally losing the collection of gene types in a set.[2]

The central distinction

Half as many homologous sets does not mean half as many kinds of gene. A normal haploid set retains a representative of each chromosome type. For an autosomal locus, it generally has one allele where a diploid cell had two.

Two sources of variation

Independent assortment changes which maternal and paternal homologues travel together. Crossing over changes which alleles travel together along an individual chromosome. They are different operations at different scales.

One balancing operation

Meiosis reduces the number of sets; fertilisation restores it. Variation is not a substitute for accurate segregation. A useful explanation must account for both the new combinations and the complete chromosome set.

How this guide fits Scottish S6 Biology

The main story follows the meiosis content in the Advanced Higher Biology specification valid from 2026–27: homologous pairs, replication, chiasmata and crossing over, independent assortment, then the two divisions. S6 pupils take different courses; this is an Advanced Higher-level treatment with Higher concepts rebuilt from first principles.

The support notes explicitly say “Names of stages are not required.” Stage names are included here as useful signposts, not as the point of the lesson. You should be able to explain what the chromosomes do and why it matters.

02 / Know what you are counting

An X is not a pair.

Most confusion about meiosis begins before the first division: two different kinds of “pair” are mistaken for each other.

Homologous chromosomes are corresponding chromosomes, typically one inherited from each parent. They have the same kinds of genes in corresponding positions, or loci. They need not have identical DNA sequences. At the same locus, one homologue might carry allele A and the other allele a.

Sister chromatids arise when one chromosome is replicated. They are copies of that chromosome, held together until a later separation. So a replicated homologous pair contains two chromosomes, each with two sisters: four chromatids altogether.

Chromosome

DNA organised with proteins. Before replication, one DNA molecule; after replication, two sister chromatids still counted as one chromosome until they separate.

Chromatid

A DNA molecule with associated proteins. Crucially, it is double-stranded DNA—not one strand of a double helix.

Gene, allele, locus

A gene is a functional DNA sequence; an allele is a version; a locus is its position. “Same genes” does not mean “same alleles”.

Centromere and kinetochore

The centromere is a chromosome region. The kinetochore is the protein assembly there that interacts with spindle microtubules. They are related, not interchangeable structures.

Think of homologues as two editions of the same volume, with corresponding chapters but possible differences in wording. Sisters are two copies made from one edition. The analogy helps with relationships, but DNA is a continuous molecule, not a pile of detachable pages. Crossing over is a molecular exchange between matching regions, not arbitrary cutting.[3] [8] [9]

Replication changes the DNA amount

Before meiosis I, S phase copies the DNA. Each chromosome now has two sister chromatids. The chromosome number has not yet doubled, because those sisters remain associated as one replicated chromosome.

Also resist the idea that chromosomes suddenly exist only when they become visible as X shapes. The DNA remains organised into chromosomes through interphase, but is usually less condensed. The compact rods in this guide are tracking symbols, not microscope photographs.

Check your mental picture

A diploid model cell just after S phase has 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids and 2 homologous pairs. It is still diploid. Four X-shaped chromosomes do not mean four sets.

Go deeper · Does “count the centromeres” always work?

It is a useful classroom shortcut, provided you apply the chromosome-counting convention consistently. Joined sister chromatids are counted as one replicated chromosome; after separation, each becomes a daughter chromosome.

At molecular resolution the centromeric DNA was replicated too, and each sister has its own kinetochore. Do not interpret the single gold join in a cartoon as proof that only one physical centromeric DNA sequence exists. The important transition for counting is sister separation, not a new round of DNA synthesis.[5] [8]

04 / The reductional division

Separate the editions.
Keep their copies.

Meiosis I is the unusual division: it separates homologous chromosomes while keeping sister chromatids together.

First, find the matching partner

As chromosomes condense, homologues pair by synapsis. Corresponding regions align, bringing non-sister chromatids close together. The paired unit is a bivalent; because it contains four chromatids, it is also called a tetrad. Pairing supplies a physical relationship between the two chromosomes whose distribution must be coordinated.

Crossovers can then exchange corresponding DNA segments. Connections visible as chiasmata, together with sister-chromatid cohesion, help maintain the association of homologues after much of the pairing apparatus has disassembled. This is not just a source of variation: in the usual crossover-based system, it is part of the mechanical solution for orderly segregation.

Next, organise the direction of travel

Spindle microtubules attach through kinetochores. In meiosis I, the two homologues attach towards opposite poles while the sisters of each homologue are co-oriented towards the same pole. The bivalents align at the spindle equator.

Imagine attaching two matching volumes to opposite sides of a sorting machine while leaving the two copies of each volume fastened together. Pulling the matching volumes apart sends one edition to each side. It does not yet separate that edition’s two copies.

Then, release the right connections

When homologues separate, each pole receives one of every chromosome type. In our orientation, M1 goes with P2 and P1 goes with M2. The allocation need not keep all maternal chromosomes together. After nuclear division and cytokinesis, each new cell has one set of replicated chromosomes.[1] [5]

Go deeper · Two-stage release of cohesin

Cohesin is a protein complex, not the centromere itself. It helps hold sisters together. In the canonical meiotic mechanism, arm cohesion is released at the first division while centromeric cohesion is protected. This resolves the connections that keep homologues associated while retaining the sister partnership needed for meiosis II.

{{COHESION}}

At the second division, centromeric cohesion is released too. The key idea is selective release in the right order. Protein names such as separase and shugoshin belong to the deeper mechanism; they are not necessary to understand the chromosome-level sequence here.

Real cells regulate attachment, recombination and progression. Meiosis is not a set of conscious decisions, and a spindle does not inspect whether an allele would be advantageous.[14]

05 / Variation within a chromosome

Not new letters.
New combinations.

Crossing over can make a chromosome a mosaic of maternal and paternal ancestry.

Consider four loci along a homologous pair. One homologue carries ABCD; the other carries abcd. The letters label alleles, not separate genes floating around the cell. Uppercase is simply a label here: this experiment is not making any claim about dominance.

After replication, there are two ABCD chromatids and two abcd chromatids. Suppose one chromatid from each homologue exchanges the segment beyond the interval between B and C. The participants become ABcd and abCD. The two non-participating chromatids keep ABCD and abcd.

That is a reciprocal exchange between non-sister chromatids. Matching segments are exchanged; we have not added or removed a locus. The novel feature is which alleles are neighbours on the same DNA molecule.[4]

Why non-sisters?

Homologues may carry different alleles. Exchanging between their non-sister chromatids can therefore change allele combinations. Exchanging equivalent segments between initially identical sisters would not create those visible marker combinations.

Why not a mutation?

In this example, A remains A and a remains a. The sequences already exist; their arrangement changes. Mutation and recombination are different sources of genetic variation, although real recombination errors can alter sequences or chromosome structure.

A subtle but important correction

Sisters start as copies of the same chromosome, but need not remain genetically identical after crossing over. In this example, M1a stays ABCD while its sister M1b becomes ABcd.

Go deeper · What the tidy crossover cartoon leaves out

The diagram is a record of outcomes, not a literal molecular film. Meiotic recombination involves DNA breakage and repair, strand interactions and intermediate structures. Not every recombination event resolves as a crossover, and crossover sites are not uniformly likely along a real chromosome.

A bivalent may undergo multiple crossovers, potentially involving different chromatid pairs. Some organisms and sexes use different recombination or segregation strategies. “One crossover between two middle tracks” is a controlled teaching example, not a universal quota.

If both homologues have the same allele at a marker locus, an exchange may leave no detectable change at that marker. An event can occur without producing a difference in the limited alleles you happen to measure.[4] [6]

06 / Variation between chromosomes

A separate choice
for every pair.

Independent assortment rearranges whole homologues. You do not need a crossover to get a new mixture of parental chromosomes.

At metaphase I, either homologue of pair 1 can face a chosen pole. Pair 2 can independently face either way; pair 3 can too. The cell does not have an “all maternal on the left” rule. Each pair contributes a two-way choice to the combination a gamete may receive.

For this experiment we temporarily turn crossing over off. That lets us isolate the effect of orientation, rather than mixing two mechanisms in the same explanation. M and P now label the origin of whole, unrecombined homologues.

Why the rule is 2ⁿ

For a particular gamete, pair 1 allows M or P: two possibilities. For each of those, pair 2 allows M or P again: four. Pair 3 doubles the possibilities once more: eight. Multiplication, not addition, describes independent choices.

2 × 2 × … × 2 = 2n

n homologous pairs in the diploid cell → n independent two-way choices.

8

2³ possible chromosome-origin combinations, ignoring crossing over.

For humans, 2²³ = 8,388,608. Combining two such gametes gives 2⁴⁶ = 70,368,744,177,664 possible parental-chromosome combinations before considering crossing over. These are combinatorial possibilities, not a count of visibly different people.

Across many meioses ≠ within one meiosis

2ⁿ is not the number of cells produced. One symmetric meiosis produces four products. Without crossing over, the two complementary meiosis-I combinations each appear twice after sister separation. The larger range comes from different orientations across many meiotic events.

Also distinguish chromosome-origin combinations from distinguishable allele combinations. If an individual is homozygous at every locus you measure, different parental-chromosome assortments may look identical in your data. The calculation tracks origins under a simplified model; it is not a guarantee that every outcome has a unique phenotype.[5]

07 / The equational division

Now separate the copies.

After meiosis I, the set has already been halved. Meiosis II distributes the existing sister chromatids.

Each haploid cell forms a spindle, and its individual replicated chromosomes align at the equator. This time the sister kinetochores face opposite poles. Once the remaining cohesion is released, the sisters move apart.

The moment they separate, the chromatids are counted as daughter chromosomes. Each future nucleus receives one of each chromosome type, with one DNA molecule in each chromosome. Nuclear division and cytokinesis produce the four haploid products in our symmetric model.

There is no DNA replication between the two divisions. Putting another S phase between meiosis I and II would leave a further set of copies to distribute. The whole logic depends on one replication followed by two different separation events.

Meiosis II resembles mitosis in what it separates, but begins with a haploid complement in this diploid-organism model. Nor are the sisters necessarily still identical: the crossover you followed earlier may have changed just one of them.[5]

Mitosis does not mean diploid. A haploid cell can divide by mitosis and remain haploid. “Mitosis versus meiosis” describes a division process; “haploid versus diploid” describes the number of sets.

08 / Make the bookkeeping work

Two counters.
Two different stories.

The symbols n and C answer different questions. Keeping them separate makes almost every chromosome-counting problem easier.

n tracks homologous sets. A diploid nucleus has two sets, 2n. A haploid nucleus has one, n. The value of n is species-dependent: two in our model, 23 in the usual human chromosome complement.

C tracks DNA amount. One C is the nuclear DNA content of one unreplicated haploid genome. A diploid cell before S phase has 2C. Replication makes 4C, but the cell remains 2n. After meiosis I, each nucleus is n with 2C. After meiosis II, each is n with 1C.

C is a relative amount of DNA, not literally a count of molecules: chromosomes differ in length. For the complete complements in this table, the DNA-molecule count and C value change in parallel. An extra chromosome in an aneuploid cell does not necessarily add the same amount of DNA as another chromosome would.

The anaphase trap: draw your boundary

During anaphase I, before cytokinesis, the whole model cell still contains four chromosomes and eight DNA molecules. Each pole is receiving two replicated chromosomes: n and 2C for the future nucleus.

During anaphase II, each of the two still-undivided cells contains four daughter chromosomes, because its two sister pairs have separated. Its DNA amount remains 2C. Each pole receives two chromosomes and 1C. Once the cell divides, the final product has two chromosomes—not four.

That temporary increase in chromosome count is not a fresh S phase and not restoration of the homologous pair. In questions about anaphase, say “in the whole undivided cell” or “at each pole”. A number without its boundary can be technically defensible and still deeply misleading.

A three-question method

1. Has DNA replicated? 2. Have homologues or sisters separated? 3. Am I counting the whole cell, one pole, or one final nucleus? Answer those before writing a number.

Worked example · An unfamiliar species with 2n = 12

Its haploid number is six. Before S: 12 chromosomes and 12 DNA molecules. After S: still 12 chromosomes, now 24 DNA molecules. After I: each cell has six replicated chromosomes and 12 DNA molecules. After II: each product has six chromosomes and six DNA molecules.

During anaphase II, before the second cytokinesis, each dividing cell temporarily contains 12 daughter chromosomes but still only 12 DNA molecules. Each pole has six. You did not need to memorise another species; the same structure generated every answer.

09 / Read the evidence

When genes travel together.

Independent assortment applies to different chromosome pairs. Genes on the same chromosome can be linked.

A chromosome carries many loci, not just one. If A and B sit close together, they often travel into the same gamete as a combination. A crossover between them can change that combination. A crossover elsewhere may leave their relationship unchanged.

For a heterozygote with parental combinations AB / ab, the non-recombinant gamete classes are AB and ab; the recombinant classes are Ab and aB. “Recombinant” is always relative to the parental arrangement you have specified. For a different parent arranged Ab / aB, those labels reverse.[10]

Use a test cross to reveal the combinations

In a suitable simple test cross, an AB/ab heterozygote is crossed with an ab/ab individual. The tester contributes ab each time. Offspring classes can therefore reveal the gametes made by the heterozygote, provided the markers are distinguishable and the experiment meets its assumptions.

Suppose we observe 420 AB, 420 ab, 80 Ab and 80 aB classes among 1,000 offspring. Both recombinant classes belong in the numerator:

(80 + 80) / 1,000 × 100 = 16%

16% recombination: 160 recombinant offspring out of 1,000. About 16 cM as a simple short-interval estimate.

Why distance helps—and why 50% is a ceiling

Nearby loci have fewer opportunities for a crossover between them, so they tend to stay together. For a short interval, a 1% recombination frequency corresponds approximately to one map unit, or centimorgan. That is a genetic distance inferred from recombination, not a universal number of DNA bases.

At larger separations, multiple crossovers complicate the relationship. For two marker loci, exchanges can restore a parental-looking arrangement, hiding some crossover events from the two-locus count. Recombination frequency therefore underestimates longer map distances.

In the standard two-locus model, the expected recombinant fraction approaches 50%, the independent-inheritance limit. A result near 50% could mean different chromosomes or widely separated loci on the same chromosome. It does not prove which. A finite sample can exceed 50% through sampling variation or because assumptions are wrong; that is not evidence for a straightforward map distance greater than 50 cM.[6]

One crossover in one bivalent involves two of four chromatids. That does not make the observed recombination frequency of every gene pair 50%. The event must fall between the loci, it may not occur in every meiosis, and multiple events can alter what the markers reveal.

Error in meiosis I

Both homologues go to one pole and neither to the other. Both daughter lineages are affected. Normal sister separation then produces two n + 1 products and two n − 1 products.

Error in meiosis II

Meiosis I was normal. If sisters fail to separate in just one of the two second-division cells, that lineage gives n + 1 and n − 1. The other lineage gives two normal n products.

If an n + 1 gamete fuses with a normal n gamete, the resulting chromosome count is 2n + 1; for the affected chromosome type there are three copies, or trisomy. An n − 1 gamete similarly gives 2n − 1, or monosomy for that chromosome.

11 / Put it back into life

Four nuclei.
Not always four gametes.

The symmetric four-cell diagram explains chromosome segregation. Real reproductive life cycles add important differences.

In sperm formation

A primary spermatocyte completes the two meiotic divisions to produce four haploid spermatids. They still undergo differentiation to become spermatozoa. “Four haploid meiotic products” is therefore not the same event as “four mature sperm”.

In egg formation

Oogenesis partitions cytoplasm unequally. Most is retained in one large cell, while small polar bodies receive the other chromosome sets. In humans, the ovulated secondary oocyte is arrested in meiosis II; fertilisation normally triggers completion. The equal circles in the main model must not be read as four equally sized, functional eggs.[7]

Go deeper · Plants, spores and the haploid generation

Do not generalise the animal shorthand “meiosis makes gametes” to every eukaryote. In the usual plant alternation of generations, a diploid sporophyte produces haploid spores by meiosis. Spores grow by mitosis into a haploid gametophyte, which makes gametes by mitosis.

2n → meiosis → n → mitosis → n

Fertilisation restores the diploid sporophyte lineage. The invariant idea is the reduction from two sets to one, not the assumption that the immediate meiotic product always fuses with another cell.[12]

Go deeper · What about X and Y?

The diagram uses autosomes so that two homologues can be drawn with matching gene order. Human X and Y are not homologous along their full lengths. They share limited corresponding regions that support pairing and recombination in typical male meiosis.

That exception does not change the standard human numerical checkpoints—46 chromosomes before meiosis and 23 in a normal gamete—but it matters when translating a simplified “matching volumes” picture into the actual genome. The model is explicitly not an all-purpose drawing of every human chromosome.[13]

The sentence to take away

Meiosis copies the DNA once, separates homologues and then sisters, and produces haploid nuclei with new possible allele combinations. The organism’s life cycle determines what happens to those nuclei next.

Six original exam-style tasks

These are practice prompts with indicative marking points, not official questions or marking schemes. Write your answer before revealing the explanation.

Draw a bivalent without confusing the units

Draw one replicated homologous pair. Label a chromosome, sister chromatids, two non-sister chromatids and a centromere region. State how many chromosomes and DNA molecules you have drawn.

Reveal a worked answer

Draw two X-shaped chromosome units beside one another. Both sides of one X are sisters; a chromatid on one X and a chromatid on the other are non-sisters. Label the constricted joining region, not a chromosome tip. Your bivalent contains two chromosomes and four DNA molecules.

✓ Two replicated homologues, not one X labelled as a pair.

✓ Sister and non-sister relationships identified correctly.

✓ Correct chromosome and DNA-molecule counts.

Explain the reduction

A species has 2n = 8. Explain why each nucleus after meiosis I has four chromosomes even though its chromosomes are still replicated.

Reveal a worked answer

The starting cell has four homologous pairs. The homologues of each pair separate to opposite poles in meiosis I, so each future nucleus receives one chromosome from each pair: four chromosomes, one set. Sister chromatids remain joined, so those four chromosomes contain eight DNA molecules.

✓ Homologues, not sisters, separate in meiosis I.

✓ One member of each of four pairs reaches each nucleus.

✓ Haploid despite retained sister chromatids.

Diagnose a graph

A cell’s DNA content rises from 2C to 4C, then a tracked daughter falls to 2C, then to 1C. A pupil says the first rise makes the cell tetraploid. Correct the explanation.

Reveal a worked answer

The rise is DNA replication during S phase, making sister chromatids. It changes DNA amount from 2C to 4C without increasing the number of homologous sets: the cell is still diploid. Meiosis I gives a haploid nucleus with 2C, and meiosis II gives one with 1C.

✓ Distinguishes DNA quantity from ploidy.

✓ Assigns replication and each division to the right changes.

Explain two mechanisms, not one

Describe how independent assortment and crossing over generate variation. Your answer must distinguish what is being rearranged in each case.

Reveal a worked answer

Different homologous pairs orient independently at the equator in meiosis I. Their segregation creates different combinations of maternal and paternal homologues in the products. Crossing over exchanges corresponding DNA segments between non-sister chromatids of homologues, creating new combinations of alleles along a chromosome.

✓ Whole-homologue combinations for assortment.

✓ Non-sister exchange for crossing over.

✓ Consequence stated as new allele combinations, not a guaranteed new mutation.

Interpret a product pattern

Four products from a 2n = 6 cell contain 4, 2, 3 and 3 chromosomes. Assuming one non-disjunction event and otherwise normal meiosis, identify the division and explain the pattern.

Reveal a worked answer

Here n = 3. One meiosis-II cell produced n + 1 and n − 1, while the other produced two normal n products. That fits failure of sister segregation in one of the two meiosis-II cells. A single meiosis-I error would affect all four products in the simple model.

✓ Establishes n = 3 before interpreting the numbers.

✓ Identifies the affected second-division lineage.

Calculate—and limit the conclusion

A test cross with known AB/ab parental phase yields 360 AB, 340 ab, 150 Ab and 150 aB offspring. Calculate the recombination frequency. Explain why it is not a precise measurement of physical distance along DNA.

Reveal a worked answer

Total offspring = 1,000; recombinants = 150 + 150 = 300. Recombination frequency = 30%. It measures the fraction of detected recombinant marker combinations, not base pairs. Multiple exchanges can go undetected by two end markers, and recombination rates vary along chromosomes. A simple “30 cM” estimate is less reliable than a short-interval estimate and must be qualified.

✓ Adds both recombinant classes and uses the full total.

✓ States percentage and distinguishes genetic from physical distance.

✓ Notes limitations rather than treating every percentage as an exact map distance.
