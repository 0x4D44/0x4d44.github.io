Inside Formula One Grand Prix Part I — The original

Chapter 06

Sound

The game's sound comes from one small FM synthesis chip, the Yamaha OPL2 on an AdLib card. The music is a catalogue of four songs. The engine note is not recorded or sequenced at all: it is three chip voices, retuned from the engine's RPM fifty times a second. This chapter explains how the original drives that chip, and how the port rebuilt the chip itself without copying anyone else's emulator.

A driver and a catalogue

The original splits sound into two kinds of file. Drivers are small flat binaries that know how to talk to one sound device. Catalogues hold the music as data. Each supported device gets four drivers: one for the intro, one for in-game music, one for sound effects and one for the credits. Only three devices have catalogues.

The X* files are not a fourth device. They are the installed slot. The installer copies the chosen device's four drivers over the X* names, and GP.EXE only ever loads X*. On the install the project studied, the X* files were byte-identical to the A* files, so AdLib had been chosen. Nothing picks a device at run time. Device identity lives entirely inside the driver binary: the catalogue filename it embeds and the I/O ports it uses. The preferences file F1PREFS.DAT has no device field; its only sound byte, at +0x408, switches music on or off.

One consequence shapes everything else. The effects driver and the front-end music driver load at the same run-time segment, 9438:0000. The original plays music or effects, never both at once. The menus have music. The race has an engine, tyres and crashes, and no music.

How it works

A driver is a flat binary loaded at segment:0000. Bytes 0x00 to 0x23 are a small data block that GP.EXE writes into directly — this is how the game tells the effects driver the current RPM. At 0x24 sit seven near-offset words. The game copies them into seven far pointers on its stack and calls through them. AX is the only argument.

The effects driver has no slot 6. The word where its pointer would sit is actually a configuration constant, 0x0006. The game only calls slot 6 on the music path, so it never fires in practice. But a port that treats all drivers alike and calls "fade" on the effects driver would jump into nonsense.

Inside ADLIB.CAT

All three catalogues share one container. It starts with a directory of little-endian (u32 offset, u32 length) pairs. The first offset is also the directory's own size, so the entry count is first_offset / 8. The chunks follow back to back: the first begins where the directory ends, each next one begins where the last ended, and the last ends exactly at the end of the file.

ADLIB.CAT has four entries. The first question was what they hold. An early guess was that some chunks were sound-effect tables. They are not. Every chunk is a song. The three effect drivers contain no catalogue filename at all; they carry their own sounds inside themselves.

The event stream

Each song is close to a standard MIDI track. Every event starts with a variable-length delta time, then a status byte and its data. Running status lets repeated statuses be omitted. Only four channel statuses ever occur: note-on (0x9n, where a velocity of zero means note-off), control change (0xBn, only controllers 1, 7 and 10), program change (0xCn) and pitch bend (0xEn). There is no tempo event anywhere. The stream ends with the two bytes FF 2F, and that end marker carries no length byte. That last detail is where the format departs from a real MIDI file.

The port's reader, src/adlib_cat.rs, rejects any other status rather than skipping it. The original driver never emits anything else, so an unexpected status means the decoder has lost its place. Quietly resynchronising would hide that.

The instrument bank

AdLib chunks, and only AdLib chunks, start with an instrument bank: a u16 count N, then N records of 24 bytes each. The event stream begins at +2 + 24N. The stream parses cleanly at exactly that offset and at no other, including the tempting +3 + 24N.

Each record uses 11 of its 24 bytes. Modulator and carrier values are interleaved, which is the classic SBI instrument order:

BytesOPL2 registerMeaning
0, 10x20 mod, cartremolo, vibrato, sustain, key-scale rate, frequency multiple
2, 30x40 mod, carkey-scale level and total level
4, 50x60 mod, carattack and decay rates
6, 70x80 mod, carsustain level and release rate
8, 90xE0 mod, carwaveform select
100xC0feedback and connection
11–23—zero padding in the catalogue

Byte 3, the carrier's level, is the same placeholder value in every record. The driver overwrites it with a level worked out from each note's velocity and its channel's volume.

How we know

The reading is over-determined rather than merely plausible. All eleven chunks across the three catalogues parse to exactly FF 2F at exactly the chunk end, with zero bytes left over. Note-ons balance note-offs exactly in every file: 4,937 against 4,937 for AdLib, 4,863 for Roland, 304 for the PC speaker. All 39 AdLib instrument records pass range checks with zero violations: byte 8 is always a valid modulator waveform, byte 9 a valid carrier waveform, byte 10 at most 0x0F, and the tail always zero. The alternative "all modulators, then all carriers" order would put a waveform at byte 4; none of the 39 records fits it.

Three encodings of the same music

Chunk i is the same song on every device, and that turns the Roland stream into a check on the AdLib one. The total tick counts agree: song 0 is 4,481 ticks on both; song 1 is 4,831 against 4,830; song 3 is 662 against 663. The best transposition between them is zero semitones for all four songs. When note-onset times are compared, each song matches its own counterpart far better than any other song.

The sharpest single piece of evidence is one strange event. Program change to value 126 occurs exactly once, in song 2, at tick 535 — in the AdLib, Roland and PC-speaker encodings. Three independent encodings agree on one event's position. The value 126 is larger than any song's instrument bank, so it cannot be a patch. It is a sentinel, and the driver reads it as "all notes off". Playback carries on past it.

The PC-speaker catalogue carries only songs 0, 2 and 3, and its credits driver is a 57-byte stub. Two independent facts point the same way, so the project infers that song 1 is the credits music. That remains an inference.

The 300 Hz clock

A song's delta times are in ticks, but the catalogue never says how long a tick is. That lives in the timer, and for a while the timer rate looked unknowable.

The game reprograms the PC's interval timer itself. At file offset 0x6D3B3 in the unpacked executable it loads divisor 3977 and far-calls a routine that writes command 0x36 to port 0x43 — channel 0, mode 3 — followed by the divisor. The timer's input clock is 1,193,182 Hz, so the interrupt rate is 1,193,182 / 3977 = 300.02 Hz.

The interrupt handler calls the sound driver's fast tick on every interrupt. It reloads a counter with 5 and calls the main tick on every sixth. So the two sound rates are 300 Hz and exactly 50 Hz. The RPM hand-off to the effects driver happens at the 50 Hz rate too.

Three independent facts agree on this rate. First, 3977 is the only divisor constant in the executable that gives a round rate. Second, the game's saved frame period gives a frame rate of 300 / N, and that reproduces the documented 8.1, 10 and 15 frames per second exactly (300/37, 300/30 and 300/20). Third, song 0's 4,481 ticks come to about ninety seconds at 50 Hz, which is the right length for a menu theme.

The trap

An earlier pass through the same code concluded that the game never reprograms the timer, only reads it. The reason: writing 0x00 to port 0x43 is a latch command, used to read the counter, but 0x36 is mode programming. Reading only the latch sites produces a confident, wrong "no reprogramming" answer and loses the tick rate. The lesson the project kept: check what a negative result actually searched before trusting it.

The music sequencer

The music driver turns a song into chip writes. The port's equivalent is MusicPlayer in src/sound_driver.rs. Its job, once per 50 Hz main tick, is simple to describe:

  1. If an event is waiting, count its delta down by one.
  2. While the remaining wait is zero, fire the pending event and read the next one with its delta.

The loop in step 2 matters. A chord is several note-ons with a delta of zero between them. Firing until the wait becomes non-zero makes all of them sound on the same tick.

The delta+1 bug

The first version of the sequencer did the two steps the other way round in effect. It decremented the wait on a tick where the event could not also fire. Each event then cost its delta plus one tick. Nothing sounded wrong at first listen — the music simply ran slow. Song 0 took 6,093 ticks to play instead of 4,482, about 36 per cent longer.

The fix is the rule now written in the code: the tick that uses up a delta is the tick the event fires on, so a delta of one costs exactly one tick. The test that guards it plays song 0 to its end and requires exactly 4,482 calls. Why 4,482 and not 4,481? The stream's deltas add up to 4,481. The first event fires on the first call, and the end marker fires on the call at absolute time 4,481, so playing the song out takes one call more than the total delta.

Voices, volume and fades

Songs use sixteen logical MIDI channels, but the chip has only nine voices. The original's allocator, at XINGAME.BIN:0x1328, picks a voice for each new note in this order: a free voice that already holds the right instrument (so it needs no reprogramming), else any free voice, else voice 8 regardless. The port keeps that exact policy.

A note's pitch comes from a twelve-entry F-number table, one entry per semitone. The OPL2 sets octave through a separate "block" field, so twelve entries cover the whole range. Each entry is round(440 × 2^((k − 57) / 12) × 2^20 / 49716), and the tests re-derive them.

Only controller 7, channel volume, changes what the chip hears. The songs also carry modulation (controller 1) and pan (controller 10). An OPL2 can do nothing with either, so the driver reads and discards them. It does not synthesise pitch bend either. A fade-out runs for 127 main ticks, raising every sounding voice's attenuation step by step until the player falls silent.

From the port

A mutation test found a real bug in the port's volume handling. When a volume controller arrived mid-note, the first version rescaled the level already derived from the note's velocity. The result was inaudible. The voice now remembers the velocity it was struck with and recombines it with the new volume. The same round of mutation testing caught two tests that passed for the wrong reason: one compared a constant with itself, and one released voices in an order that made the check it was meant to test redundant.

The engine note

The engine is the sound you hear most, and it is not music at all. The effects driver builds it from three OPL2 voices whose pitches it recomputes whenever the RPM value changes. Voices 0, 7 and 8 are permanently reserved for it.

RPM in

On the game side, the timer handler updates an RPM word in game data and hands it to the driver's word 0. Before it does, it clamps the value to the F1GP rev range and adds a little noise:

rpm   = clamp(rpm + delta, 500, 14873)
out   = max(rpm + (random & 0x7F), 500)   ; 0..127 rpm of jitter
driver word 0 = out

The 500 to 14,873 band settles the units: the driver's word 0 is engine speed in real revolutions per minute. Separately, the viewed-car engine update at 0752:92E0..93E9 publishes a second word, at offset 0x10 of the driver's own memory. That routine also handles the automatic gear, with a signed gear test and an alternating downshift gate above 8,500 rpm. The word it publishes comes from one of two game words, chosen by whether the car is accelerating. The effects driver compares it with 12 to choose between its two engine loudness sets. The first design document had listed the meaning of that word as unknown; the port settled it by porting the writer.

Three voices out

The driver scales the RPM once, then derives three voices from it:

F   = (rpm × 0x597A) >> 16         ; about rpm × 0.34955
ch0 = ((F × 2) & 0xFFFF) >> 2     ; F / 2      fundamental
ch7 = ((F × 6) & 0xFFFF) >> 2     ; F × 1.5    ×3 harmonic
ch8 = ((F × 5) & 0xFFFF) >> 2     ; F × 1.25   ×2.5 harmonic
ch0 += random & 0x0030
ch7 += random & 0x0070
ch8  = base + (random & 0x0070)    ; re-jittered on every fast tick

The frequency ratio is exactly 1 : 3 : 2.5. Each linear value becomes an F-number and a block by repeated halving until it fits the chip's 10-bit F-number field; the number of halvings is the block. The audible frequency is about v × 49716 / 2^20, or v × 0.047418 Hz, which gives a fundamental of roughly 66 Hz at 8,000 rpm. Because only the low 16 bits of each product survive the shift, the arithmetic would wrap above about 31,000 rpm. The clamp means it never gets there.

Loudness does not jump. When the RPM has changed, the main tick moves each voice's level one step toward its target, so a change is heard as a swell. An unchanged RPM costs no chip writes at all. The register write complements the value (level XOR 0x3F) because the OPL2 register holds attenuation, not volume. The noise itself comes from a tiny generator: add a constant, then rotate right three bits.

Where the engine's timbre lives

The engine's sound colour is not in ADLIB.CAT. It is in the effects driver's own instrument bank: thirteen records at XSOUND.BIN:0x0094, in the same 24-byte layout the catalogue uses. The engine takes records 3, 8 and 9. All three are two-operator FM with a sustained envelope and frequency multiple 1 on both operators; they differ only in waveform and feedback.

The trap

The port's first bank reader reused the catalogue rule "the record tail must be zero". It rejected the genuine driver. The effects bank keeps four extra words in the tail — a duration, a random mask and a random base — for the one instrument whose effect sweeps its own pitch. The reader now checks the register fields (valid waveforms and feedback) and leaves the tail alone.

Reading the timbre from the player's own driver keeps the sound original without bundling anything. The installed XSOUND.BIN and the AdLib ASOUND.BIN are byte-identical, so either name works.

Effects and the channel map

With percussion mode off, the effects driver uses a fixed channel layout:

The start entry, slot 3 at XSOUND.BIN:0x0532, doubles AX and dispatches through a table. The port's reading of the methods, confirmed as each was connected to its real caller in the race code, is:

MethodWhat reaches it
0reset; programs voices 0, 7 and 8 from bank records 3, 8 and 9
1the continuous tyre squeal on channel 5, pitch from a random byte and a base word
2a race-sequence state machine (the routine at DAC8)
3the pit-stop animation; a sweep between 0x10FE and 0x132E

5a secondary sound sequence driven from the race frame

The first design document, written before these callers were traced, had guessed different names for methods 2 to 4 (collision, siren, kerb noise). The later port names come from following the real call sites, so this chapter uses those. The separate stop entry, slot 4, walks methods 9 down to 0. Only methods 1 and 5 have real stop handlers; the rest share a full reset. A complete stop loop issues 116 chip writes.

When contact steals channel 7, the engine's ×3 harmonic goes quiet for the burst. The port restores the engine key-on when the burst ends, and keeps reasserting the contact pitch in the meantime, because the engine's own main tick also writes channel 7.

The trap

The driver's chip writer, XSOUND.BIN:0x02BE, puts the register in AL, the value in AH, and then reads the status port several times as a delay. Its last read replaces AL. One routine passes that whole AX straight on to program the next voice, so the chip's status byte leaks into a pitch. A port that invents a constant status value changes that voice's pitch. The port therefore treats the final status observation of every write as an ordered input: each source-level call must be supplied with it, and the call fails up front if it is missing.

Building the chip

To play any of this, the port needs an OPL2. The obvious route was to borrow one. The project's own DOS emulator, mddosem, already has an OPL core. Reading it closely changed the plan: that core is a line-for-line Rust transliteration of Nuked-OPL3, which is licensed under the LGPL 2.1 or later. Its C source sits vendored in that repository, the Rust cites it in nineteen doc comments, and its constant tables match byte for byte. Copying it would make the port a derivative of an LGPL work and break the project's clean-room boundary.

So the port writes its own, in src/opl2.rs. It is integer-only, has no heap allocation and no floating point, and uses only the core library. It takes register writes and produces signed samples at the chip's own rate: its 3.579545 MHz master clock divided by 72, which is 49,716 Hz. Resampling to the sound card's rate is the caller's job.

Every table is a formula

The work turned out to be smaller than it sounds, because every constant table the chip needs is a closed-form function of its index. Nothing has to be copied from anyone's ROM dump or emulator.

What the model covers

The model covers all nine melodic channels: the phase generator with F-number, block and frequency multiple; the four-stage envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release) with key-scaled rates and key-scale level; the four OPL2 waveforms; FM and additive connection; modulator self-feedback; and the global tremolo (a triangle of about 3.7 Hz, 1.2 dB or 4.8 dB deep) and vibrato. A few chip quirks are worth knowing:

  • The key-scale level settings are not in increasing order. Setting 0 is off; 1, 2 and 3 give 3, 1.5 and 6 dB per octave.
  • Frequency-multiple setting 0 means one half, and settings 11, 13 and 15 repeat their neighbours. The model stores twice the multiple to stay in integers.
  • Each sustain-level step is 3 dB, except the last, which means silence rather than 45 dB.
  • With register 0x01 bit 5 clear, every operator plays a plain sine whatever its waveform register says.
  • The eighteen operators sit in a 22-slot register offset space with gaps, so a third of the offsets in each register block address nothing.

Two things are deliberately left out. Percussion mode (register 0xBD bit 5) is stored but inert: the effects driver writes 0xBD = 0xC0 at init, which leaves it off, and the music allocator hands out all nine voices, which percussion mode would cut to six. The chip's two timers produce no sound; the original uses them only to detect the card.

How we know

The chip's validation needs no licensed code. The tables are checked against their formulas. Physical tests check the documented behaviour: a programmed F-number and block must produce a tone at v × 49716 / 2^20 Hz, counted by zero crossings; eight total-level steps must halve the amplitude; a sustaining operator must hold until key-off; a percussive one must decay with the key still down. A battery of eighteen deliberate breaks in the chip and parser — wrong stride, swapped operator bytes, dropped bounds checks — was killed by the tests. Four tests earned their keep: one exposed a sustaining operator decaying at its release rate, and three turned out to pass for the wrong reason and were fixed.

How the port plays it

The port has two separate sound paths, mirroring the original's "music or effects" split.

In the race: the original driver's own logic

During a race, the port runs the original effects driver's methods as ported source. src/original_runtime_effect_trigger.rs covers the start and stop entries; src/original_runtime_timer_sound.rs covers the fast tick at 057F and the main tick at 05C5; src/original_runtime_engine_sound.rs covers the viewed-engine update. The driver's memory image belongs to the caller, because the original mutates data inside its own code segment as it runs. No copy of XSOUND.BIN is built into the program; it is read from the player's install.

A portable library cannot perform port I/O. So each source OUT pair to ports 0x388 and 0x389 becomes one ordered OplWrite record in a buffer the caller supplies. Everything is transactional: driver memory, game memory and the write list are published together only after the whole stage succeeds.

The chip is advanced by the game's own programmed timer time. Timer cycles convert to chip samples as cycles × 12 × 49716 / 14318180 (the timer clock is the 14.31818 MHz crystal divided by 12). Register batches are stamped at the timer boundary where they were produced; the port has no instruction-level timestamps. A resampler then interpolates between consecutive chip samples with exact rational arithmetic. It adds at most one chip period of delay, because a sample is published only once its right-hand neighbour exists. A single-producer, single-consumer queue of 8,192 samples feeds the device callback. If it fills, the newest samples are dropped and counted, and underflows are counted too. The rule, stated in src/native_original_audio.rs, is that device demand never advances the original clock or the sound driver. Silent stretches are skipped in bulk rather than sample by sample.

The one hardware feature the race path touches but does not model is the chip's timers. An early adapter rejected any write that started timer 1 or 2. That failure also threw away the valid writes before it in the same batch. The fix, on 2026-09-13, forwards every ordered register pair to the chip. A write to register 0x04 with bit 7 set clears the adapter's timer flags, and that status goes back to the driver. The host timer stays the game's only interrupt clock.

In the menus: the port's own sequencer

Front-end music uses the port's clean-room MusicPlayer on its own Opl2, driven at 50 Hz from the sample stream, and resampled to the device rate. It reads ADLIB.CAT from the player's install. Which song plays where is the port's choice, based on the inferences above rather than a trace of the original's calls:

Changing screen fades the current song out before starting the next. When a driving session starts, a scoped, nestable "suspension" silences menu music for the whole session and releases it on return — the port's version of the original's shared driver segment. The - and = keys adjust sound level during a race, as in the original, and the settings file carries a plain sound=on or sound=off line.

Recording

Launching with --record captures the whole presentation — menus, races and sound — into a Matroska file with VP9 video and Opus audio, named YYYY.MM.DD - big-race.mkv in the user's Videos (Movies on macOS) folder. Audio is captured at 48 kHz stereo through its own resampler tap, and the menu music has a non-blocking tap of its own, so the game loop never waits for the encoder. The encoder lives in a shared crate from the mddosem project.

How the sound came together

  1. 2026-08-31

    A placeholder engine

    The first native races got a provisional clean-room engine tone: an integer saw/square wave at RPM/10 Hz, with ramps to silence on pause. It was explicitly temporary.

  2. 2026-09-05

    The AdLib investigation

    One day produced the design record of the catalogue, driver interface, engine arithmetic and channel map; the discovery that the emulator's OPL core was an LGPL derivative; the independent chip and catalogue parser; the sequencer and engine voice; the 300.02 Hz timer rate; and the engine played through the real chip with the timbre read from the player's driver.

  3. 2026-09-08

  4. 2026-09-09

    Contact and trackside sound

    Wall-impact sound was connected before wall damage, as the source orders it, and methods 6–9 brought passing-car sound to the stationary camera.

    The provisional engine and effects host was removed. The original race sound interrupt replaced it; menu music kept the catalogue, sequencer and chip.

    The last shipped audio rejection — timer-control writes — was closed without inventing a second clock.
