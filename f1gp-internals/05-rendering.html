Inside Formula One Grand Prix Part I — The original

Chapter 05

Drawing the world

Every frame, GP.EXE builds a 320 by 164 picture of the circuit out of integer arithmetic, then shows it through the windows of a cockpit it keeps inside the executable. This chapter follows that picture from the camera to the last pixel, then explains how the port proved it draws the same pixels.

The 3D renderer in Formula One Grand Prix has no depth buffer, no floating point and no textures in the modern sense. It is a painter. It draws the far things first and the near things last, and each write simply overwrites what was there. Everything else in this chapter is about how it decides what to paint and in which order: which track records are in view, how their points reach the screen, how edges become filled polygons, and where the trackside objects and cars slot in between the road's pieces.

The addresses below use the segment labels the port uses. Renderer code is cited as 15F2:XXXX; the game's main code segment as 0752:XXXX. The same routine can appear under a second, runtime label (for example 1699:0999 for the fill at 15F2:1409), because the renderer segment is relocated when the program loads.

One frame, start to finish

The frame driver is 15F2:8C3E. It runs a fixed sequence of stages. Most of them only build lists; drawing happens late, in one back-to-front sweep.

In words:

  1. Mark the cars. 15F2:AFA3 stamps "a car is here" onto the track records the visible cars occupy. It draws nothing. The road pass will find the stamps and treat those records as objects.
  2. Walk the road. The pass (15F2:46C0) walks the track records in view, projects a block of points for each one, and writes a list of segment entries, object marks and scene headers. Its fix-up (5473) settles the horizon rows.
  3. Paint the sky. 15F2:7D2E fills the sky bands, copies the horizon panorama and lays down the ground colour.
  4. Build the edges. 15F2:5682 turns pairs of projected points into clipped, scan-converted screen edges.
  5. Draw. 15F2:8A9A sorts the object marks (5BB9), then for each stretch of road fills the road colour band, the verges, the strips and the kerbs, drawing each object when its turn comes.
  6. Draw the rest of the objects (5E8B).
  7. Draw the nested scene, a second, smaller road scene used for the pit lane.
  8. Optionally shade the road texture (15F2:89D4).
  9. Unmark the cars (15F2:B1A7).
  10. Present. Later, a presenter copies the finished canvas into video memory through the cockpit's windows.

One small piece of defensive engineering sits under all of it. GP.EXE hooks the divide-error interrupt (INT 0) so that a division that overflows sets a flag and returns, instead of crashing the machine. The renderer divides constantly: every projected point is a division by depth. A bad divide therefore degrades a frame rather than ending the game. The port reproduces that behaviour as a checked result wherever the original relies on it.

A 320 by 164 canvas behind a cockpit

The game does not draw its 3D scene straight into VGA memory at A000:0000. It draws into an offscreen canvas in the segment the program calls 580C (its own "upper" segment 50BA, relocated by the load segment 0752). A far pointer at offset +0x1C of the scene data names the exact start. The port's capture tools read that pointer from the running game and got 580C:1400. The design for the enhanced renderer records the offset as 0 in the cockpit view and 0x1400 in the external views. 0x1400 is 5,120 bytes, which is exactly sixteen rows of 320 pixels.

The scene is 320 pixels wide and 164 rows tall. Every clip, every edge stream and every fill assumes those numbers. The bottom 36 rows of the 200-row screen belong to the cockpit panel and are never part of the scene.

The cockpit itself is a complete 320 by 200 indexed picture stored inside GP.EXE. When a frame is ready, the presenter at 19ED:31FA copies the scene into the VGA page. It does not clear the page first, and it does not copy the whole scene:

  • Rows 0 to 102 are copied whole. Above row 103 there is nothing in front of the windscreen.
  • Rows 103 to 163 are copied as two spans per row, a left one and a right one. Between them sits the car's nose and the dashboard, which stay as they were. A few rows copy nothing at all, and some spans stop short of the screen edge where the cockpit frame intrudes.

Two consequences follow, and both mattered to the port. First, the presenter copies only about 61% of the VGA page per frame. The rest is whatever an earlier frame or the cockpit drew. A test that reads the whole VGA page and compares it compares history, not one frame. Second, pixels the scene draws behind the cockpit nose are real work that nobody sees. When the port later compared its canvas byte for byte, 13,190 of the 52,480 scene bytes differed on each test frame, and all of them lay outside the presenter's spans. The presented pages were identical.

The trap

The first renderer oracles read A000:0000, the VGA page. The port's own capture tooling later printed the game's real target, 0x580c 0x1400. Any whole-page diff taken at A000 mixes the current frame with retained pixels from earlier frames. The fix was to capture the canvas and the presented page separately, and to compare each against the right thing.

The camera

The camera is built each frame from the car the view is following. The routine (image offsets 8BD8..8DE6 in the main segment) takes the car's record, how far along the record it is, how far across, its heading, its ride height and its own pitch term. From those it produces a world position, a height, a heading with its cosine and sine, a horizon row and a perspective scale.

Two views, two sets of terms

The game has two parallel routines that set the session camera, at 0752:E985 and 0752:E9BC. They differ in exactly three things:

From the port

For a long stretch the port hardcoded an eye height of 0x180 and a pitch of -0x400, because the first camera capture happened to be taken in View B. Every later capture was in View A and disagreed. Five iterations produced plausible explanations that were all wrong. The answer came from a raw scan for writes to [0x233f]: exactly two sites, 64 bytes apart, the two view routines. The race-start residuals were exactly View B minus View A: 384 − 160 = 224 units of height and 1024 units of pitch, about 25 rows of horizon.

The horizon row

The camera's pitch is the sum of four terms: the road's own pitch where the car stands, the view's fixed pitch, the car's pitch term (entrant +0xB6) and a small camera bob that race control integrates. The horizon row is then:

horizon_row = 0x50 + (sin(pitch) >> 6)      // clamped at the view bottom

With a level camera the horizon sits on row 80 (0x50). The sine is a 1.14 fixed-point value, so shifting it right by six could move the horizon by at most 256 rows either way; the observed movement is a few rows. The port observed [bp+0x130] at 64 while driving and 66 at rest in one Suzuka session, which is this term at work. The vertical perspective scale [bp+0x17c] read 0x6e80 in the running game.

The car's world position has one subtlety. When the camera places the car between two records, it rotates by an interpolated cosine (see the next section), while pitch uses the plain table value. A moving car on a curve stayed one world unit wrong in the port until that interpolation (0752:03C8) was found.

The trap

Two camera comments in the port once cited 8C53 and 8D6A for the pitch sum and the sine. A later check found that the first lands mid-instruction and the second is buffer zeroing, not trigonometry. The arithmetic is right; those two citations are not. The project now checks that any cited address falls on an instruction boundary before trusting it.

Angles and the cosine table

Angles in F1GP are 16-bit words: 0x10000 is a full turn, 0x4000 a right angle. There is one trigonometry table in GP.EXE: a half-turn of cosines, one 16-bit word for every eight heading units, 4,097 words plus a spare. Values are 1.14 fixed point, so 0x4000 means 1.0.

  • Cosine takes the absolute value of the angle as a signed word, shifts it right three, and reads that word. Cosine is symmetric, so the half-turn covers everything.
  • Sine is cosine of 0x4000 − angle.
  • Interpolated cosine (0752:03C8..03FA) reads the next word as well and blends by the angle's three low bits: c + ((next − c) × (angle & 7)) >> 3. Only some callers use it; the others deliberately use the plain lookup.

The port never approximates these with floating point. It reads the table from the player's own GP.EXE and applies the same shifts. That is the only way a sine, multiplied by a record heading and shifted six places, lands on the same pixel.

From world to screen

Every point the renderer draws, whether a road edge, a kerb corner or a vertex of a grandstand, goes through the same short chain: move into camera space, rotate by the heading, divide by depth.

The anchor transform

The shared stage is the anchor transform at 15F2:2B1B. The road enters it at 2B49, a sixteen-bit camera entry with no pre-scale. Objects enter at 2B1B itself. The routine:

  1. takes the 32-bit horizontal and depth offsets from the camera and, unless the object is flagged near ([0x2a4] negative), shifts both right by three;
  2. multiplies their low words by the 1.14 sine and cosine and rotates the pair;
  3. keeps the horizontal result shifted right six, and the depth as the high word after two left shifts.

Near objects keep eight times more precision than far ones. The near flag travels with every point as bit 15 of its flag word, and the near-plane clipper later has to reconcile points of the two scales.

The perspective divide

The per-point projector (15F2:8D8F, and its body 9078..91FC) works like this:

  • If the depth is below 8, the point takes the near path. It gets flag 0x10 plus left/right and above/below bits from the signs of its horizontal and vertical offsets. Its screen words are never written.
  • Otherwise the column is 160 + (horizontal << 8) / depth.
  • The row multiplies the vertical offset by the perspective scale, keeps the high word after one left shift, shifts that left by five (or eight for a near object), divides by depth, and applies a small rounding step (8EBC..8EE2) shared by every vertical divide. The result is subtracted from the camera's base row.
  • A column that will not fit in a word goes through a normalising branch (2A1D) that shifts numerator and vertical together until they fit, and records the remaining shift in the flags.
  • Finally each point gets clip bits for being off the left or right (column not in 0..319) or above or below the scene.

Visibility: the AND of the flags

Before an object's elements are walked, the shape names a short list of points to test (15F2:9877..989E). The renderer ANDs their clip flags together, starting from the mask 0x1c (left, right and near). If a bit survives the whole list, every test point lies beyond the same edge, and the object is skipped. If the accumulator reaches zero, the object is drawn at once.

No single point needs to be on screen. A tall sign can have its base off the left edge and its top off the right edge and still cross the screen. The port once required at least one visible point, and lost objects that the original drew.

Sky, panorama and ground

Between the road pass and the edge builder, 15F2:7D2E..7DFD paints everything above the road. It needs two rows: the road's top row (the higher row of the first scene header, and never above the tracked horizon), and the camera horizon [bp+0x130], moved by the panorama's eight rows unless the car's section flag 0x08 hides the panorama.

The sky comes from a table at DS:0x76 in the executable (7CE1..7D2D). It is a list of pairs: a row count and a colour. The stage fills upward from the lower of the two rows, one band per pair, through the band filler 19ED:314A. A colour at or below 0x10 is special: it produces a single row dithered between the neighbouring pairs' colours, with the next colour on even columns (7CCC). The shipped table is fifteen three-row bands with dithered seams, then one 250-row band that covers everything above. That gives the smooth-looking gradient with a handful of palette entries.

The panorama is a 512 by 8 strip of scenery that each circuit file supplies, copied at load time. The drawer at 19ED:39ED places its bottom row on the camera horizon, clips it to the road top, and picks the starting column from the heading:

column = (camera_heading >> 5) & 511

A full turn is 65,536 heading units, and 65,536 shifted right five is 2,048 columns. The 512-pixel strip therefore repeats four times per full turn. When the wet-weather flag SS:[0x122E] is set, a helper then maps every copied panorama row through one of the four shade banks, picked from SS:[0x0184] plus one and clamped to 0..3, so the far scenery greys out in the rain.

The ground colour ([bp+0x1af], or [bp+0x1ae] when a flag at [es:0x175] has bit 7) fills from the road top down to the view bottom when the "outer fill" is on, or between the horizon and the road top otherwise.

From the port

An early frame test fed the road scene no panorama, reasoning that the horizon was a separate stage. It is not: this stage paints it. The test was scoring a whole band of sky against a sky it never drew. Supplying the panorama moved the race-start score from 26.62% to 51.06% in one step, and it retracted a whole iteration's worth of shading analysis that had been built on the artefact.

The road pass

A circuit is a ring of track records, 46 bytes each (Chapter 02 describes them). The road pass turns the records in view into three lists: segment entries, point blocks and object marks.

Walking back toward the car in bands

The pass at 15F2:46C0 starts at the car's record plus its draw distance (a byte at record +0x20, 60 by default) and walks back toward the car, 46 bytes at a time. It divides the walk into four detail bands. The farther a band, the fewer features it draws:

Each band reads a different feature byte from the record: +0x2b for outer edges, +0xa for strips, +0x2a for edges and kerbs, +0x27 for the feature pairs. The pass doubles the byte into both halves of a word and masks it to 0x3ff. A running mask [0x142] collects the bits the bands allow, and 3D76 clears the bits of any feature whose points have all left the screen.

A band's first record goes through 15F2:3EAA; the rest go through the loop 15F2:3FBF. Two more loops handle the stretch in front of the car (40EE, which forces the edge and kerb polygons closed three records out and switches the road colour four records out) and the records just behind it (4380).

Segment entries mark changes, not records

A record produces a 16-byte segment entry only when its feature byte carries bits the pass still allows. Most records on a straight have nothing new to say, so they add nothing. In one captured Suzuka frame, 22 entries covered 60 records. The list lives at 0xA13E..0xA47E, room for 52 entries.

An entry holds the features, the closing bits that say where each feature's polygon ends, a flag (0x80 in [0x14a]) for a record that faces more than a quarter turn away from the camera, the road colour, the kerb and strip colours (remapped through tables at DS:0x208 and DS:0x220), and a distance key [0x66] the draw loop later uses to interleave objects.

The point block: eighteen slots per record

For each entry the band routine projects a block of eighteen twelve-byte anchor records, 216 bytes (3474, 3782, 39EC and 3BF1, each falling through to the next lower band). Each point is the record's position moved sideways along its lateral axis, then run through the anchor transform at 2B49. The road uses exactly the same arithmetic as the objects.

The port proved this against 240 records captured over three frames and a throttle run. Every slot that appeared matched exactly: 162 outer edges, 79 road edges and 95 strips and twins. The later whole-frame capture exercised the feature pairs for the first time and exposed two small errors, both in where the second point of a pair sits.

The road colour

The road colour comes from a triple of colours at DS:0x258 (far, near, nearest), and the pass switches between them at the near band and four records out. Which of three triples it uses is chosen at 4669..469F from a word at [bp+0x150]: below -0x200 picks the second triple, 0x200 or more the third, anything between the first.

The trap

The port's reconnaissance named that word "pitch", and the code comments described the triples as "level, nose down, nose up". Tracing the writer showed 0752:75D8 copying the entrant's +0x0A field there. That is the car's lateral position on the road, not its pitch. The field keeps its old Rust name for compatibility; the behaviour follows the writer.

Scene headers and the horizon tracker

A road that rises and falls hides parts of itself. The original handles this without a depth buffer. When both outer edges of a record are present, the pass feeds their points to a horizon tracker (320E/3341). If the road rises above every earlier row, or dips back down after a crest, the tracker starts a scene header. Each header marks a stretch of road that will be drawn together, with the ground and sky filled up to its row.

When a point is off the screen sideways, the tracker recomputes its row from the true distance, through a three-step Newton square root (0752:024E) that lands one below the exact root for perfect squares. Afterwards 5473..5681 picks the header range, sets the horizon rows [0x136..0x13a], writes a terminator header at row 0xa4 (164), and reduces the entries before the first header to their strips.

A negative SS:0136 selects a reverse walk (entered at file offset 0x14D73), with the next physical record as origin. This is the view looking back along the track. It is independent of the mirrors.

Edges, clipping and scan conversion

After the pass, the edge builder 15F2:5682..5BB8 walks the entries from the far end and turns pairs of points into screen edges. Each entry gets a 120-byte structure: thirty four-byte descriptors, each a status byte and a pointer into a scan-line arena. A feature's point is joined to the same point of the last segment that carried that feature. If the record says the feature continues but no earlier segment supplied it, the point is joined to a fixed sentinel at screen (160, 160). A cross edge joins a feature's two points where its polygon ends.

The near plane

A point with depth below 8 has no screen position. An edge with such an endpoint cannot be clipped on screen, and the original does not try. The clipper tests the 0x10 bit and hands the edge to 15F2:0B3A..0D53, which finds where the edge crosses the plane depth = 8 in camera space and only then projects that crossing.

  1. If only one endpoint belongs to a near object, that endpoint's horizontal and depth are shifted right three, to match the other's scale. If that pushes the far endpoint inside the plane too, the edge is abandoned.
  2. The parameter of the crossing is a 2.14 fraction: ((8 − near_depth) << 14) / span, with the span floored at 8.
  3. Each axis is interpolated and projected in one step: (near << 5) + ((far − near) × t) >> 9.

The << 5 looks arbitrary. It is not. The ordinary projection is (x << 8) / depth, and on the near plane the depth is 8, so (x << 8) / 8 is x << 5. The >> 9 brings the 2.14 product back onto that scale.

From the port

The port's first projector returned "no position" for a near point and dropped any polygon that had one. Half a grandstand vanished. A provisional rasteriser that tried to draw the result flooded the frame from a vertex 17,648 pixels off screen. Near-clipped geometry lands far outside the frame by design; the answer is outcodes and a clipping fill, not a bigger canvas.

Outcodes and the screen clip

The screen clipper at 15F2:0E59 is a Cohen-Sutherland clipper. Each point carries a four-bit outcode:

The clipper orders each edge lower-to-upper and clips in a fixed order: bottom, top, left, right. The screen is half-open: pixels 0 to 319 and rows 0 to 163. But clipped endpoints keep the exclusive boundary values 320 and 164, because the scan converter and fill that follow expect them there.

The intersection arithmetic truncates twice. For a clip at distance d along an edge with deltas a and b, the clipper forms a 16.16 ratio (a << 16) / b, truncated, then multiplies and keeps the high word, truncated again. It compares the magnitudes with signed jumps even though the divide and multiply are unsigned. Every one of those details moves a pixel on some frame.

An edge rejected without a stream gets status 0x80. Bottom clipping that leaves both points beyond the same side yields 0x81 or 0x84; top clipping yields 0x88 or 0x82. The fill reads these statuses later to decide which screen border a polygon may run along.

Edge streams

The scan converter (15F2:12D2..1408) writes each surviving edge into the arena as a stream: a four-word header (lower y, upper y, lower x, upper x), then one x word for every row from the bottom upward, then the terminator 0x8000. It steps with a Bresenham-style error term that starts at the complement of half the major delta. The arena is reset every frame ([0x2aa] = 0xB458).

Filling polygons

The filler at 15F2:1409..22A8 is the only routine that paints road and object polygons. It is handed a ring of edge descriptors, a colour and the polygon's accumulated status flags ([0x640]).

The walk

The fill first computes the ring's size in bytes and stores it in two places: [0x2fa], which stays fixed for the wrap arithmetic, and [0x63c], the live count of descriptor bytes still to consume. An empty ring returns at once.

The chooser (1473..1491) walks the ring four bytes at a time, reading the word at each descriptor's stream pointer: the lower y of that edge. It keeps the maximum with a signed, strictly-greater compare, so the first of any tie wins. If that edge's stream runs in its stored direction, it starts the left side and the previous descriptor starts the right; a reversed start swaps the two. The left cursor then walks forward through the ring and the right cursor backward, until the two meet. A shared count stops either side from taking a descriptor the other already owns.

Rows are filled bottom-up. The first filled row is one above the lower y, and each row is written as the half-open span [left, right), without swapping if the two x values cross.

The three-way fork

After the chooser, 15F2:1495..14AB forks three ways, on the ring's accumulated corner bits and the chosen descriptor's 0x40 status bit:

PathConditionTargetPolygons at race start
Ano corner bits in [0x640]15F2:17979

The three paths differ in one thing. B and C pair the chosen descriptor with its successor or predecessor, exactly as A does, but they also back [0x63c] up by one four-byte entry, so a polygon that touches a screen corner consumes one more descriptor. The 0x40 bit records whether the edge's stored direction was reversed.

How we know

A breakpoint census on all three targets, over the twelve polygons that reach the fill in the race-start frame, gave the 9 / 2 / 1 split above. Every earlier probe had been set on path A, so paths B and C were invisible. On a two-descriptor ring the predecessor and the successor are the same descriptor, so for most polygons the branch cannot change the result at all. That helps explain why four earlier attempts to wire the choice each cost pixels: for most polygons the difference had to be elsewhere.

Running along the screen border

A polygon that leaves the screen has edges that stop at the border. The fill bridges the gap with a synthetic stream along the border itself: x = 0 from a 165-word table at DS:0x360..0x4A8, or x = 320 from DS:0x4F2..0x63A. The table is indexed backwards from the row the real edge ended on, and its terminator joins the next real descriptor. The polygon's flags must allow each bridge: 0x08 permits the left border up to row 0, 0x02 the right; a start clipped at the bottom bridges from row 164 under 0x01 or 0x04. Any inconsistency abandons the polygon at 1DE4, keeping the rows already filled.

Row masks: cutting out the cockpit

Rows 103 to 163 are partly hidden by the cockpit nose. Rows from the view bottom [bp+0x132] down take a masked writer (182A, with 1AAB..1B1E). Each such row has five words, stored as five parallel arrays at SS:0x6364: a canvas offset, a covered interval and an outer interval. The writer clamps the span to the outer interval, and if it crosses the covered middle it writes the surviving left and right pieces separately.

These masks are built at startup (0752:CF2B..CF9C) by scanning the cockpit picture itself for a marker colour in the left half of each row; the right bounds are the reflection about column 320. The table in the executable image is non-zero, but the game overwrites every row it uses. In cockpit frames the view bottom was observed at 103, so the mask path is live for the whole lower window. An earlier reading had concluded the masked path never ran; later captures showed it does.

The patterned colour 0x1b

One polygon colour is not a colour. 0x1b asks for a textured fill, used for things like the grandstand crowd. When the scene flag at [bp+0x124a] is clear, the fill simply writes 0x0a instead. When it is set, the fill copies each span from a 512-byte-wide pattern: its source is part of the cockpit picture (the 1,024 bytes at +1000), with four shaded copies made through the shade banks at startup (ECC3..ECE8), and a list of 64 per-row offsets produced from 4,096 draws of the game's random number generator (8150..817D). Each row advances a row index, adds that row's offset, wraps at 511 and copies.

From the port

The biggest single difference in the race-start frame was once a wedge of flat 0x1b where the original had a crowd dither: 1,699 pixels of "ours 0x1b, theirs 0x27". The grandstand call turned out to be one fill of 4,447 pixels over columns 0 to 130, rows 19 to 80, through the textured boundary path at 1E9A. It was not a missing transform at all.

Drawing the road

With every list built, 15F2:8A9A paints. First it sorts the object marks (next section but one). Then it resets thirteen polygon rings, one per polygon type, at DS:0xA70A. Each ring is 0x106 bytes: a left cursor, a right cursor, a flags word and sixty-four descriptor slots, with both cursors starting at slot 32.

The strip-only entries before the first header are drawn first. Then, for each scene header in turn:

  1. The band filler 19ED:314A fills the rows from the previous header's row down to this one with the road colour.
  2. 5EE0 draws, per entry, the left and right verges, the lateral and longitudinal feature pairs and, in a second loop, the ground beyond the outer edges.
  3. 6E95 draws the strips, the kerbs and the kerb-to-outer-edge polygons, drawing each object whose key has come due between them.

A ring collects one polygon across consecutive entries. An edge that joins the previous segment is pushed on the ring's left side (with its reversal bit flipped by xor ax,0x40) or its right side. When an entry's closing bits say the polygon ends, the cross edge closes the left side, the colour is shaded, the ring goes to the filler, and the same cross edge restarts the ring as the far edge of the next polygon.

The shade comes from 15F2:22FA. The distance is the entry's key minus the car's key [0x68], with a per-feature floor (2 for verges, 4 for pairs, 5 for strips, 1 for kerbs, 0 for ground). The shade level is (distance − 10) >> 3, capped at four levels, and indexes the four shade banks.

How we know

One Suzuka practice frame was traced fill by fill in the running original: 26 entries, 59 road fills, 18 object fills, 8 object draws and 34 framebuffer dumps. The port reproduces every road polygon's ring, colour and flags in order, and every pixel of the first thirty fills against the framebuffer the original held before its next stop, masking only pixels that objects painted. Dropping the left-side reversal flip turned the test red at the first strip. This test runs in an ordinary cargo test with no emulator.

Trackside objects and shapes

Trees, grandstands, bridges, signs, marshals and cars are all objects. Objects are placed on the track by the loader as 46-byte runtime object records, and each record's class byte indexes the circuit's object-setting table at DS:[0x23a].

Placement

The object stage at 15F2:A89A takes one runtime object record and derives its world position, heading, height, shape, palette list and scale patch. Sideways offsets use the record's lateral axis:

offset_along_axis = ((axis >> 6) × distance) >> 5

In the port's measurements an object distance of 256 is one road half-width. It then calls the shape walker at 15F2:9315. The walker looks up the view-relative rotation, moves the position into camera space, chooses near or far precision, and lets the anchor transform write the anchor that every shape point is projected from. 9454..96E5 then works out the depth used to pick a detail level, a screen-angle correction and the optional scale-word patch.

The shape format

A shape starts with a 32-byte header. Five words in it are pointers, and they divide the rest of the shape into sections in a fixed order.

The pieces:

  • Detail records start at header +0x16 and repeat every ten bytes: the largest depth the level serves, a mask that selects scale words, an angle shift and the offset of an angle-indexed table of element lists. The walker takes the first record whose depth threshold is not below the object's depth. A shape that is too far for its last level is not drawn.
  • Points are eight bytes: two axis words, a signed vertical offset, and a partner index. A point whose first word has bit 15 set is a reference point: it reuses another point's column and depth and only recomputes its own row. A shape may have at most 86 points; the projected-point records at DS:0x830..0xD90 and the visited flags at DS:0xF10..0xF66 both allow exactly that many.
  • Vectors are two-byte pairs of point indices: the edges polygons are built from.
  • Graphical elements follow the visibility test points. An indicator with bit 7 is a line (three bytes) when bit 5 is set, otherwise a bitmap (four bytes, six with an inline palette offset). Anything else is a polygon: a colour, then directed vector bytes to a zero, plus a cull point when the colour carries 0x40.
  • Element lists by angle. For the current view angle the walker shifts the angle by the detail record's shift, makes it even, and indexes the table to find which elements to draw and in what order. This is how a building shows the right faces from each side without any hidden-surface test.
The trap

Same silhouette, wrong colour means the wrong face, not a palette bug. The port first drew structures from the flat element stream instead of the detail level's angle-indexed list. Fixing it gained 0.61% of the whole race-start frame, the largest gain since the car grid landed.

Scale tables: no multiplies per point

A shape's points do not store coordinates directly. Their two axis words are offsets into three small tables that the walker builds once per object (15F2:96EC..9834) at DS:0xD90, DS:0xE10 and DS:0xE50. For every scale word the detail mask selects, the tables hold ±(scale word × rotated axis component): the vertical, the cosine and the sine of the object's view-relative heading and slope. The negated copy of slot k sits at slot k + 16.

The product is a scale word times a 1.14 component. A near object keeps it as product >> 14; a far object as product >> 17, three bits less, to match the anchor's far pre-scale. A point's camera position is then just additions:

horizontal = cos_table[first]  + sin_table[second] + anchor.horizontal
depth      = cos_table[second] − sin_table[first]  + anchor.depth
vertical   = point.vertical + vert_table[second]   + anchor.vertical

Each point is projected the first time a test or element names it, and the record is reused after that. The port verified this word for word against live records for two Phoenix shapes.

Seventeen shapes inside GP.EXE

Most shapes come from the circuit file. Seventeen live in the executable instead. 15F2:9346 resolves a shape id through a far-pointer table at 25B3:358D; ids below 17 point into the program's own data segment. Ten are polygon shapes, including shape 0, the car. Seven (5 and 7 to 12) are billboards with null section pointers and a lone detail record naming a bitmap. Shape 5 is the tree; 7 and 9 to 12 are marshals, whose bitmap bytes game logic animates at run time.

Before drawing, 15F2:AA3C..AA64 writes an object setting's word into three resident shapes: the sign-face bitmap in shapes 1 and 13, and the tree's bitmap index in shape 5. The original patches its resident copy in place, so one tree shape serves every tree species a circuit asks for.

Shade banks, distance and weather

The game has no lighting. Distance and weather are expressed by remapping palette indices through four 256-entry shade banks. The banks do not exist in the executable image; its bytes at that location are zeros. They are computed at startup from the palette by EA2D (with EA7F, EB9A and EC3D) and live at 7BCE:7BC0. The arithmetic deliberately treats palette components as raw bytes and keeps the 8086 byte wrapping, because the game's palette staging can hold values larger than six bits.

The road's polygons shade by record distance, as described above. Objects shade by depth through 15F2:9271:

  1. Take the anchor depth (an eighth of it for near objects), but at least an eighth of the shape's extent.
  2. Add 0x80 and clamp to 0..0x3c00.
  3. The level is ((high_byte − 5) >> 3) clamped to 0..4, then minus one.
  4. Level −1 copies the object's sixteen source colours unchanged. Levels 0 to 3 remap them through that shade bank.

The code has a level-4 branch that would flatten all sixteen colours to the fog colour [ss:0x1b2]. It can never run. The clamp caps the level at 4 before the decrement, so the deepest level is 3. The fog fill is dead code in v1.05.

The result is a sixteen-entry colour table per object. A polygon's colour byte is an index into that table, not a palette index. A line uses entry zero.

The trap

Because polygon colours are shade-row indices, only values 0 to 15 mean anything. When the port authored new scenery for Bahrain, the shapes decoded correctly but some fills vanished: the authored colours 0x10 to 0x30 indexed past the end of the sixteen-entry row. The encoder now rejects them.

Weather. When the wet flag SS:[0x122E] is set, three places change. The road shade clamps the distance to 0..0x78, multiplies by the fog scale SS:[0x182], and takes the high byte, clamped to four levels. The object shade takes the depth times the same scale, clamped 1..4 and decremented, so it starts at level 0 even for a nearby object. The bitmap drawer (1931) picks its bank from its divisor and the same two words. And the panorama rows are shaded, as described in the sky section.

The texture mode. 15F2:89D4 is an optional pass that runs after every road, object and nested-scene draw, and re-shades only the road and ground indices 0x12 and 0x1A in the finished canvas. Its gate is bit 7 of [ss:0x11a6], whose only writer toggles it on the release edge of an option bit, through a one-shot latch: a player-operated switch. It is not part of a default frame.

Bitmaps and billboards

Distant cars, trees, marshals, signs and wheels are bitmaps. The game keeps them in two archive files. F1GPDATA's first 32-bit word sizes its directory; a directory entry with bit 31 set routes the record to F1GPDATB. A two-byte record whose first word has bit 15 set (but not bit 14) is an alias: the drawer masks it to a byte and looks that record up instead.

Choosing a frame by angle

A billboard should show the side of the object that faces the camera. The shape's bitmap element does not name an archive record directly. It names a seed, and the selector at 15F2:9969..9A6F combines it with angles:

  • Seeds of 0x4b and above are fixed records; two indicator bits pick a centred or negated direction.
  • Seeds 0x42 to 0x4a quantise the object's own angle term in 0x1000-unit steps.
  • Lower seeds start from the view heading adjusted by a screen-angle table, fold the result into a half turn, and step through records every 0x200 units.

The screen-angle table has 256 entries indexed by the projected column's distance from the centre, 160. It holds atan(offset / 256), which matches the projection's 256 focal length: an object at the edge of the screen is seen from a slightly different angle than one in the middle. Wiring the live table removed a small bias the port had when it assumed zero.

An object can also be drawn entirely as one bitmap. After the anchor, 15F2:961F picks the detail record; a record whose scale mask has no sign bit sends the object to the billboard path at 15F2:A4E5..A648 instead of the point projector.

The bitmap drawer

The drawer at 15F2:2458 takes the record, a direction, the anchor column and row, the object's depth as the divisor and a palette list. It:

  1. derives a 13.19 fixed-point scale step from the record header and the divisor;
  2. builds an endpoint table of up to 256 screen columns, stepping by that scale (reversed for a negative direction, so the same bitmap draws mirrored);
  3. builds sixteen palette words, either straight from the palette list or through a shade bank;
  4. walks rows upward from the base row. A 32-bit accumulator advanced by the step per screen row gives the source row in its high word (25D3..2721 set it up), so bitmaps shrink vertically by the same scale as horizontally;
  5. decodes each source row as runs: a command byte picks one of the sixteen palette words, and two endpoint bytes pick columns from the endpoint table.

A row ends at its zero terminator. In the shipped archives, 658 of 9,404 row fragments hold a second, unused sequence after the terminator, in the filler before the next row's offset. The drawer ignores it, and so must any decoder. Rows from 103 down take a separate loop that applies the cockpit masks.

Cars

Cars are not a separate renderer. They are objects that borrow track records for one frame.

Marking the records

15F2:AFA3 walks the track-order ring at 25B3:0C65 backwards from the viewpoint car's own place. That ring is the spatial order of the field round the lap, sorted on record +0x26, not the race classification. For each car it accepts, it stamps one track record:

  • +0x26 gets 0xC0, +0x2a and +0x0a get bit 7;
  • +0x1e, the class byte, becomes 0x80 | place_byte, where the place byte (entrant +0x66) is twice the car's place in the track order.

Classes therefore run 0x80, 0x82, …, and the consumers' guard cmp cx,0xb4 (0x80 | 2×26) sends anything at or above it down the generic object path. The walk examines at most 26 places. It stops when a draw budget [0x2225] runs out; that budget is derived from the frame rate and clamped to 12..23. A car excluded by entrant +0x96 bit 7 costs nothing; the camera's own car, when hidden, costs a place.

If the car's own record is already claimed this frame, the walk steps forward to the next free record, and each step adds one to the car's draw-order bias at entrant +0x84. The bias starts at 1, or 2 when the folded angle between camera and road at [es:0x186] is 0x2000 or more. Both readers negate it.

After every pass has drawn, 15F2:B1A7 takes the stamps off. It is a mask, not a restore: it clears +0x26 bits 6 and 7, bit 7 of +0x2a and +0x0a, writes zero to +0x1e, and also clears bit 7 of +0x2b, which the mark never set.

The trap

The port had two of the three subtleties wrong at first. It used the entrant's index instead of twice its place, and it saved and restored the stamped bytes. A restore diverges from the original the moment a marked record already carried any of those bits, and it leaves the fifth byte's bit set when the original would have cleared it.

The draw order

The road pass collects an object mark for every record whose feature byte (or +0x26 in the counted loops) has its sign bit set: a key, a second key and the record. Before drawing, 15F2:5BB9..5E8A sorts them into two lists. Each mark's object setting supplies a draw-order offset (its word +0xa: low byte at least 2, high byte flags); car classes look theirs up through the order ring and the entrant's +0x84 bias instead.

  • A record with +0x26 bit 0x40 goes to the first list with its key reduced by the offset. That list is drawn once both strips of the current entry are present.
  • Every other mark goes to the second list with its key raised by the offset (or replaced by the distance to a car when the record's +0x1f bit 2 is set), clamped below the current header when the setting asks.
  • Both lists are bubble-sorted, largest key first. The draw loop consumes the second list before each entry.

This is the whole of the game's hidden-surface logic between road and objects: a sort key and a hand-tuned bias per object type.

Drawing a car

A marked record reaches the object stage like any other. The class picks resident shape 0, the car. Near, it is a polygon shape whose element list includes the wheels as bitmaps, gated by entrant +0x96 bit 0x20; far, the detail level switches to a billboard and the angle selector picks the frame. The team's colours come from a palette list chosen with a signed subtract, including edge cases at team 0 and team 129. Damaged or special cars (entrant +0x97 bit 7 or +0x9A bit 0x10) go through their own composition at 15F2:ABA7..AFA2.

Walls

Walls are drawn by the road pass, not the object system. Two track-file commands mark them: 0x8a sets bit 0x80 (lateral wall) and 0x8b sets bit 0x40 (longitudinal wall) in record +0x1f, across a run of records, and build a small table at scene 0xb6: five 8-entry byte tables, three computed outputs and two static sources. Suzuka carries three 0x8a and two 0x8b commands; a live capture showed 76 of its records with a wall bit.

During the pass, 15F2:3474 handles a lateral wall and 15F2:3606 a longitudinal one (the same routine reading +0x1d and bit 0x40). The wall's type (its feature byte minus 0x7c) indexes the three computed tables, which supply a height adjustment and sideways offsets, and each point goes through the same 2B49 projection as the road.

The trap

For a while every wall call site in the port passed "no walls". The whole wall draw body could be deleted and 738 tests stayed green. The four frozen Suzuka frames happened to contain no wall record. An oracle only covers the path its test takes; the project now breaks a new path deliberately before citing any suite as evidence for it.

The mirrors

The two rear-view mirrors do not render a second road scene. They are cheaper than that:

  1. Inside the sky stage, a far call into the 19ED segment copies two opaque 48 by 22 rectangles from the cockpit picture's atlas, at (0,16) and (64,16), into the mirror positions at (0,116) and (272,116). It runs only when [0x981] is zero, and before the sky and ground bands.
  2. Cars behind the player are then drawn as reflected car billboards. A shape-0 object below a scene threshold enters a reflected path; the sign of its horizontal position picks the mirror and a centre offset of −140 or +140; the usual quantised trigonometry, projector and farthest bitmap detail feed the ordinary billboard selector.
  3. Mirror bitmaps clip against their own mask table for rows 116 to 137, built at startup by scanning the mirror interiors in the cockpit picture for two marker colours. The vertical quotient is scaled by SS:017E, except for three specific records.

The port's lesson here was about finding code, not reading it. The mirror copy is a far call, so a scan for near call rel16 instructions never found it, and the routine switches its data segment mid-way, so memory dumps taken under the frame's segment returned plausible but wrong words.

How we know

The mirror replay matched 43 captured mirror shape calls, 51 bitmap canvases, 78 band canvases and 4 backdrops, as complete canvas comparisons. The first run failed at one moving-car pixel: the endpoint generator put the negative edge one step early. Fixing that shared generator made all 43 natural bitmap canvases match.

Proving it pixel by pixel

Writing a renderer that looks like F1GP is easy. Writing one that produces the same bytes is not, and nothing short of the same bytes would do for the port. The method changed several times before it worked.

The race-start page oracle

The first problem was the camera. The port's early renderer drew through a camera built from its own provisional physics, so its car was never exactly where the original's was. The way through was to pick the one frame where no physics has run: the race start. The car is stationary, so the original's own car state can be injected exactly.

The capture recipe starts the game under the emulator, selects Quick Race (the top menu item, which goes straight to Suzuka), presses Enter on the focused O.K., and waits a fixed 600 ticks of the recipe's clock. Three runs from independently staged copies of the install gave the same frame checksum and a byte-identical car record, so the frame is deterministic. The recipe asserts both hashes, and the captured frame is never committed: it is regenerated from the player's own install.

Whole-frame diffs, and the confounds

The first comparisons were scores: the percentage of the frame's 64,000 pixels that matched. The journey shows how misleading a score can be.

Six times, a "renderer defect" turned out to be the test running less of the pipeline than the game does. Among them: no panorama, no cockpit presenter, and a race-start frame that drew no competitor cars. The project's rule became: check that the test runs what play runs before theorising about a residual. The other rule was to look at the pictures. Two iterations of histograms and per-row analysis produced a wrong cause; a whole-frame difference map (red where pixels differ, a dimmed original elsewhere) named the crowd, the gantry, the barrier, car outlines, mirrors and HUD text as separate problems in one glance.

The trap

A differential test that suppresses a call and sees "no change" proves nothing unless the destination was poisoned first. Otherwise the suppressed call simply leaves the previous frame's pixels in place. This produced a false refutation of the nested pit scene routine before the poison step was added.

The exact oracle

The final acceptance does not score. It captures, from the running original, the complete memory banks after the camera stage, the resource bank, the texture state, fingerprints of the code involved, the original canvas and the presented page, for four frames: one stationary and three moving. The port then renders from those inputs and compares complete 320 by 200 pages.

Three tests pass on those captures: the runtime renderer against the original pages, the production native renderer against the same pages, and the two against each other. The run was repeated on fresh captures on 15 and 18 September 2026. By 18 September each of the sixteen circuit files had four natural captures and eight authored seam and distance cases, checked on entries, marks, headers, edges, retained road state and order state.

The claim has edges, and the project states them. The race-start captures are dry, so they do not exercise the wet shading call sites. A source-authenticated Phoenix moving frame exposed a road-edge rejection mismatch, fixed and independently verified, but the all-circuit native road comparator still needs a complete Phoenix rerun before parity is claimed for every circuit.

The enhanced renderer

Once the exact renderer matched, the port added an optional second picture: the same scene redrawn at the window's resolution, with sharp edges instead of scaled-up pixels. It is off by default and chosen on the Big Race options page.

The exact frame is always drawn anyway. It reads and writes game memory during a frame (car marks on road records, the pit-crew overlay's use of the shared random number generator), so skipping it would desynchronise the race. And it cannot simply be given a bigger canvas: the scan-line arena, the edge rings and every rounding step assume 320 by 164.

So the enhanced renderer replays the exact one. Every terminal drawing site of the exact pipeline, main and pit scenes alike, can hand a command to a sink in the order it paints:

The key observation came from the design review: every writer in the exact pipeline is an opaque overwrite into one layer, in back-to-front order, with no coverage test inside any fill. The recorded sequence is therefore a complete painter's order, and replaying it needs no depth buffer. An earlier design that recorded camera-space road points was rejected by three independent reviewers and an external review, for concrete reasons: the road's points are projected from track space, two of the eighteen slots are derived rather than projected, near points keep the previous frame's screen words, and a fill's edge endpoints are found by feature carry rather than adjacency. Only the resolved screen-space commands hold exactly what was painted.

The replay scales x and y independently (x × W / 320, y × H / 164) and fills polygons from float edges with half-open pixel coverage, so edges are straight and sharp. Per-pixel patterns (the sky dither, the 0x1b crowd, the panorama) sample the source pixel, so they look as they do in the original, only bigger. Bitmaps use the original row-walk rule with the geometry scaled and nearest-neighbour sampling. The output is palette indices, not colours, so palette fades apply to both halves of the picture and a palette-only change is a lookup. The cockpit, HUD and mirrors stay as the original draws them, scaled.

Measured in a release build at 1280 by 960, a full composition plus a cached palette-only one took 1.7 ms. Replayed at the native 320 by 164 against the in-repo Suzuka fixture, 0.21% of pixels had a colour that appears nowhere in the matching 3 by 3 neighbourhood of the exact frame. And with the renderer on, the game's memory after a frame is byte-identical to the off case: the new picture only reads.

How it works

The whole renderer reduces to one idea the enhanced mode makes visible: F1GP decides everything at 320 by 164, in integers, and then paints in order. Reproduce the decisions exactly and the order comes for free. That is why the port could prove the original pixel for pixel first, and only then draw it at any size.
