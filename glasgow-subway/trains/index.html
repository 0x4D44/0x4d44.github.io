Engineering notes · Glasgow Subway

Small trains,
big current

The Subway is a 10.5 km circle of two single-bore tunnels, each only 3.35 m across. Four generations of train have squeezed through them: a steel rope and a steam engine, a third rail, orange cars with automatic driving, and now a four-car, driverless-ready Stadler. This page looks at the machines, at the 600 V DC system behind them, and at what a lap of the circle costs in energy.

The machines

1896-1935

Cable gripper cars

The 1896 Subway had no motors at all. A single power house on Scotland Street, between West Street and Shields Road stations, drove two endless steel cables, one per tunnel, at roughly 12.5 mph (some accounts say up to 15 mph). Each rope was 1.5 inches thick and about 36,300 ft (11 km) long, a little longer than the 6.5 mile circuit it served. It ran over about 1,700 sheaves per tunnel, a few inches above the rails, and never stopped while the line was open.

Two horizontal Corliss steam engines (42 in bore, 6 ft stroke, about 1,500 hp each, 55 rpm) turned 14 ft grooved drums through a 25 ft, 50-ton flywheel, with eight Lancashire boilers and mechanical stokers behind them. One engine was a spare. A Tension Run 193 ft long, now a listed structure, let a weighted carriage take up the stretch. Four small dynamos supplied the car lighting.

The car carried a gripper: a steel-faced clamp under the leading bogie that the driver closed on the passing rope with a handwheel. Closing it hauled the car at cable speed; opening it let the car coast to a stop in the station. The cars (about 9 t, 48 seats, 40 ft long) ran in pairs with a 5 t trailer. There was no rail link to the workshops, so cars were craned in and out at Govan. Headway was 3.5 minutes.

1935-1977

Electrified cars

Glasgow Corporation bought the Subway in 1923 and spent a decade deciding what to do with it. A third rail was proposed in 1924 so that night work would not interrupt day service. In 1934 the newest gripper car, No. 60, had its wheelbase stretched by 6 inches to take two 60 hp motors and was tested with a 10-ton load. Orders followed for 19 cars and 38 power bogies, with Metropolitan-Vickers traction equipment, Westinghouse air brakes, air-operated doors and Hurst Nelson underframes.

The inner circle went electric on 31 March 1935; the last cable-hauled train ran on 30 November 1935. The conversion cost about GBP 120,000. Power came from Pinkston power station through existing tramway substations (Dalhousie Street, Kinning Park and Partick), then down cables to the tunnel. The old pick-up wheels became skids. Interior lighting was still taken from two wall-mounted T-iron rails until 1977. The crew fell from three to two.

These cars, the "Shoogly Train", lasted until the line closed in 1977. Motor ratings per car, top speed and the rail layout are the things I could not establish from open sources.

1980-2024

Metro-Cammell “Clockwork Orange”

The 1977-80 rebuild closed the Subway for almost three years and replaced nearly everything. Metro-Cammell of Washwood Heath built 33 cars between 1977 and 1979. Each is 12.58 m long, 2.34 m wide and 2.65 m high and weighs about 20 t. Power cars have a cab at one end and four GEC traction motors. They ran as two-car married sets; in 1992 eight centre trailers from Hunslet of Leeds built some sets up to three cars, giving the 37.74 m, 270-passenger trains familiar until 2024. Exactly how many sets were 2-car and how many 3-car is not clear from the sources.

The fleet came with automatic train operation: the driver closed the doors and pressed a button, and the train accelerated, cruised and stopped itself at the platform. Line speed was about 54 km/h. ABB at Derby refurbished the power cars in 1993-95, and the 1996 resignalling brought computer interlocking (VPI). Power came from five substations around the circle (Broomloan Depot, Byres Road, Dundasvale, Bridge Street and Cornwall Street); the network can run a full service with any one out.

What I could not find: motor ratings, the control gear (resistance, chopper or otherwise), brake type, and whether any braking energy was returned to the rail. Treat any claim on those as unverified.

2023-

Stadler METRO

SPT awarded Stadler and Ansaldo STS (now Hitachi Rail) a GBP 200 m contract in March 2016 for 17 four-car trains, signalling, telecoms, an operations control centre and platform screen doors, inside a GBP 288 m modernisation. The trains were built at Altenrhein, shown at InnoTrans in 2018, first delivered in May 2019 and entered service on 11 December 2023. The Metro-Cammell fleet ended on 28 June 2024.

The train is two identical half-trains joined by a Scharfenberg coupler, with an open gangway throughout. Each half has a power car on two motored bogies and a trailer car whose single bogie sits at the centre of the set: Bo′Bo′ + 2′ + 2′ + Bo′Bo′, twelve axles in all with eight driven. Bogies have a wheelbase of just 1,650 mm, pneumatic suspension, resilient wheels (540 mm new, 480 mm worn) and radial steering. Small wheels let the floor sit at 695 mm so the gangways are level.

Each driven axle has a water-cooled, three-phase asynchronous motor (TSA TMW 28-23-4, 68 kW) through a 10.06:1 two-stage gearbox, fed by redundant water-cooled IGBT converters from ABB. Braking is regenerative. The CBTC system (Hitachi) tracks each train by tachometer and trackside balises, and the train can start, stop and operate its doors itself; the temporary driver’s cab will go once unattended operation is approved.

The power system

Today’s Subway is supplied at 600 V DC through a conductor rail beside the track. The current path is conventional for a third-rail metro: positive rail, train, running rails, negative feeder cables, substation. The numbers around it are partly known, partly inferred, and the charts below are labelled which is which.

Collecting the current

What we know

  • 600 V DC, third rail, collected by shoe (skid since 1935)
  • Gauge 4 ft (1,219-1,220 mm)
  • Warning legend “DANGER LIVE RAIL” stencilled on the tunnel wall (photographs)
  • Rail polarity: positive live rail, negative running rails (standard practice; not stated for the Subway)
  • Traction negative feeder cables replaced at Byres Road and Dundasvale 2023

From the grid to the wheel

  • Grid to substation. A tender notice describes the DC system as fed from an 11 kV or 6.6 kV supply and rectified to 600 V DC, with five substations, one inside the depot . SPT’s 2023 paper confirms it has analysed “the supporting High Voltage supply network” for compliance with modern standards . The connection point and utility are not stated.
  • Rectification. Each substation steps the HV down and rectifies it. Their ratings are not published. For scale: one Stadler train takes about 1 MW (1.7 kA at 600 V) while accelerating, and the chart below shows 3 to 5 trains doing so at once on a busy lap. A rating of a few MW per substation would therefore be reasonable, but this is my estimate .
  • Redundancy. The network can run a full service of six trains per circle with one substation inoperative (Wikipedia, as of the legacy fleet) . Try the outage tool below.
  • Regeneration. Rail Engineer says the IGBT drive provides regenerative braking . In a DC network only a train that is accelerating nearby can absorb it, and a diode rectifier substation cannot send power back to the grid. SPT’s paper says “the capturing and storage of regeneration braking energy is being assessed” as part of the substation design .
  • History. 1896-1935 the energy source was coal under eight Lancashire boilers at Scotland Street. In 1935 it became a share of the city’s tramway supply from Pinkston power station. The five-substation arrangement appears to belong to the 1977-80 rebuild, but I found no date.

The line is a roller-coaster. How the numbers were checked

No single source covers the Subway in depth, and the sources disagree. These cross-checks are what I did to decide what to believe.

  1. Stadler motor power. Wikipedia lists 4 x 68 kW = 272 kW. But the train has eight driven axles (two half-trains, each with a Bo′Bo′ power car), so the whole train has 8 x 68 = 544 kW of motors. Stadler’s datasheet says 520 kW continuous at the wheel, 95.6% of 544 kW, which is a believable transmission efficiency. So 272 kW is one half-train.
  2. Peak power and tractive effort. 130 kN at 23 km/h (6.39 m/s) is 831 kW. The datasheet says 820 kW maximum at the wheel. They agree to 1.3%.
  3. Mass. No tare mass was published. The drive supplier lists a 7.5 t axle load; with twelve axles, that is 90 t at crush load. Subtract 310 passengers at 75 kg (23 t) and the tare is about 67 t. Check: 130 kN divided by 90 t with 8% for rotating parts gives 1.34 m/s2, matching the datasheet’s 1.3 m/s2 gross starting acceleration. Label: derived, low.
  4. Old train length. Three Metro-Cammell cars at 12.58 m is 37.74 m, which is the figure Rail Engineer gives. The 39.24 m sometimes quoted for the old stock is really the new train.
  5. Seats and standing. 104 + 6 + 200 = 310, which matches Rail Engineer’s 310. Rolling Stock World reports 116 seats and 204 standing, a slightly different count, so a range of 310-322 is fair.
  6. Cable length. Each cable is 36,300 ft (6.9 miles) against a 6.5 mile circuit: one endless loop with a tension run, not an out-and-back. The 57 t weight is on the heavy side for 1.5 in rope; I rate it medium.
  7. Contract value. Most sources say GBP 200 m for trains and signalling; one Wikipedia passage says GBP 280 m. The 200 m figure is repeated by Railway Gazette and Rail Engineer.
  8. Lap time. The model gives about 22.4 minutes for a lap with 25 s dwells. Published sources say a circuit takes about 24 minutes. The 7% difference is plausible: the model has no timetable margin or restrictions.
  9. Where I could not verify. Substation ratings and exact positions; the regeneration capability of the 1980 fleet; the rail layout detail; tunnel ventilation arrangements; the annual energy bill. These are left as unknown or flagged as estimates.

What I did not find

No open source gives the Subway’s tunnel ventilation arrangements, the stray-current control scheme, or annual traction energy. In general a 3.35 m bore with a 2.3 m wide train has a very high blockage ratio, so each train acts as a piston and pushes air through stations and shafts; and in a wet tunnel with rail return, insulated running rails and negative feeders are the usual tools to hold stray current down. Both statements are general engineering, not Subway facts. SPT papers do mention 21 pumping stations dealing with groundwater, and earthing studies for the substations.
