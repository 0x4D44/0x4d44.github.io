Algebra & functions

Algebra is the language of the whole course, and it earns the biggest share of the marks: about 30–45% across the two papers. This deck covers the algebra skills in the course specification: polynomials, quadratics, functions and their graphs, exponentials and logarithms, and recurrence relations.

Captain Grant has put the cadets to work in the purser's office, where HMS Lodestone's stores, accounts and supplies are kept. Algebra is how the purser predicts what will be left and when it will run out.

1 · Polynomials and the factor theorem ALG · polynomials

A polynomial is a sum of terms, each a number times a whole-number power of $x$, such as $2x^3 + x^2 - 13x + 6$. Its degree is the highest power: 2 for a quadratic, 3 for a cubic, 4 for a quartic. The numbers in front of the powers are the coefficients. A root (or zero) of $f(x)$ is a value of $x$ that makes $f(x) = 0$. On a graph, the roots are where the curve crosses or touches the $x$-axis.

At National 5 you factorised quadratics by spotting numbers. A cubic or quartic is too big to spot, so Higher gives you one tool: the factor theorem.

📘 The factor theorem

For a polynomial $f(x)$: if $f(a) = 0$, then $(x - a)$ is a factor of $f(x)$. The reverse is also true: if $(x - a)$ is a factor, then $f(a) = 0$.

More generally (the remainder theorem), when $f(x)$ is divided by $(x - a)$ the remainder is $f(a)$. A remainder of zero means $(x-a)$ divides exactly: it is a factor.

Watch the signs. A root $x = 2$ gives the factor $(x - 2)$. A root $x = -3$ gives $(x + 3)$.

Synthetic division

You could test a value by substituting it, but synthetic division does two jobs at once. It finds the remainder $f(a)$ and gives you the quotient, the polynomial left over after dividing by $(x - a)$. Here it is for $f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 13x + 6$ with $a = 2$.

The remainder is $0$, so $(x-2)$ is a factor, and the bottom row says the quotient is $2x^2 + 5x - 3$:

$$2x^3 + x^2 - 13x + 6 = (x-2)(2x^2 + 5x - 3) = (x-2)(2x-1)(x+3).$$

So the equation $f(x) = 0$ has roots $x = 2$, $x = \tfrac12$ and $x = -3$.

The purser's divider: synthetic division, step by step

Type the coefficients, highest power first, and include a 0 for any missing power ($x^3 - 7x + 6$ is 1, 0, -7, 6). Pick a trial root and step through. Or press Hunt for roots to test every whole-number factor of the constant term, the way you would in an exam.

Which numbers should I try?

If all the coefficients are whole numbers, any whole-number root must be a factor of the constant term. For $x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6$ the candidates are $\pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \pm6$. Try the small ones first; $x = 1$ is easiest because $f(1)$ is just the sum of the coefficients. When the leading coefficient is not 1 there might be a fraction root, but exam questions almost always give you a factor or a whole-number root to start from.

When the quadratic factor will not factorise

Sometimes the quotient is a quadratic with no real roots. Then the polynomial has fewer real roots than its degree. To prove that the quadratic cannot be factorised, show that its discriminant is negative (see the next chapter).

2 · Quadratics: completing the square and the discriminant ALG · quadratics

Every quadratic $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ has a U-shaped graph called a parabola. If $a \gt 0$ it opens upwards and has a minimum turning point; if $a \lt 0$ it opens downwards and has a maximum. The gun crews know the shape well: a shot fired from the bow chaser follows (nearly) a downward-opening parabola.

Completing the square

Completing the square rewrites $ax^2 + bx + c$ as $a(x + p)^2 + q$. In that form you can read off the turning point, $(-p,\ q)$, and its nature, from the sign of $a$. The axis of symmetry is the line $x = -p$. At Higher the coefficient $a$ need not be 1.

The name comes from a picture. $x^2 + 6x$ is an $x$-by-$x$ square plus six $x$-by-1 strips. Split the strips into two groups of three and place them on two sides of the square. The shape is now almost a bigger square, $(x+3)$ by $(x+3)$, with a $3$-by-$3$ corner missing. So $x^2 + 6x = (x+3)^2 - 9$.

Block-built squares: completing the square

Set $a$, $b$ and $c$. The blocks complete the square on $x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x$ (they can only show positive lengths, so they appear when $\frac{b}{a} \gt 0$). The algebra on the right works for any values, and the graph marks the turning point it predicts.

Worked example 4 · P1 · $x^2$ coefficient not 1

Express $2x^2 + 12x + 5$ in the form $a(x+p)^2 + q$, and state the coordinates of the turning point of $y = 2x^2 + 12x + 5$.

Show solution
  1. $2(x^2 + 6x) + 5$ Take the $x^2$ coefficient out of the first two terms only.
  2. $2\big[(x+3)^2 - 9\big] + 5$ Complete the square inside the bracket: half of 6 is 3, and subtract $3^2$.
  3. $2(x+3)^2 - 13$ Multiply out the outer bracket: $2 \times (-9) = -18$, and $-18 + 5 = -13$.

Turning point $(-3,\ -13)$. It is a minimum because $a = 2 \gt 0$.

Worked example 5 · P1 · a negative $x^2$ coefficient

Write $5 + 4x - x^2$ in the form $a(x+p)^2 + q$.

Show solution
  1. $-x^2 + 4x + 5 = -(x^2 - 4x) + 5$ Reorder, then take out $-1$. Inside, the sign of $4x$ flips.
  2. $-\big[(x-2)^2 - 4\big] + 5$ Half of $-4$ is $-2$.
  3. $-(x-2)^2 + 9$ $-1 \times (-4) = +4$, and $4 + 5 = 9$.

So $a = -1$, $p = -2$, $q = 9$, and the graph has a maximum turning point at $(2,\ 9)$.

⚠ Exam lookout · the number outside

The most common slip is in the last step: forgetting to multiply the $-9$ (or $-4$) by the $a$ outside the bracket. Expand your answer back out to check. $2(x+3)^2 - 13 = 2x^2 + 12x + 18 - 13$, which is correct.

The discriminant

The quadratic formula solves $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$

You must know this formula; it is not on the formulae list. The part under the square root, $b^2 - 4ac$, is called the discriminant. Its sign tells you how many real roots there are, without solving anything.

The gunner's discriminant: how many roots?

Move $a$, $b$ and $c$ and watch the parabola cross, touch or miss the $x$-axis as $b^2 - 4ac$ changes sign. Turn on the shading to see the solution of a quadratic inequality.

Finding an unknown from the nature of the roots

A typical question gives a quadratic with an unknown $k$ and tells you about its roots. Write down the right condition on the discriminant and solve for $k$. Be careful: the condition may lead to a quadratic inequality in $k$.

Worked example 6 · P1 · equal roots

Find the values of $k$ for which $kx^2 - 6x + k = 0$ has equal roots.

Show solution
  1. $a = k,\ b = -6,\ c = k$ and equal roots means $b^2 - 4ac = 0$ State the condition.
  2. $(-6)^2 - 4 \times k \times k = 36 - 4k^2 = 0$ Substitute. Brackets round the $-6$.
  3. $k^2 = 9 \Rightarrow k = 3$ or $k = -3$ Solve.

Worked example 7 · P1 · no real roots, so an inequality

Find the range of values of $k$ for which $x^2 + (k+1)x + 4 = 0$ has no real roots.

Show solution
  1. $b^2 - 4ac \lt 0$ The condition for no real roots.
  2. $(k+1)^2 - 4 \times 1 \times 4 \lt 0 \Rightarrow k^2 + 2k - 15 \lt 0$ Substitute and simplify: $k^2 + 2k + 1 - 16$.
  3. $(k+5)(k-3) \lt 0$: the parabola in $k$ has roots $-5$ and $3$. Factorise and find the roots.
  4. $-5 \lt k \lt 3$ It opens upwards, so it is below zero between the roots. Sketch it to be sure.

Quadratic inequalities

To solve $ax^2 + bx + c \gt 0$ (or $\lt$, $\ge$, $\le$), never divide or square-root. Instead: find the roots, sketch the parabola through them, and read off where the graph is above (or below) the axis.

Worked example 8 · P1 · a downward parabola

Solve $6 + x - x^2 \ge 0$.

Show solution
  1. $6 + x - x^2 = -(x^2 - x - 6) = -(x-3)(x+2)$, so the roots are $x = 3$ and $x = -2$. Find the roots.
  2. The coefficient of $x^2$ is negative, so the parabola is $\cap$-shaped and is on or above the axis between its roots. Sketch.
  3. $-2 \le x \le 3$ State the answer, including the roots because of $\ge$.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • complete the square when the $x^2$ coefficient is not 1, and read off the turning point;
  • state and use the three discriminant conditions, including "real roots" ($\ge 0$);
  • find an unknown from the nature of the roots, even when that needs a quadratic inequality;
  • solve a quadratic inequality with a sketch.

3 · Where graphs meet: intersections and tangents ALG · intersections

Two graphs meet where their $y$-values are equal. So to find the intersection of $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$, solve $f(x) = g(x)$. Then substitute each $x$ back into either equation to get $y$.

Worked example 9 · P1 · a line and a parabola

Find the points of intersection of $y = x^2 - 3x + 4$ and $y = x + 1$.

Show solution
  1. $x^2 - 3x + 4 = x + 1$ Equate the two expressions for $y$.
  2. $x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0 \Rightarrow (x-1)(x-3) = 0$ Rearrange to "$= 0$" and factorise.
  3. $x = 1$ or $x = 3$ Solve.
  4. $(1,\ 2)$ and $(3,\ 4)$ Substitute into $y = x + 1$, the simpler equation.

Tangency: one point, equal roots

When you equate a line and a parabola you get a quadratic. Its discriminant tells you what the line does:

  • $b^2 - 4ac \gt 0$: the line cuts the curve at two points;
  • $b^2 - 4ac = 0$: the line meets the curve at exactly one point. It is a tangent;
  • $b^2 - 4ac \lt 0$: the line misses the curve.

Sighting the shot: when does a line just touch?

The curve is $y = x^2 + 4$. Move the line $y = mx + k$ and watch the quadratic $x^2 - mx + (4 - k) = 0$ that their meeting gives. Tangency happens exactly when its discriminant is zero.

Worked example 10 · P1 · show that a line is a tangent

Show that the line $y = 4x - 5$ is a tangent to the curve $y = x^2 - 2x + 4$, and find the point of contact.

Show solution
  1. $x^2 - 2x + 4 = 4x - 5 \Rightarrow x^2 - 6x + 9 = 0$ Equate and rearrange.
  2. $b^2 - 4ac = 36 - 36 = 0$ Find the discriminant.
  3. The discriminant is zero, so there are equal roots and the line is a tangent. Communicate the conclusion.
  4. $(x-3)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 3$, $y = 4(3) - 5 = 7$. Point of contact $(3,\ 7)$. Find the point.

Factorising to $(x-3)^2 = 0$ and saying "repeated root, so tangent" is an equally good method.

The same idea answers "find $k$ so that $y = 2x + k$ is a tangent to $y = x^2 + 4$": equate to get $x^2 - 2x + (4 - k) = 0$, set $b^2 - 4ac = 4 - 4(4 - k) = 0$ and solve: $k = 3$.

Two curves work the same way. For $y = x^2 + 2$ and $y = 2x^2 - 2$: $x^2 + 2 = 2x^2 - 2$ gives $x^2 = 4$, so $x = \pm 2$, and both points have $y = 6$.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • find where a line meets a curve, or two curves meet, by equating and solving;
  • use the discriminant to prove tangency or to find an unknown that makes a line a tangent.

4 · Functions: domain, range, composite and inverse ALG · functions

A function is a rule that takes each allowed input and gives exactly one output. Think of a Minecraft furnace: put in iron ore, get out an iron ingot, every time. $f(x) = 3x - 1$ is a furnace that triples whatever goes in and subtracts one.

  • The domain is the set of inputs the function is allowed to take.
  • The range is the set of outputs it actually produces.

In Higher questions, the domain is usually "all real numbers except the ones that break something". Three things break a function:

Dividing by zero
$f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-4}$ needs $x \ne 4$.
Square root of a negative
$f(x) = \sqrt{x+3}$ needs $x \ge -3$.
Log of zero or a negative
$f(x) = \log_2(x-1)$ needs $x \gt 1$.

Ranges matter too. $g(x) = x^2 + 1$ can take any $x$, but its outputs are all at least 1: the range is $g(x) \ge 1$. Likewise $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ have range $-1$ to $1$.

The course also uses set notation. You need to read it, not write it: $x \in \mathbb{R}$ means "$x$ is a real number". The sets are $\mathbb{N}$ (natural numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots$), $\mathbb{W}$ (whole numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots$), $\mathbb{Z}$ (integers), $\mathbb{Q}$ (rational numbers) and $\mathbb{R}$ (real numbers). The symbol $\notin$ means "is not a member of".

The words you may meet: a set is a collection of objects, written in curly brackets, such as $\{1, 2, 3\}$. Each object is a member or element of the set, written $2 \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. A subset is a set made of some of the members of another: $\{1, 3\} \subset \{1, 2, 3\}$, and $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$. The empty set, $\{\ \}$ or $\emptyset$, has no members: the set of real solutions of $x^2 = -1$ is empty. A domain is often written like $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \ne 4\}$, read "all real $x$ such that $x$ is not 4".

Composite functions

A composite function feeds the output of one function into another. $f(g(x))$ means "do $g$ first, then $f$". Read it from the inside out, like two furnaces in a row where the hopper from $g$ feeds $f$.

Two furnaces in a row: composite functions

Choose $f$ and $g$ and an input. The minecart carries the value through $g$ first, then $f$. Swap the order to see that $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ are usually different.

Worked example 11 · P1 · both orders

$f(x) = 3x - 1$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 2$. Find (a) $f(g(x))$ and (b) $g(f(x))$.

Show solution
  1. (a) $f(g(x)) = f(x^2 + 2) = 3(x^2 + 2) - 1 = 3x^2 + 5$ Replace every $x$ in $f$ with the whole of $g(x)$, in a bracket.
  2. (b) $g(f(x)) = g(3x - 1) = (3x-1)^2 + 2 = 9x^2 - 6x + 3$ Now $f(x)$ goes into $g$.

Worked example 12 · P1 · domain of a composite

$f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 5}$ and $g(x) = x^2 + 1$. Find $f(g(x))$ and state a suitable domain.

Show solution
  1. $f(g(x)) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1 - 5} = \dfrac{1}{x^2 - 4}$ Substitute $g(x)$ into $f$ and simplify.
  2. $x^2 - 4 = 0$ when $x = \pm 2$, so the domain is $x \in \mathbb{R},\ x \ne \pm 2$ Exclude any value that makes the denominator zero.

Worked example 13 · P1 · using the range

$f(x) = \log_2 x$ and $g(x) = 2 + \cos x$. Explain why $f(g(x))$ is defined for every real number $x$.

Show solution
  1. $-1 \le \cos x \le 1$, so the range of $g$ is $1 \le g(x) \le 3$. Find the range of the inner function.
  2. Every output of $g$ is positive, and $\log_2$ is defined for all positive inputs, so $f(g(x))$ is defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Link the range of $g$ to the domain of $f$.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

On composite functions, "most candidates did not apply knowledge of the range of a function". The question to ask is always: what can come out of the inner function, and can the outer function cope with it?

Inverse functions

The inverse $f^{-1}$ undoes $f$: if $f(3) = 1$ then $f^{-1}(1) = 3$. Running a furnace backwards, ingot to ore. Only a function that never gives the same output twice (a one-to-one function) has an inverse. To find it:

  1. Write $y = f(x)$.
  2. Rearrange to make $x$ the subject.
  3. Swap the letters: replace $y$ with $x$, and write the result as $f^{-1}(x) = \dots$.

Worked example 14 · P1 · inverse of a rational function

$f(x) = \dfrac{2}{x - 1}$, $x \ne 1$. Find $f^{-1}(x)$.

Show solution
  1. $y = \dfrac{2}{x-1} \Rightarrow x - 1 = \dfrac{2}{y}$ Start to rearrange: multiply by $(x-1)$, divide by $y$.
  2. $x = 1 + \dfrac{2}{y}$ Make $x$ the subject.
  3. $f^{-1}(x) = 1 + \dfrac{2}{x}$, $x \ne 0$ Swap the letters and write it as $f^{-1}(x)$.

Check: $f(3) = 1$ and $f^{-1}(1) = 1 + 2 = 3$.

Graphically, the inverse is the reflection of $y = f(x)$ in the line $y = x$, because reflecting swaps every point $(a, b)$ to $(b, a)$. The domain of $f^{-1}$ is the range of $f$.

The mirror in the chart room: inverse functions

Choose a function and press Reflect. Each marked point $(a, b)$ swings across the line $y = x$ to $(b, a)$, and the reflected curve is the graph of $f^{-1}$.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

"A few candidates stated multiple expressions for '$y = \dots$' or '$g^{-1}(x) = \dots$', which did not gain full marks." Give one final answer, clearly written as $g^{-1}(x) = \dots$, and score out any rough attempts.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • state a suitable domain, and use the range of an inner function;
  • find $f(g(x))$ and $g(f(x))$ for algebraic, trigonometric, exponential or log functions;
  • find $f^{-1}(x)$ by rearranging, and know its graph is the reflection in $y = x$.

5 · Graph transformations ALG · related functions

If you know the graph of $y = f(x)$, you can sketch many related graphs without plotting a single point. There are four basic moves, and every one is a rule about what happens to each point $(x, y)$.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why does $f(x + 3)$ move the graph left? Plus usually means right."

Sailing Master Fraser: "Ask where the new graph reaches a particular height. Say $f(0) = 5$. The new graph $y = f(x+3)$ gives 5 when $x + 3 = 0$, that is when $x = -3$. Every feature now happens 3 earlier, which on a chart means 3 to the left."

The chart-table transformer: $y = a\,f(bx + c) + d$

Pick a base graph, set the four numbers, then press Transform. The original stays faint, and the labelled points travel to their new positions so you can see each rule at work.

Combining transformations: follow the points

In an exam you sketch the new graph by moving the labelled points: turning points, intercepts and any marked coordinates. Do the moves one at a time to each point. For $y = af(x + c) + d$: first shift the $x$-coordinate (subtract $c$), then multiply the $y$-coordinate by $a$, then add $d$ to it.

With a number multiplying $x$ inside the bracket, $y = f(bx + c)$, a point at $x_0$ moves to $\dfrac{x_0 - c}{b}$: shift first, then squash. For example $y = f(2x)$ halves every $x$-coordinate, so the graph is squashed towards the $y$-axis. The widget below uses this general rule.

Worked example 15 · P1 · two labelled points

The graph of $y = f(x)$ has a maximum turning point at $(-1,\ 2)$ and a minimum turning point at $(1,\ -2)$. Sketch $y = 2f(x - 1) + 3$, showing the images of the turning points.

Show solution
  1. $f(x - 1)$: move right 1. $(-1, 2) \to (0, 2)$ and $(1, -2) \to (2, -2)$. Inside the bracket, $-1$ means right 1.
  2. $2f(\dots)$: double each $y$. $(0, 2) \to (0, 4)$ and $(2, -2) \to (2, -4)$. Vertical stretch, scale factor 2.
  3. $+3$: up 3. Maximum $(0,\ 7)$, minimum $(2,\ -1)$. Draw the same shape through these points.

Since $a = 2$ is positive, the maximum is still a maximum. If $a$ were negative, the reflection would turn maxima into minima.

6 · Exponentials and logarithms ALG · logs and exponentials

An exponential function has the variable in the power: $f(x) = a^x$, where the base $a$ is positive and not 1. If $a \gt 1$ the graph grows ever faster; if $0 \lt a \lt 1$ it decays towards zero. Every graph $y = a^x$ passes through $(0, 1)$ (because $a^0 = 1$) and through $(1, a)$, and gets ever closer to the $x$-axis without touching it: the line $y = 0$ is an asymptote.

The most important base is $e \approx 2.718$. The function $y = e^x$ is called the exponential function, and it appears whenever something grows or decays at a rate in proportion to its size.

📘 What a logarithm is

$\log_a x$ is the power you raise $a$ to in order to get $x$:

$$y = \log_a x \iff x = a^y.$$

So $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$, and $\log_{10} 0.01 = -2$ because $10^{-2} = 0.01$. The natural logarithm $\ln x$ means $\log_e x$.

Because logs undo exponentials, the graph of $y = \log_a x$ is the reflection of $y = a^x$ in the line $y = x$. It passes through $(1, 0)$ and $(a, 1)$, and the $y$-axis is an asymptote. You can only take the log of a positive number.

The log line: an exponential and its mirror

Change the base $a$. The exponential graph and the log graph are always reflections of each other in $y = x$. Notice the fixed points $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$, and the points $(1, a)$ and $(a, 1)$.

The laws of logarithms

These come straight from the laws of indices, and are not on the formulae list. For $x, y \gt 0$:

$$\log_a x + \log_a y = \log_a (xy)$$

Adding logs multiplies the numbers.

$$\log_a x - \log_a y = \log_a \frac{x}{y}$$

Subtracting logs divides.

$$\log_a x^n = n\log_a x$$

A power comes down to the front, and a number in front can go up as a power.

$$\log_a 1 = 0,\quad \log_a a = 1$$

Because $a^0 = 1$ and $a^1 = a$.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Is $\log(x + y)$ the same as $\log x + \log y$?"

Captain Grant: "No, and the purser would lose his job if it were. There is no law for the log of a sum. $\log_{10}(10 + 10) = \log_{10} 20 \approx 1.30$, but $\log_{10} 10 + \log_{10} 10 = 2$. The laws only turn a sum of logs into the log of a product."

Worked example 16 · P1 · simplify

Evaluate (a) $\log_2 40 - \log_2 5$ and (b) $2\log_5 10 - \log_5 4$.

Show solution
  1. (a) $\log_2 \frac{40}{5} = \log_2 8 = 3$ Subtracting logs divides; $2^3 = 8$.
  2. (b) $\log_5 10^2 - \log_5 4 = \log_5 \frac{100}{4} = \log_5 25 = 2$ Move the 2 up as a power first, then combine.

Solving log equations

Combine everything into a single log, then convert to exponential form using $\log_a x = y \iff x = a^y$. Finally check each answer: you cannot take the log of zero or a negative, so some algebraic solutions must be rejected.

Worked example 17 · P1 · reject a solution

Solve $\log_3(x+1) + \log_3(x-1) = 1$, $x \gt 1$.

Show solution
  1. $\log_3\big((x+1)(x-1)\big) = 1$ Use a log law to make one log.
  2. $(x+1)(x-1) = 3^1 \Rightarrow x^2 - 1 = 3$ Convert to exponential form.
  3. $x^2 = 4 \Rightarrow x = 2$ (since $x \gt 1$, reject $x = -2$) Solve and use the domain.

Worked example 18 · P1 · a quotient

Solve $\log_2(x+5) - \log_2(x-2) = 3$.

Show solution
  1. $\log_2 \dfrac{x+5}{x-2} = 3$ Subtracting logs divides.
  2. $\dfrac{x+5}{x-2} = 2^3 = 8$ Exponential form.
  3. $x + 5 = 8x - 16 \Rightarrow 7x = 21 \Rightarrow x = 3$ Check: $x - 2 = 1 \gt 0$, so both logs exist.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

"Some candidates did not convert from logarithmic form to exponential form correctly, and some candidates incorrectly attempted to 'cancel out' logs." You can never cross out "log" from both sides of $\log_2(x+5) - \log_2(x-2) = 3$. Combine into one log, then use the definition.

Solving exponential equations

When the unknown is in a power, take logs of both sides (any base, but $\ln$ or $\log_{10}$ is on your calculator) and bring the power down.

Worked example 19 · P2 · unknown in the power

Solve (a) $5^x = 30$ and (b) $e^{2x} = 7$.

Show solution
  1. (a) $\ln 5^x = \ln 30 \Rightarrow x\ln 5 = \ln 30 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{\ln 30}{\ln 5} = 2.11$ Take logs; the power comes down.
  2. (b) $2x = \ln 7 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{\ln 7}{2} = 0.973$ $\ln$ undoes $e$ directly.

Modelling with exponentials

Real things often grow or decay exponentially: money with compound interest, a population, a cooling cannon barrel. A typical model is $A = A_0 e^{kt}$, where $A_0$ is the starting amount, $k \gt 0$ for growth and $k \lt 0$ for decay. Questions ask you to use the model, or to find an unknown in it, which always needs logs.

After the broadside: a cooling gun barrel

After firing, the barrel's temperature is modelled by $T = 20 + 180e^{-kt}$ °C, $t$ minutes later. Set the cooling rate $k$ and the safe temperature for reloading. The widget solves for $t$ with logs, showing every line.

Worked example 20 · P2 · how long until it is safe?

The barrel temperature is $T = 20 + 180e^{-0.05t}$ °C. (a) What is the temperature just after firing? (b) How long does it take to cool to 50 °C?

Show solution
  1. (a) $t = 0$: $T = 20 + 180e^0 = 200$ °C $e^0 = 1$.
  2. (b) $20 + 180e^{-0.05t} = 50 \Rightarrow e^{-0.05t} = \dfrac{30}{180} = \dfrac16$ Isolate the exponential first.
  3. $-0.05t = \ln\dfrac16 \Rightarrow t = \dfrac{\ln 6}{0.05} = 35.8$ minutes Take natural logs. $\ln\frac16 = -\ln 6$.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

In 2026's question the model was $800e^{-0.124t} + 25$. "A few candidates gave 800 as the answer to part (a)": the starting value is $800e^0 + 25 = 825$, so do not drop the $+25$. Many "made errors when converting the equation from exponential form to logarithmic form". Isolate the exponential term before you take logs: $\ln(20 + 180e^{-0.05t})$ does not simplify.

Reading and sketching related graphs

Exam questions often show a transformed log or exponential graph and ask for its equation, or give the equation and ask for the graph of the inverse. Use the two facts you know: the fixed points ($a^0 = 1$, $\log_a 1 = 0$, $\log_a a = 1$) and the asymptote. Then use the transformation rules from chapter 5.

Worked example 21 · P1 · identify a log function from its graph

The graph of $y = \log_b(x - a)$ has the line $x = 2$ as an asymptote and passes through $(7, 1)$. Find $a$ and $b$.

Show solution
  1. $a = 2$ $y = \log_b x$ has asymptote $x = 0$. Replacing $x$ by $x - a$ moves the graph $a$ to the right, so the asymptote is $x = a$.
  2. $1 = \log_b(7 - 2) = \log_b 5$ Substitute the point $(7, 1)$.
  3. $b^1 = 5$, so $b = 5$ Convert to exponential form: $\log_b 5 = 1$ means $b^1 = 5$.

Check: the graph must also pass through $(3, 0)$, since $\log_5 1 = 0$. It does.

Worked example 22 · P1 · sketch the inverse

Sketch $y = 3^x - 2$ and, on the same axes, the graph of its inverse. Mark the asymptotes and two points on each graph.

Show solution
  1. $y = 3^x$ passes through $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$ with asymptote $y = 0$. Moving it down 2 gives $(0, -1)$, $(1, 1)$ and asymptote $y = -2$. Transform the known points of the basic graph, $y = f(x) - 2$.
  2. The inverse is the reflection in $y = x$: swap every coordinate. It passes through $(-1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, with asymptote $x = -2$. Reflecting swaps $x$ and $y$, for the asymptote too.
  3. Its equation, if asked: $x = 3^y - 2 \Rightarrow y = \log_3(x + 2)$. Swap $x$ and $y$, then make $y$ the subject.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • identify a transformed exponential or log function from its graph, and sketch the inverse of one;
  • switch between $y = \log_a x$ and $x = a^y$;
  • use the three laws of logs to simplify and to solve equations, rejecting impossible solutions;
  • solve $a^x = b$ and $e^{kx} = b$ by taking logs;
  • use and find unknowns in exponential models;
  • sketch $y = a^x$ and $y = \log_a x$ as inverses of each other.

7 · Straight lines from curves: log graphs ALG · log graphs

The Sailing Master measures something, plots it, and gets a curve. Is it $y = ab^x$ (exponential) or $y = ax^b$ (a power)? And what are $a$ and $b$? Taking logs turns the right kind of curve into a straight line, and a straight line is easy to read.

Exponential: $y = ab^x$

Take logs: $\log y = \log a + \log b^x$, so

$$\log y = (\log b)\,x + \log a.$$

Plot $\log y$ against $x$. You get a straight line with gradient $\log b$ and intercept $\log a$.

Power: $y = ax^b$

Take logs: $\log y = \log a + \log x^b$, so

$$\log y = b\log x + \log a.$$

Plot $\log y$ against $\log x$. You get a straight line with gradient $b$ and intercept $\log a$.

Compare each with $Y = mX + c$. The base of the logs can be anything (10, $e$, 2…) as long as you use the same base throughout.

The Sailing Master's experiment: straightening the data

Pick a data set, then choose a pair of log axes. The points slide to their new positions. Only the right choice of axes lines them up, and then the line's gradient and intercept give $a$ and $b$.

Worked example 23 · P1 · a power law

The graph of $\log_2 y$ against $\log_2 x$ is a straight line through $(0,\ 3)$ and $(4,\ 11)$. Find $a$ and $b$ such that $y = ax^b$.

Show solution
  1. Gradient $= \dfrac{11 - 3}{4 - 0} = 2$, intercept $= 3$, so $\log_2 y = 2\log_2 x + 3$ Find the line's equation.
  2. $\log_2 y = \log_2 x^2 + \log_2 2^3 = \log_2(8x^2)$ Write $3$ as $\log_2 8$ and combine.
  3. $y = 8x^2$, so $a = 8$ and $b = 2$ Remove the logs: both sides are $\log_2$ of something.

Worked example 24 · P1 · an exponential

The graph of $\log_3 y$ against $x$ is a straight line through $(0,\ 2)$ with gradient 1. Express $y$ in the form $y = ab^x$.

Show solution
  1. $\log_3 y = x + 2$ The line's equation.
  2. $y = 3^{x+2} = 3^2 \times 3^x = 9 \times 3^x$ Exponential form, then split the power.
  3. $a = 9$, $b = 3$ Compare with $ab^x$.

Alternatively compare with $\log_3 y = x\log_3 b + \log_3 a$: $\log_3 b = 1$ gives $b = 3$, and $\log_3 a = 2$ gives $a = 9$.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

The 2026 report says: "We intended this question to be one of the more demanding questions in the paper." And "a few candidates incorrectly used the relationship $y = ax^b$ instead of the relationship stated in the question, $y = ab^x$. These candidates did not gain any marks." Underline the relationship in the question, and check which axes are logged.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • take logs of $y = ab^x$ and $y = ax^b$ to get straight-line forms;
  • find $a$ and $b$ from a straight-line graph or from two points;
  • decide which model data fit by seeing which log graph is straight.

8 · Recurrence relations ALG-GEO · sequences

HMS Lodestone's freshwater tank holds 100 litres at the start of a voyage. Each day the crew uses 15% of what is in it, and then the distilling still adds 40 litres. How much is there after a week? After a year?

A recurrence relation gives each term of a sequence from the one before. Here, if $u_n$ is the volume after $n$ days,

$$u_{n+1} = 0.85u_n + 40, \qquad u_0 = 100.$$

Keeping 85% is multiplying by $0.85$. At Higher every recurrence relation is linear: $u_{n+1} = au_n + b$.

The water tank: watching a sequence settle

Set $a$, $b$ and the starting value. The cobweb diagram bounces between the line $y = ax + b$ and the line $y = x$: each bounce is one day. On the right, the terms are plotted against $n$. Try $a$ outside $-1 \lt a \lt 1$ and see what goes wrong.

The limit

If $-1 \lt a \lt 1$, the terms get closer and closer to a fixed value $L$ called the limit. At the limit, applying the rule changes nothing, so $L = aL + b$. Rearranging gives

$$L = \frac{b}{1 - a}.$$

For the water tank, $L = \dfrac{40}{1 - 0.85} = 266.\dot6$ litres. In the long run, the tank holds about 267 litres, whatever it started with.

If $a \ge 1$ or $a \le -1$ the sequence does not, in general, converge to a limit. For $a \gt 1$ or $a \lt -1$ the terms run away from $\frac{b}{1-a}$ (growing without end, or swinging ever more wildly); for $a = -1$ they flip between two values for ever; for $a = 1$ they go up or down by $b$ each time. So $L = \frac{b}{1-a}$ is not a limit there, even when it gives a number. (Only the start $u_0 = \frac{b}{1-a}$ stays put, and then every term is the same.)

Worked example 25 · P1 · state why a limit exists

A sequence is defined by $u_{n+1} = 0.6u_n + 12$. Explain why it has a limit, and find it.

Show solution
  1. A limit exists because $-1 \lt 0.6 \lt 1$. State the condition with the actual value of $a$.
  2. $L = 0.6L + 12$ or $L = \dfrac{12}{1 - 0.6}$ Set up the limit.
  3. $L = \dfrac{12}{0.4} = 30$ Evaluate.

Worked example 26 · P1 · find the rule from three terms

A sequence satisfies $u_{n+1} = au_n + b$, with $u_1 = 4$, $u_2 = 10$ and $u_3 = 22$. Find $a$ and $b$.

Show solution
  1. $10 = 4a + b$ and $22 = 10a + b$ Write the rule for $u_2$ and for $u_3$.
  2. Subtract: $12 = 6a \Rightarrow a = 2$ Solve simultaneously.
  3. $b = 10 - 8 = 2$ Substitute back.

Worked example 27 · P2 · when does it pass a target?

The tank model is $u_{n+1} = 0.85u_n + 40$, $u_0 = 100$. After how many days does the tank first hold more than 250 litres?

Show solution

Use your calculator's "Ans" key: type 100, then repeatedly enter 0.85 × Ans + 40, counting the presses. Write the terms down so the marker can see your method.

  1. $u_1 = 125$, $u_2 = 146.25$, $u_3 = 164.31$, … , $u_{14} = 249.54$, $u_{15} = 252.11$ Generate terms until you pass 250.
  2. The tank first holds more than 250 litres after 15 days. State the value of $n$ in context.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

"Many candidates did not clearly communicate why a limit did not exist … and only some candidates stated the final value of $n$." Use the exact words: "a limit exists (or does not exist) because $a = \dots$, which is (or is not) between $-1$ and $1$." And finish a "how many" question with a sentence that gives the number.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • write a recurrence relation from a description (percentage kept, amount added);
  • calculate terms, and find $n$ for a target;
  • state the condition for a limit, find the limit and interpret it in context;
  • find $a$ and $b$ from given terms.
