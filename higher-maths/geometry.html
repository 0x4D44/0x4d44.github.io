Lines, circles & vectors

Sailing Master Fraser's chart is a coordinate grid. Every course is a straight line on it, the range of a lighthouse is a circle, and the rigging of a ship is a web of vectors in three dimensions. This deck covers the geometric skills in the course specification: about 15–35% of the marks.

1 · The straight line ALG-GEO · rectilinear shapes

A rectilinear shape is one made only of straight lines: triangles, quadrilaterals and other polygons drawn on a coordinate grid. You met these skills at National 5. Higher uses them constantly, so make sure they are automatic. For points $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$:

$$m_{AB} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

Gradient. A vertical line has no gradient (undefined); a horizontal line has $m = 0$.

$$y - b = m(x - a)$$

The line through $(a, b)$ with gradient $m$. The most useful form at Higher.

$$\left(\tfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \tfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$

Midpoint of $AB$.

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$

Distance, by Pythagoras.

Gradient and angle: $m = \tan\theta$

The angle $\theta$ that a line makes with the positive direction of the $x$-axis (measured anticlockwise) is linked to its gradient by

$$m = \tan\theta.$$

The gradient is "up over along", and so is the tangent of the angle in the right-angled triangle under the line. For a positive gradient, $\theta = \tan^{-1} m$ is an acute angle. For a negative gradient the line slopes down and $\theta$ is obtuse: your calculator gives a negative angle, so add $180°$.

The Sailing Master's ruler: gradient, angle and equation

Drag $A$ and $B$ (or use the sliders). The widget gives the gradient, the angle with the positive $x$-axis, and the equation of the line. Switch on the perpendicular bisector or the perpendicular through $A$.

Worked example 1 · P1 · from the specimen paper

Line $l_1$ has equation $\sqrt3\,y - x = 0$. (a) Line $l_2$ is perpendicular to $l_1$. Find the gradient of $l_2$. [2] (b) Calculate the angle $l_2$ makes with the positive direction of the $x$-axis. [2]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. (a) $\sqrt3\,y = x \Rightarrow y = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}x$, so $m_{l_1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ Rearrange to $y = mx + c$ to read off the gradient.
  2. $m_{l_2} = -\sqrt3$ Perpendicular: $m_1 \times m_2 = -1$, so flip the fraction and change the sign.
  3. (b) $\tan\theta = -\sqrt3$ Use $m = \tan\theta$.
  4. $\theta = 180° - 60° = 120°$ (or $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$ in radians: see Trigonometry) $\tan 60° = \sqrt3$ is an exact value. The gradient is negative, so the angle is obtuse.

The bullets •1 to •4 follow the official marking instructions: one mark per step. Paper 1 has no calculator, so angles like this always come from the exact values: $m = 1$ gives $45°$, $m = \sqrt3$ gives $60°$, $m = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ gives $30°$.

Parallel, perpendicular and collinear

  • Parallel lines have equal gradients: $m_1 = m_2$.
  • Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to $-1$: $m_1 \times m_2 = -1$. So the perpendicular gradient is the negative reciprocal: $\tfrac23 \to -\tfrac32$.
  • Points $A$, $B$, $C$ are collinear (on one straight line) if $m_{AB} = m_{BC}$ and $B$ is a point common to both.

Worked example 2 · P1 · median meets altitude

Triangle $ABC$ has vertices $A(3, -4)$, $B(1, -8)$ and $C(9, -2)$. (a) Find the equation of the median from $A$. [3] (b) Find the equation of the altitude from $B$. [3] (c) Find the point where they meet. [2]

Show solution [8 marks]
  1. (a) Midpoint of $BC$: $M = \left(\tfrac{1+9}{2}, \tfrac{-8-2}{2}\right) = (5, -5)$ Midpoint: a median goes to a midpoint.
  2. $m_{AM} = \dfrac{-5 - (-4)}{5 - 3} = -\tfrac12$ Gradient of the median.
  3. $y + 4 = -\tfrac12(x - 3) \Rightarrow x + 2y + 5 = 0$ Equation through $A$.
  4. (b) $m_{AC} = \dfrac{-2 - (-4)}{9 - 3} = \tfrac13$ Gradient of $AC$: the altitude from $B$ is perpendicular to $AC$.
  5. $m_\perp = -3$ Perpendicular gradient: the negative reciprocal.
  6. $y + 8 = -3(x - 1) \Rightarrow y = -3x - 5$ Equation through $B$.
  7. (c) $x + 2(-3x - 5) + 5 = 0 \Rightarrow -5x - 5 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -1$ Strategy: solve simultaneously by substituting $y$, then find $x$.
  8. $y = -3(-1) - 5 = -2$. They meet at $(-1, -2)$. Find $y$ and state the point.

Worked example 3 · P2 · a perpendicular bisector and the angle between two lines

$P$ is $(-2, 1)$ and $Q$ is $(4, 3)$. (a) Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of $PQ$. [4] (b) Find the acute angle between the perpendicular bisector and the line $y = x$. [3]

Show solution [7 marks]
  1. (a) Midpoint of $PQ$: $(1, 2)$ Midpoint: a perpendicular bisector passes through it.
  2. $m_{PQ} = \dfrac{3 - 1}{4 - (-2)} = \tfrac13$ Gradient of $PQ$.
  3. $m_\perp = -3$ Perpendicular gradient: the negative reciprocal.
  4. $y - 2 = -3(x - 1) \Rightarrow y = -3x + 5$ Equation from the point and the gradient.
  5. (b) For $m = -3$: $\tan^{-1}(-3) = -71.6°$, so $\theta_1 = 180° - 71.6° = 108.4°$ Strategy: use $m = \tan\theta$. A negative gradient gives an obtuse angle with the positive $x$-axis.
  6. For $y = x$: $m = 1$, so $\theta_2 = 45°$ The second line's angle: $\tan 45° = 1$.
  7. Angle between the lines $= 108.4° - 45° = 63.4°$ Answer: subtract the two angles. It is under $90°$, so it is the acute angle. If the difference were over $90°$, the acute angle would be $180°$ minus it.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

In 2026 most candidates found the midpoint and gradient for a perpendicular bisector, but "only some candidates used $m = \tan\theta$ to calculate the angles between the two lines and the positive direction of the $x$-axis appropriately". To find the angle between two lines, find each line's angle with the positive $x$-axis and subtract.

  • find the equation of a line parallel or perpendicular to a given line;
  • use $m = \tan\theta$ in both directions, including obtuse angles;
  • find medians, altitudes and perpendicular bisectors, and where lines intersect;
  • decide whether two lines are perpendicular, or three points are collinear.

2 · The circle ALG-GEO · circles

A circle is every point at a fixed distance $r$ (the radius) from a centre $(a, b)$. By Pythagoras, a point $(x, y)$ is on it exactly when $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$. Both forms below are on the formulae list.

Centre–radius form

$$(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$$

Centre $(a, b)$, radius $r$. Watch the signs: $(x + 3)^2$ means $a = -3$.

General form

$$x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$$

Centre $(-g, -f)$, radius $\sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}$. It is a real circle only if $g^2 + f^2 - c \gt 0$.

The general form is just the centre–radius form multiplied out. To use it, halve the coefficients of $x$ and $y$ and change their signs to get the centre. The coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ must both be 1; if they are both 2, divide everything by 2 first.

Worked example 4 · P1 · general form

Find the centre and radius of the circle $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0$.

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. $2g = -6 \Rightarrow g = -3$; $2f = 4 \Rightarrow f = 2$; $c = -12$. Centre $(-g, -f) = (3, -2)$ State the centre: match with the general form, then change the signs.
  2. $r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 2^2 - (-12)} = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 12} = 5$ State the radius. Brackets round negatives; $-c$ becomes $+12$.

Worked example 5 · P1 · from a diameter (specimen paper)

$A(-7, 3)$ and $B(1, 5)$ are the ends of a diameter of a circle. Find its equation.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. Centre $= $ midpoint of $AB = (-3,\ 4)$ The centre is halfway along a diameter.
  2. $r = \sqrt{(1 - (-3))^2 + (5 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{17}$ Distance from the centre to $B$. The radius earns its own mark.
  3. $(x + 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 17$ $r^2 = 17$: leave it exact.

The lighthouse's range: a circle and a line

Set $g$, $f$ and $c$ to define the circle (it vanishes if $g^2 + f^2 - c \le 0$). Then move the line $y = mx + k$. Substituting the line into the circle gives a quadratic, and its discriminant tells you whether the line cuts, touches or misses.

Worked example 6 · P1 · where a line meets a circle

Find where the line $y = x - 2$ meets the circle $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y - 20 = 0$.

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. $x^2 + (x - 2)^2 - 4x + 2(x - 2) - 20 = 0$ Strategy: substitute the line into the circle.
  2. $2x^2 - 6x - 20 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 - 3x - 10 = 0$ Express in standard quadratic form: expand, collect and divide by 2.
  3. $(x - 5)(x + 2) = 0$ Factorise.
  4. $x = 5$ or $x = -2$ Find both $x$-coordinates. Two distinct roots: the line cuts the circle twice.
  5. $y = 3$ or $y = -4$: the points are $(5, 3)$ and $(-2, -4)$. The corresponding $y$-coordinates. Use the line to find each $y$; it is simpler than the circle.

Tangents to a circle

A tangent touches the circle at one point, and it is always perpendicular to the radius at that point. That gives two ways in:

  • Tangent at a known point $P$: find the gradient of the radius from the centre to $P$, take the negative reciprocal, and use $y - b = m(x - a)$ through $P$.
  • Is a given line a tangent? Substitute the line into the circle. If the resulting quadratic has discriminant zero (equal roots), the line is a tangent.

Worked example 7 · P1 · tangent at a point

The point $P(6, 2)$ lies on the circle $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$. Find the equation of the tangent at $P$.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Centre $(3, -2)$ State the centre: read off from the equation.
  2. $m_{\text{radius}} = \dfrac{2 - (-2)}{6 - 3} = \tfrac43$ Gradient of the radius, from the centre to $P$.
  3. $m_{\text{tangent}} = -\tfrac34$ Gradient of the tangent: perpendicular to the radius.
  4. $y - 2 = -\tfrac34(x - 6) \Rightarrow 3x + 4y - 26 = 0$ Equation through $P$.

Worked example 8 · P1 · prove tangency

Show that the line $y = 2x + 10$ is a tangent to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 20$, and find the point of contact.

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. $x^2 + (2x + 10)^2 = 20$ Strategy: substitute the line into the circle.
  2. $5x^2 + 40x + 80 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 + 8x + 16 = 0$ Express in standard quadratic form: expand, collect, and divide by 5.
  3. $b^2 - 4ac = 8^2 - 4(1)(16) = 64 - 64 = 0$ Use the discriminant (or factorise to show equal roots).
  4. Equal roots, so the line is a tangent. Communication: discriminant zero means one point of contact. Say so.
  5. $(x + 4)^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -4$, $y = 2(-4) + 10 = 2$. Point of contact $(-4, 2)$. Solve and find $y$ from the line.

Two circles

Compare the distance $d$ between the centres with the radii $r_1$ and $r_2$. (Here $|r_1 - r_2|$ means the difference between the radii with any minus sign dropped: $|3 - 5| = 2$.)

  • $d = r_1 + r_2$: the circles touch externally;
  • $d = |r_1 - r_2|$: they touch internally (one inside the other);
  • $|r_1 - r_2| \lt d \lt r_1 + r_2$: they intersect at two points;
  • $d \gt r_1 + r_2$: they are separate; $d \lt |r_1 - r_2|$: one is inside the other without touching.

Two lighthouses: when do the beams' ranges touch?

Drag the second centre and change the radii. The widget compares the distance between the centres with $r_1 + r_2$ and $|r_1 - r_2|$.

Worked example 9 · P1 · touching circles

(a) Show that the circles $x^2 + y^2 = 16$ and $(x - 6)^2 + (y - 8)^2 = 36$ touch externally. [4] (b) Find the point of contact. [2]

Show solution [6 marks]
  1. (a) First circle: centre $(0, 0)$, radius $4$ Centre and radius of the first circle.
  2. Second circle: centre $(6, 8)$, radius $6$ Centre and radius of the second circle.
  3. $d = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$ Distance between the centres.
  4. $r_1 + r_2 = 4 + 6 = 10 = d$, so the circles touch externally. Communication: compare with the sum of the radii and state the conclusion.
  5. (b) The point of contact lies on the line of centres, $4$ units from $(0, 0)$ and $6$ from $(6, 8)$: it divides the line of centres in the ratio $4 : 6 = 2 : 3$. Strategy: the radii give the ratio.
  6. $\tfrac25\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix} = \left(\tfrac{12}{5}, \tfrac{16}{5}\right)$, i.e. $(2.4,\ 3.2)$ Coordinates: start at the first centre and go $\tfrac25$ of the way.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"How do I know if a point is inside a circle?"

Sailing Master Fraser: "Put its coordinates into the left-hand side of $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2$. That's the squared distance from the centre. If it's less than $r^2$ the point is inside; equal, on the circle; more, outside. The same works with the general form: substitute and see whether you get less than, equal to, or more than zero."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • switch between the two forms and find the centre and radius;
  • state when $x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$ is a circle;
  • find the equation of a tangent at a point, and prove a line is a tangent;
  • find where a line meets a circle;
  • decide whether two circles touch, intersect or do neither, and find a point of contact.

3 · Vectors in three dimensions GEO · vector connections

A vector has size (magnitude) and direction. A Minecraft world is the perfect place to learn them, because it is built on a three-dimensional grid of unit cubes. Moving 3 blocks east, 2 blocks north and 5 blocks up is the vector

$$\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} = 3\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 5\mathbf{k},$$

where $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ are unit vectors (length 1) along the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-axes. Both notations are used in exams.

⛏ Minecraft's axes are not the exam's

In Minecraft's own coordinates (press F3) the y-axis points up and z runs north–south. In these notes, as in most exam diagrams, $z$ is the up axis. Every exam diagram labels its axes: use those, not the game's.

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$$

The vector from $A$ to $B$ is "end minus start". $\mathbf{a}$ is the position vector of $A$: the vector from the origin to $A$.

$$|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$

Magnitude, by 3D Pythagoras.

$$\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{1}{|\mathbf{v}|}\mathbf{v}$$

A unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$: divide by its length.

$$t\mathbf{v}$$

Multiplying by a number $t$ scales the length. If $\mathbf{u} = t\mathbf{v}$, then $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are parallel.

Worked example 10 · P1 · a unit vector

Find a unit vector parallel to $\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$.

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt9 = 3$ Find the magnitude first.
  2. $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \tfrac13(2\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}) = \tfrac23\mathbf{i} - \tfrac13\mathbf{j} + \tfrac23\mathbf{k}$ State the unit vector: divide every component by the length.

Check: $\left(\tfrac23\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac13\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac23\right)^2 = \tfrac{4 + 1 + 4}{9} = 1$. $-\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is also a unit vector parallel to $\mathbf{v}$, pointing the other way.

Vector pathways

To get from one point to another, you can follow any route through known vectors and add them. This is how questions on cuboids and pyramids work. Going backwards along a vector changes its sign: $\overrightarrow{BA} = -\overrightarrow{AB}$.

A block-built cuboid: vector pathways in 3D

Drag the cuboid (or use the slider) to turn it. Choose a target point: the widget walks the path along the edges, adding one vector at a time. $O$ is the origin; $\overrightarrow{OA} = 6\mathbf{i}$, $\overrightarrow{OC} = 4\mathbf{j}$ and $\overrightarrow{OD} = 3\mathbf{k}$.

Worked example 11 · P1 · a pathway

In the cuboid above, $M$ is the midpoint of $FG$. Express $\overrightarrow{AM}$ in component form.

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. $\overrightarrow{AM} = \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BF} + \overrightarrow{FM}$ State a pathway: choose a route along known edges.
  2. $\overrightarrow{AB} = 4\mathbf{j}$, $\overrightarrow{BF} = 3\mathbf{k}$, $\overrightarrow{FM} = \tfrac12\overrightarrow{FG} = -3\mathbf{i}$, so $\overrightarrow{AM} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ Substitute and add the components. $\overrightarrow{FG}$ points back along the $x$-axis: $-6\mathbf{i}$.

Check with coordinates: $A(6, 0, 0)$ and $M(3, 4, 3)$, and $\mathbf{m} - \mathbf{a} = (-3, 4, 3)$.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2025 and 2026 course reports

Vector pathways: in 2026, many candidates "stated a vector pathway as a sum of directed line segments", and many gained full marks. Write the route first, as $\overrightarrow{PQ} = \overrightarrow{PR} + \overrightarrow{RQ}$, then substitute. The 2025 and 2026 reports both ask teachers to "consider how best to practise determining vector pathways in three dimensions". In 2025, "many candidates did not gain any marks" on the pathway question: "some candidates stated incorrect pathways, and some candidates did not add vectors presented in terms of i, j and k". To add vectors in $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ form, collect each letter separately, or convert to columns first.

Collinearity with vectors

Three points $A$, $B$, $C$ are collinear if $\overrightarrow{AB} = k\overrightarrow{BC}$ for some number $k$ (so the vectors are parallel) and the two vectors share a point ($B$). Both parts belong in your answer.

Worked example 12 · P1 · collinear points

$A$ is $(1, 2, -1)$, $B$ is $(3, 5, 1)$ and $C$ is $(7, 11, 5)$. (a) Show that $A$, $B$ and $C$ are collinear. [3] (b) Find the ratio in which $B$ divides $AC$. [1]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. (a) $\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ An appropriate vector: end minus start.
  2. $\overrightarrow{BC} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\overrightarrow{BC} = 2\overrightarrow{AB}$ A second vector, and its relationship to the first.
  3. $AB$ and $BC$ are parallel, $B$ is a common point, so $A$, $B$ and $C$ are collinear. Communication: this mark needs all three statements.
  4. (b) $AB : BC = 1 : 2$ State the ratio: $\overrightarrow{BC}$ is twice as long as $\overrightarrow{AB}$.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

"Only a few candidates communicated the condition for collinearity in a coherent way. Some incorrect responses implied that the points were parallel, the lines were collinear, or did not identify that the line segments were parallel. … A few candidates attempted to find a 'gradient of a vector'." Points are collinear; vectors (or line segments) are parallel. Use those words exactly.

The same report: "A few candidates made statements such as $\overrightarrow{DE} = (2, -4, 6) \div 2 = (1, -2, 3)$, which did not gain full marks." A vector cannot equal a different vector. Write $\overrightarrow{DE} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-4\\6\end{pmatrix} = 2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\3\end{pmatrix}$, keeping the scalar multiple (the number multiplying the vector) outside, rather than an "equals" chain that changes the vector.

Dividing a line in a ratio

If $P$ divides $AB$ internally in the ratio $m : n$, then $P$ is $\frac{m}{m+n}$ of the way from $A$ to $B$:

$$\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{a} + \frac{m}{m+n}\overrightarrow{AB} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf{p} = \frac{n\mathbf{a} + m\mathbf{b}}{m + n}.$$

Rigging a stay: dividing a line in a ratio

Set the ratio $m : n$. The point $P$ slides along $AB$, and its coordinates are worked out both ways.

Worked example 13 · P1 · internal division

$A$ is $(2, -1, 4)$ and $B$ is $(7, 9, -1)$. $P$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $3 : 2$. Find the coordinates of $P$.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix}$ Find $\overrightarrow{AB}$: end minus start.
  2. $P$ is $\tfrac35$ of the way along, so $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{a} + \tfrac35\overrightarrow{AB}$ Strategy: $m + n = 5$ parts. Start at $A$ and add the fraction of the journey.
  3. $\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac35\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 5 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $P(5, 5, 1)$ Process and state the coordinates. Give coordinates, not a position vector, if the question asks for coordinates.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

In 2026 the question asked for the point $F$ that divided a line in a given ratio, and "a few candidates left their answer as a position vector or did not use point F as the internal division point in their calculation". Read which point is doing the dividing, and answer in the form asked.

4 · The scalar product GEO · working with vectors

The scalar product (or dot product) of two vectors is a single number. It has two formulas, both on the formulae list:

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta \qquad\qquad \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors. Putting them together gives the angle between any two vectors:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|}.$$

The most important consequence: two non-zero vectors are perpendicular if and only if $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0$, because $\cos 90° = 0$.

Bracing the yard: the scalar product and the angle

Set the components of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, and drag the view to turn it. The widget works out $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ both ways and the angle between the vectors. Try to make them perpendicular.

Worked example 14 · P2 · the angle between two vectors

$\mathbf{a} = 2\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{b} = 3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}$. Find the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 2(3) + (-1)(4) + 2(0) = 2$ The scalar product, in component form. $\mathbf{b}$ has no $\mathbf{k}$ part, so $b_3 = 0$.
  2. $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$ Magnitude of $\mathbf{a}$: one mark.
  3. $|\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 0} = 5$ Magnitude of $\mathbf{b}$: a separate mark.
  4. $\cos\theta = \dfrac{2}{3 \times 5} = \dfrac{2}{15}$ The formula, with your values in.
  5. $\theta = 82.3°$ Inverse cosine, calculator in degree mode.

Worked example 15 · P1 · perpendicular (specimen paper)

The points $A(0, 9, 7)$, $B(5, -1, 2)$, $C(4, 1, 3)$ and $D(x, -2, 2)$ are such that $AB$ is perpendicular to $CD$. Find $x$.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. $\overrightarrow{CD} = \begin{pmatrix} x - 4 \\ -3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ End minus start.
  2. $\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ -10 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix}$ Each vector earns a mark.
  3. $\overrightarrow{AB}\cdot\overrightarrow{CD} = 5(x - 4) + (-10)(-3) + (-5)(-1) = 0$ Perpendicular vectors have scalar product zero. Write the condition.
  4. $5x - 20 + 30 + 5 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -3$ Solve.

The angle at a vertex

To find angle $ABC$ in a 3D shape, use the vectors $\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\overrightarrow{BC}$: both must start at the vertex $B$. Using $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{BC}$ (one going in, one going out) gives the angle outside the corner, $180° - \theta$.

Worked example 16 · P2 · the angle at a vertex

$A$ is $(1, 3, 2)$, $B$ is $(2, 1, 0)$ and $C$ is $(4, 2, 1)$. Find the size of angle $ABC$.

Show solution [7 marks]
  1. $\overrightarrow{BA} = \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}$ Start at the vertex $B$.
  2. $\overrightarrow{BC} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}$ Also starting at $B$.
  3. $\overrightarrow{BA}\cdot\overrightarrow{BC} = -2 + 2 + 2 = 2$ The scalar product.
  4. $|\overrightarrow{BA}| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$ Each magnitude is a separate mark.
  5. $|\overrightarrow{BC}| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt6$
  6. $\cos ABC = \dfrac{2}{3\sqrt6}$ The formula, with your values in.
  7. $ABC = 74.2°$ Calculator in degree mode.

Properties you can use

  • $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a}$, and $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}$ (you can expand brackets).
  • $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^2$, since the angle between a vector and itself is $0°$.

Worked example 17 · P1 · using the properties

$|\mathbf{a}| = 3$, $|\mathbf{b}| = 4$ and the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is $60°$. Evaluate $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b})$.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ Expand the brackets.
  2. $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^2 = 9$ Evaluate $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}$ using $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^2$.
  3. $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos 60° = 3 \times 4 \times \tfrac12 = 6$, so $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = 9 + 6 = 15$ Evaluate $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ with the exact value $\cos 60° = \tfrac12$, and complete.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

The scalar product is a number, not a vector. Writing $\begin{pmatrix}3\\2\\6\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-5\\-2\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-15\\-4\\24\end{pmatrix} = 5$ "did not gain a mark". Write $3(-5) + 2(-2) + 6(4) = 5$.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • evaluate a scalar product from components, or from lengths and an angle;
  • find the angle between two vectors, taking both vectors from the vertex;
  • use $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0$ for perpendicular vectors;
  • expand $\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c})$ and use $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} = |\mathbf{a}|^2$.
