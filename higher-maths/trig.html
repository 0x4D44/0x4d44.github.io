The quarterdeck · waves, angles and the swell

Trigonometry

On the quarterdeck, the officer of the watch feels the ship rise and fall on the swell, measures the Sun's angle with a sextant and times the tide. All three are trigonometry. This deck covers radians and exact values, trig graphs, solving trig equations, the addition and double angle formulae, and the wave function: about 10–25% of the marks, and the trig skills you need again in calculus.

1 · Radians, exact values and the four quadrants ALG · trigonometric expressions

Radians

Degrees are a human choice: 360 in a full turn. Mathematicians prefer a natural unit. One radian is the angle at the centre of a circle when the arc it cuts off is exactly one radius long. A full circle has circumference $2\pi r$, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians:

$$2\pi \text{ radians} = 360°, \qquad \pi \text{ radians} = 180°.$$

To convert degrees to radians multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$; to convert back multiply by $\frac{180}{\pi}$. The ones you will meet constantly are $\frac{\pi}{6} = 30°$, $\frac{\pi}{4} = 45°$, $\frac{\pi}{3} = 60°$, $\frac{\pi}{2} = 90°$, $\pi = 180°$, $\frac{3\pi}{2} = 270°$ and $2\pi = 360°$. Calculus at Higher only works in radians, so get used to them now.

⚠ Exam lookout · calculator mode

If a question is in radians (it mentions $\pi$, or the interval is $0 \le x \lt 2\pi$), switch your calculator to radian mode. If you leave it in degrees, every answer will be wrong. The 2026 course report asks teachers to "consider how best to practise using radian measure and the exact values of trigonometric ratios".

Exact values

Paper 1 has no calculator, so you need the exact values. Don't memorise a table: learn two triangles and read them off. Half a square with sides 1 gives the $45°$ values. Half an equilateral triangle with sides 2 gives the $30°$ and $60°$ values.

Angles in all four quadrants

Picture a point moving anticlockwise round a circle of radius 1, centred on the origin, starting from the positive $x$-axis. After turning through angle $\theta$ the point is at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. That is the real definition of sine and cosine, and it works for any angle, not just those in a right-angled triangle.

The capstan: sine and cosine from a turning circle

Drag the point round the circle, press play, or tap an exact angle. The height of the point is $\sin\theta$ and its across-distance is $\cos\theta$. As it turns, those two lengths trace out the sine and cosine graphs.

Because the point's coordinates change sign from quadrant to quadrant, so do the ratios. The memory aid is CAST, reading anticlockwise from the fourth quadrant: Cos positive, All positive, Sin positive, Tan positive.

Worked example 1 · P1 · exact values anywhere

Find the exact values of (a) $\cos 150°$, (b) $\sin\frac{4\pi}{3}$ and (c) $\tan\frac{7\pi}{4}$.

Show solution
  1. (a) $150° = 180° - 30°$, second quadrant, where cos is negative: $\cos 150° = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ Related acute angle, then the sign from CAST.
  2. (b) $\frac{4\pi}{3} = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3}$, third quadrant, where sin is negative: $\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ $\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
  3. (c) $\frac{7\pi}{4} = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{4}$, fourth quadrant, where tan is negative: $\tan\frac{7\pi}{4} = -1$ $\tan\frac{\pi}{4} = 1$.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • convert between degrees and radians;
  • give exact values of sin, cos and tan for multiples of $30°$ and $45°$ (or $\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\frac{\pi}{4}$) in every quadrant.

2 · Trig graphs ALG · related functions

The graphs of $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$ are waves that repeat every $360°$ ($2\pi$), between $-1$ and $1$. The graph of $y = \tan x$ repeats every $180°$ ($\pi$) and has asymptotes where $\cos x = 0$. The graph transformations from the algebra deck work on them exactly as on any other graph:

In $y = a\sin(bx + c) + d$What it changes
$a$Amplitude $|a|$: the height of each peak above the centre line. Negative $a$ flips the graph upside down.
$b$Number of complete waves in $360°$. The period is $\frac{360°}{b}$ (or $\frac{2\pi}{b}$).
$c$Shift sideways by $\frac{c}{b}$, because $bx + c = b\left(x + \frac{c}{b}\right)$. It is inside the bracket, so it works the opposite way: $+c$ moves the graph left.
$d$Moves the centre line up to $y = d$. The maximum is $d + |a|$ and the minimum is $d - |a|$.
⛏ The Overworld's sun is a sine wave

A Minecraft day lasts 20 minutes (24 000 game ticks), with the sun rising at tick 0 and highest at tick 6000. Its height above the horizon is modelled well by $h = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{24\,000}\right)$: period $24\,000$ ticks, amplitude 1, and below the horizon (night) whenever $h \lt 0$. The moon follows the same wave shifted by half a period, $h = -\sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{24\,000}\right)$.

Reading the swell: $y = a\sin(bx + c) + d$

Change the four numbers and watch the amplitude, period and centre line change. The readout gives the maximum and minimum values and where the first maximum occurs.

Worked example 2 · P1 · identify the equation from a graph

A graph has equation $y = a\cos bx + d$. It has a maximum of $5$ at $x = 0$, a minimum of $-1$ at $x = 60°$, and the pattern repeats every $120°$. Find $a$, $b$ and $d$.

Show solution
  1. $d = \dfrac{5 + (-1)}{2} = 2$ The centre line is halfway between maximum and minimum.
  2. $a = 5 - 2 = 3$ The amplitude is the distance from the centre line to a peak. A cosine graph with $a \gt 0$ starts at a maximum, as here.
  3. $b = \dfrac{360°}{120°} = 3$ Period $= \frac{360°}{b}$.

So $y = 3\cos 3x + 2$.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • sketch $y = a\sin(bx + c) + d$ and the cosine version, in degrees or radians;
  • find the amplitude, period, maximum and minimum from an equation, and the equation from a graph.

3 · Solving trig equations ALG · trigonometric equations

A trig equation usually has several solutions in a given interval, because the graph keeps repeating. The method is always the same:

  1. Rearrange to get $\sin(\ldots) = k$, $\cos(\ldots) = k$ or $\tan(\ldots) = k$.
  2. Find the acute angle from the exact values or with $\sin^{-1}|k|$ etc.
  3. Use CAST (the sign of $k$) to find every solution in one full turn.
  4. If the angle is $2x$, $3x$, …, extend the interval first: for $0 \le x \lt 360°$, $2x$ runs from $0$ to $720°$. Then divide by 2 at the end.
  5. Give the solutions in the interval and in the units asked.

Fixing a position: where the line meets the wave

Choose the function, the multiple angle $b$ and the value $k$. Every solution of the equation in the interval is a point where the horizontal line $y = k$ meets the graph. The widget lists them and explains where each comes from.

Worked example 3 · P1 · a multiple angle

Solve $2\cos 2x° = \sqrt3$, for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $\cos 2x° = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ Rearrange.
  2. Acute angle $30°$; cos is positive in the first and fourth quadrants, so $2x = 30,\ 330$ CAST.
  3. $0 \le 2x \lt 720$, so add $360$: $2x = 30,\ 330,\ 390,\ 690$ Extend to two full turns.
  4. $x = 15,\ 165,\ 195,\ 345$ Divide by 2.

Worked example 4 · P1 · in radians

Solve $2\cos x + 1 = 0$, for $0 \le x \lt 2\pi$.

Show solution
  1. $\cos x = -\tfrac12$ Rearrange.
  2. Acute angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$; cos is negative in the second and third quadrants. CAST.
  3. $x = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ and $x = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}$ Related angles in radians.

Quadratic trig equations

If the equation contains $\cos^2 x$ and $\cos x$, treat it as a quadratic in $\cos x$: factorise, then solve each factor separately. One factor may give an impossible value (sin and cos are always between $-1$ and $1$).

Worked example 5 · P1 · a quadratic in $\cos x$

Solve $2\cos^2 x° - \cos x° - 1 = 0$, for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $(2\cos x° + 1)(\cos x° - 1) = 0$ Factorise, as $2c^2 - c - 1 = (2c + 1)(c - 1)$.
  2. $\cos x° = -\tfrac12$ or $\cos x° = 1$ Set each factor to zero.
  3. $x = 120,\ 240$ or $x = 0$ Solve each.

So $x = 0,\ 120,\ 240$.

Worked example 6 · P1 · use $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$

Solve $2\sin^2 x° - \cos x° - 1 = 0$, for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $2(1 - \cos^2 x°) - \cos x° - 1 = 0$ Replace $\sin^2 x$ by $1 - \cos^2 x$, so that only $\cos x$ is left.
  2. $2\cos^2 x° + \cos x° - 1 = 0$ Tidy up and multiply through by $-1$.
  3. $(2\cos x° - 1)(\cos x° + 1) = 0$ Factorise.
  4. $\cos x° = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow x = 60,\ 300$; $\cos x° = -1 \Rightarrow x = 180$ Solve each factor. $\cos x = -1$ only at $180°$ in one turn.

So $x = 60,\ 180,\ 300$.

Worked example 7 · P1 · a shifted multiple angle in radians

Solve $2\sin\left(2x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \sqrt3$, for $0 \le x \lt \pi$.

Show solution
  1. $\sin\left(2x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ Rearrange.
  2. $0 \le x \lt \pi$, so $-\frac{\pi}{3} \le 2x - \frac{\pi}{3} \lt \frac{5\pi}{3}$ Find the interval for the whole angle: double, then subtract $\frac{\pi}{3}$.
  3. $2x - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3}$ or $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ Acute angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$; sin is positive in the first and second quadrants. Both lie in the interval, and no others do.
  4. $2x = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ or $\pi$, so $x = \frac{\pi}{3}$ or $\frac{\pi}{2}$ Add $\frac{\pi}{3}$, then halve.

Equations that need a formula first

If the equation mixes $\cos 2x$ with $\cos x$ (or $\sin 2x$ with $\sin x$), use a double angle formula so that everything is in terms of a single angle $x$. Choose the version of $\cos 2x$ that matches the other term: $2\cos^2 x - 1$ goes with $\cos x$; $1 - 2\sin^2 x$ goes with $\sin x$.

Worked example 8 · P1 · use $\cos 2x$

Solve $\cos 2x° - 3\cos x° + 2 = 0$, for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $2\cos^2 x° - 1 - 3\cos x° + 2 = 0$ Substitute $\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1$.
  2. $2\cos^2 x° - 3\cos x° + 1 = 0 \Rightarrow (2\cos x° - 1)(\cos x° - 1) = 0$ Standard quadratic form, then factorise.
  3. $\cos x° = \tfrac12$ or $\cos x° = 1$ Each factor.
  4. $x = 60,\ 300$ or $x = 0$, so $x = 0,\ 60,\ 300$ Solve.

Worked example 9 · P1 · never divide by $\sin x$

Solve $\sin 2x° = \sin x°$, for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $2\sin x°\cos x° - \sin x° = 0$ Use $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$, and bring everything to one side.
  2. $\sin x°(2\cos x° - 1) = 0$ Take out the common factor.
  3. $\sin x° = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0,\ 180$; $\cos x° = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow x = 60,\ 300$ Solve each factor.

Dividing both sides by $\sin x$ would lose $x = 0$ and $x = 180$.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

On a quadratic trig equation, "some candidates did not factorise the quadratic in $\cos x$. Incorrect responses included attempting to solve a quadratic that was not in standard form or incorrectly identifying a 'common factor' of $\cos x$. A few candidates did not communicate that $\cos x = -2$ had no solutions." Get the quadratic to "$= 0$" first, and when a factor gives a value outside $-1$ to $1$, write "no solutions" for it.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • solve trig equations in degrees or radians in a given interval, including multiple angles and shifted angles;
  • solve quadratic trig equations by factorising;
  • use a double angle formula or $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$ to get the equation in one ratio.

4 · Addition and double angle formulae ALG · trigonometric expressions

These four formulae are on the formulae list. You need to recognise when to use them, and use them fluently in both directions.

$$\sin(A \pm B) = \sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$

Sine: "sin cos, cos sin", same sign.

$$\cos(A \pm B) = \cos A\cos B \mp \sin A\sin B$$

Cosine: "cos cos, sin sin", opposite sign.

$$\sin 2A = 2\sin A\cos A$$

Put $B = A$ in $\sin(A + B)$.

$$\begin{aligned}\cos 2A &= \cos^2 A - \sin^2 A\\ &= 2\cos^2 A - 1\\ &= 1 - 2\sin^2 A\end{aligned}$$

Three versions, using $\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1$.

You also need two identities from National 5, which are not on the formulae list:

$$\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1, \qquad \tan A = \frac{\sin A}{\cos A}.$$
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Isn't $\sin(A + B)$ just $\sin A + \sin B$?"

Captain Grant: "Try it with $A = B = 90°$. $\sin 180° = 0$, but $\sin 90° + \sin 90° = 2$. Sine isn't a number you can multiply out of a bracket; it's a function. The addition formula is the true rule for what happens when you add angles. Use the widget below to see how far wrong the shortcut goes."

Two bearings combined: checking the addition formula

Set two angles $A$ and $B$. The diagram builds $A + B$ out of two right-angled triangles stacked on each other, so you can see where each term of $\sin(A + B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$ comes from. The bars compare the true value with the tempting wrong answer.

Worked example 10 · P1 · an exact value from the formula

Find the exact value of $\sin 75°$.

Show solution
  1. $\sin 75° = \sin(45° + 30°) = \sin 45°\cos 30° + \cos 45°\sin 30°$ Split into angles with known exact values.
  2. $= \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\dfrac12 = \dfrac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2\sqrt2}$ Substitute.
  3. $= \dfrac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4}$ Rationalise the denominator (multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt2$).

Worked example 11 · P1 · two right-angled triangles

Acute angles $p$ and $q$ satisfy $\sin p = \frac35$ and $\cos q = \frac{5}{13}$. Find the exact values of (a) $\sin(p + q)$ and (b) $\tan(p + q)$.

Show solution
  1. $\cos p = \frac45$ (a 3–4–5 triangle), $\sin q = \frac{12}{13}$ (a 5–12–13 triangle) Draw each right-angled triangle and use Pythagoras for the missing side.
  2. (a) $\sin(p + q) = \frac35\cdot\frac{5}{13} + \frac45\cdot\frac{12}{13} = \frac{15}{65} + \frac{48}{65} = \frac{63}{65}$ Addition formula.
  3. (b) $\cos(p + q) = \frac45\cdot\frac{5}{13} - \frac35\cdot\frac{12}{13} = \frac{20}{65} - \frac{36}{65} = -\frac{16}{65}$ You need $\cos(p+q)$ too.
  4. $\tan(p + q) = \dfrac{\sin(p+q)}{\cos(p+q)} = \dfrac{63/65}{-16/65} = -\dfrac{63}{16}$ Use $\tan = \frac{\sin}{\cos}$.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

On a question like this, "most candidates did not apply the trigonometric identity $\tan a = \frac{\sin a}{\cos a}$ from National 5", and "a few candidates did not process $\frac{56}{65} \div -\frac{33}{65}$ to find $\tan a$". Others "missed out on marks due to a lack of rigour in their supporting working, by making statements such as $169 - 25 = \sqrt{144} = 12$": $169 - 25$ is $144$, not $\sqrt{144}$. Write $\sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$, so that every line is true. Dividing fractions with the same denominator is quick: $\frac{56}{65} \div -\frac{33}{65} = -\frac{56}{33}$.

Worked example 12 · P1 · double angles

$A$ is acute and $\cos A = \frac35$. Find the exact values of $\sin 2A$ and $\cos 2A$.

Show solution
  1. $\sin A = \frac45$ 3–4–5 triangle.
  2. $\sin 2A = 2\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac35 = \frac{24}{25}$ Double angle formula.
  3. $\cos 2A = \left(\frac35\right)^2 - \left(\frac45\right)^2 = \frac{9 - 16}{25} = -\frac{7}{25}$ Any version works; $2\cos^2 A - 1 = \frac{18}{25} - 1$ gives the same.

Proving identities

To show that two expressions are identical, start with one side (usually the more complicated one) and use formulae until it becomes the other side. Never work on both sides at once, and never assume what you are trying to prove.

Worked example 13 · P1 · show that

Show that $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{1 + \cos 2x} = \tan x$.

Show solution
  1. $\text{LHS} = \dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{1 + (2\cos^2 x - 1)}$ Pick the version of $\cos 2x$ that cancels the 1.
  2. $= \dfrac{2\sin x\cos x}{2\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ Simplify by cancelling $2\cos x$.
  3. $= \tan x = \text{RHS}$ Finish with the target.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

On a demanding identity question, "many candidates did not expand the brackets accurately", simplifying $3\sin\theta \times 3\cos\theta$ to things like $3\sin\theta\cos\theta$ or $9\sin\cos\theta$. Multiply the numbers and keep every angle: $3\sin\theta \times 3\cos\theta = 9\sin\theta\cos\theta$, and $3\sin\theta \times \sin\theta = 3\sin^2\theta$.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • expand $\sin(A \pm B)$ and $\cos(A \pm B)$, and spot them in reverse;
  • find exact values using right-angled triangles and the formulae;
  • use $\sin 2A$ and all three forms of $\cos 2A$;
  • prove simple identities, setting out each line clearly.

5 · The wave function ALG · wave function

When two waves of the same period meet, they add up to another wave of that period. On HMS Lodestone, a long swell from the west meets another from the north, and the ship rides their sum. Mathematically, any expression $a\cos x + b\sin x$ can be rewritten as a single wave:

$$a\cos x + b\sin x = k\cos(x - \alpha), \qquad k \gt 0.$$

The single wave has amplitude $k$ and is shifted by $\alpha$. You can also be asked for $k\cos(x + \alpha)$, $k\sin(x - \alpha)$ or $k\sin(x + \alpha)$; the method is the same.

Two swells, one wave: $a\cos x + b\sin x = k\cos(x - \alpha)$

Set $a$ and $b$. On the left, a cos arrow of length $a$ and a sin arrow of length $b$ add to one arrow of length $k$ at angle $\alpha$; press play to turn them. On the right, the two component waves (faint) add up, point by point, to the single wave.

The method

  1. Expand the target form with the addition formula: $k\cos(x - \alpha) = k\cos x\cos\alpha + k\sin x\sin\alpha$.
  2. Compare coefficients with $a\cos x + b\sin x$: $\ k\cos\alpha = a$ and $k\sin\alpha = b$.
  3. Find $k$: $k = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.
  4. Find $\alpha$: $\tan\alpha = \frac{k\sin\alpha}{k\cos\alpha} = \frac{b}{a}$. Use the signs of $k\sin\alpha$ and $k\cos\alpha$ to pick the quadrant (CAST).

Worked example 14 · P2 · $\alpha$ in the fourth quadrant

Express $5\cos x° - 12\sin x°$ in the form $k\cos(x - \alpha)°$, where $k \gt 0$ and $0 \le \alpha \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. $k\cos x°\cos\alpha° + k\sin x°\sin\alpha°$ Expand the target.
  2. $k\cos\alpha° = 5$ and $k\sin\alpha° = -12$ Compare coefficients. Keep the minus sign with $\sin$.
  3. $k = \sqrt{5^2 + (-12)^2} = 13$ Find $k$.
  4. $\tan\alpha° = -\frac{12}{5}$; cos positive, sin negative, so $\alpha$ is in the fourth quadrant: $\alpha = 360 - 67.4 = 292.6$ $\tan^{-1}\frac{12}{5} = 67.4°$, then CAST.

So $5\cos x° - 12\sin x° = 13\cos(x - 292.6)°$.

Worked example 15 · P1 · the sine form, in radians

Express $\sin x - \sqrt3\cos x$ in the form $k\sin(x - \alpha)$, where $k \gt 0$ and $0 \le \alpha \lt 2\pi$.

Show solution
  1. $k\sin(x - \alpha) = k\sin x\cos\alpha - k\cos x\sin\alpha$ Expand with $\sin(A - B)$. Note the minus sign it brings.
  2. $k\cos\alpha = 1$ and $-k\sin\alpha = -\sqrt3$, so $k\sin\alpha = \sqrt3$ Compare the coefficients of $\sin x$ and of $\cos x$.
  3. $k = \sqrt{1^2 + (\sqrt3)^2} = 2$ Find $k$.
  4. $\tan\alpha = \sqrt3$; sin and cos both positive, so $\alpha = \frac{\pi}{3}$ First quadrant, exact value in radians.

So $\sin x - \sqrt3\cos x = 2\sin\left(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$. Check at $x = 0$: left side $-\sqrt3$, right side $2\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\sqrt3$.

Worked example 16 · P1 · solve using the wave function

(a) Express $\sqrt3\cos x° + \sin x°$ in the form $k\cos(x - \alpha)°$, $k \gt 0$, $0 \le \alpha \lt 360$. (b) Hence solve $\sqrt3\cos x° + \sin x° = 1$ for $0 \le x \lt 360$.

Show solution
  1. (a) $k\cos\alpha = \sqrt3$, $k\sin\alpha = 1$, $k = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2$ Compare and find $k$.
  2. $\tan\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}$, both positive, so $\alpha = 30$: the expression is $2\cos(x - 30)°$ First quadrant.
  3. (b) $2\cos(x - 30)° = 1 \Rightarrow \cos(x - 30)° = \frac12$ Use part (a).
  4. $x - 30 = 60$ or $300 \Rightarrow x = 90$ or $330$ Solve, then add 30. (Check $x - 30$ stays in range: $-30 \le x - 30 \lt 330$.)

Maximum and minimum values

Because $\cos$ is between $-1$ and $1$, $k\cos(x - \alpha)$ has maximum $k$ (when $x - \alpha = 0$, so $x = \alpha$) and minimum $-k$ (when $x - \alpha = 180°$). For example, $4\cos x + 3\sin x = 5\cos(x - 36.9)°$ has maximum $5$ at $x = 36.9°$ and minimum $-5$ at $x = 216.9°$.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2026 course report

"Many candidates made processing errors during at least one stage of their working", and in the harder part "only a few candidates applied the period of the graph accurately". The most common slip is a wrong quadrant for $\alpha$: always write down the signs of $k\sin\alpha$ and $k\cos\alpha$ and mark the quadrant on a CAST diagram.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • write $a\cos x + b\sin x$ as $k\cos(x \pm \alpha)$ or $k\sin(x \pm \alpha)$, choosing the right quadrant;
  • use it to solve equations and to find maximum and minimum values and where they occur;
  • sketch the resulting graph.
