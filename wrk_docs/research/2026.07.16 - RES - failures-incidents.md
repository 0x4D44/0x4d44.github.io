# Research Brief: Cruise Ship Engineering Failures, Incidents & Disasters

*For engine-room management simulation design (Chief Engineer perspective).
Agent-researched 2026-07-16; sources at end.*

---

## 1. Major Real-World Incidents & Causal Chains

### Carnival Splendor — Engine Room Fire, 8 Nov 2010 (off Mexico)

**Causal chain:** An earlier, undetected *hydrolock* event (liquid in a cylinder
during starting) bent the B1 connecting rod of Diesel Generator 5 → bent rod ran
for months → high-cycle fatigue crack → catastrophic fracture at ~06:00 at sea →
rod punched through the crankcase, ejecting hot debris and oil → Class B fire in
the aft engine room. The local Hi-Fog water-mist system was **not activated for
15 minutes**; the fixed CO2 system then failed to deploy properly due to
design/installation faults (a finding extended to all Dream-class ships). The
fire burned cable runs that passed through the space — defeating the fore/aft
engine-room redundancy split — causing **total loss of propulsion and hotel
power**. 3,299 passengers spent ~3 days adrift; USS Ronald Reagan airlifted food
("the Spam cruise"); the ship was towed to San Diego. Contributors per USCG:
poor air-cooler condition, rust ingestion, crew firefighting errors.

*Game hooks:* a latent defect (bent rod) planted hours/days before the event;
delayed suppression multiplying damage; fire severity determining whether cable
trays (and thus the "healthy" plant) survive.

### Carnival Triumph — "The Poop Cruise", 10 Feb 2013 (Gulf of Mexico)

**Causal chain:** A **flexible fuel-oil return hose on DG6 leaked**, spraying
fuel onto a hot surface → fire. Suppression (Hi-Fog, then CO2) worked as
designed and the fire was out quickly — but cable damage still killed the
electrical plant. The fleet had logged **nine flexible-fuel-line leak incidents
in the prior two years**; a compliance order to fit spray shields existed, but
this hose section was unshielded. With power lost: no propulsion, no HVAC, and —
critically — the **vacuum sewage system died**. Toilets stopped ship-wide;
passengers used red biohazard bags; when the ship heeled under tow, sewage ran
along corridors. 4,229 people, ~4.5 days under tow to Mobile.

*Game hooks:* a small, cheap component (a £50 hose, a missing spray shield) as
root cause; deferred-maintenance/compliance-backlog mechanics; cascading
hotel-systems collapse (sanitation, refrigeration, HVAC) as the real crisis
after the fire is out.

### Viking Sky — Blackout & Near-Grounding, Hustadvika, Norway, 23 Mar 2019

The gold-standard causal chain for a game (NSIA final report, 2024):

1. Ship sails from Tromsø with **1 of 4 DGs already unavailable** — no
   Safe-Return-to-Port redundancy.
2. Lube-oil sump levels on the running engines held at **28–40% of capacity**
   (maker MAN recommended 68–75%). Levels were *low but above the low-level
   alarm* — everything looked normal.
3. Storm in Hustadvika: ~38–45 kt winds, 9–10 m seas, heavy pitching/rolling.
4. Ship motion sloshed the shallow sumps; **suction pipes intermittently drew
   air** → lube-oil pressure fluctuations → **low LO pressure trips** → the DGs'
   protection systems **auto-shutdown the engines one after another** → full
   blackout at 13:37.
5. Dead ship on a lee shore: Mayday, both anchors dropped and **dragged**; ship
   came within ~100 m (one ship-length) of rocks with 1,373 aboard. Sump design
   was later found non-compliant with SOLAS II-1 Reg 26.6 (operation at dynamic
   inclinations).
6. Recovery was slow (crew training cited): partial power ~40 minutes in; 464
   people evacuated by helicopter winch over hours; ship limped to Molde.

*Game hooks:* the definitive "parameters within alarm limits but unsafe in
context" event; weather × plant-condition interaction; protection systems doing
their job and *causing* the emergency; drama of anchors + drift timer to
grounding.

### Costa Concordia — Grounding & Capsize, Isola del Giglio, 13 Jan 2012

**Causal chain:** Unauthorized close-pass "salute" at 15+ kt → struck rock at
21:45 → **53 m gash** in the port hull along the machinery spaces → **five (soon
six) contiguous watertight compartments flooded**, including both main engine
rooms and the main switchboards (WTC5 flooded in minutes) → immediate blackout
and loss of propulsion. At 21:52 the chief engineer and electrical officer
**failed to start/load the emergency diesel generator**. The ship drifted, was
blown back ashore, grounded, and capsized. Abandon-ship was ordered only at
22:54 — over an hour after impact. 32 dead. Design lesson: vital equipment
concentrated in adjacent compartments meant damage "well beyond survivability
standards."

*Game hooks:* flooding rate vs. bilge/ballast capacity; emergency generator that
fails on demand (test it in-game or it fails when needed); progressive list
disabling pumps and lifeboats (lifeboats can't launch beyond ~20° heel).

### MS Explorer — Ice Sinking, Antarctica, 23 Nov 2007

Captain misjudged ice — treated hard multi-year/glacial ice as soft first-year
Baltic ice → hull holed (fist-sized breach plus opened weld seams) → flooding
exceeded pump capacity, spread via internal openings → progressive list →
abandon ship into boats/rafts in Antarctic waters; all 154 rescued by MS
Nordnorge ~5 hours later; ship sank ~20 hours after holing. *Game hook:*
slow-motion losing battle — pumps vs. ingress — where the engineering "win" is
buying evacuation time.

### Rogue Waves

- **Queen Elizabeth 2, Sep 1995** (Hurricane Luis, N. Atlantic): ~29 m wave the
  master likened to "the White Cliffs of Dover"; ship effectively drove
  through/over it; bridge windows at 95 ft.
- **Viking Polaris, 29 Nov 2022** (Drake Passage): rogue wave shattered cabin
  windows; one passenger killed by glass, four injured; structural damage
  cancelled the voyage.
- *Game hooks:* random low-probability event during storms — momentary huge
  roll/pitch, window/hull damage on lower decks, water ingress alarms, items
  torn loose in the ER, injuries.

### Norovirus Outbreaks

Recurrent (CDC Vessel Sanitation Program logs several per year; Explorer of the
Seas 2014: ~700 ill). Engineering relevance: potable-water chlorination and
production (evaporators/RO), pool/spa treatment, galley refrigeration, HVAC as
(minor) spread vector, and surge demand on laundry/hot water during outbreak
deep-cleans. *Game hook:* a "hotel plant" crisis that loads generators and
freshwater plant while crew numbers available for maintenance drop.

## 2. Engine-Room Failure Mode Catalog

*(Format: Warning signs/alarms → escalation if ignored → correct intervention.
Core event-design data.)*

**Crankcase explosion (main/diesel engine).** Alarms: `OIL MIST DETECT HIGH`
(Graviner-type detector, per-engine), rising main-bearing/big-end temps, LO temp
deviation. Escalation: hot spot vaporizes oil → mist reaches ~50 mg/L LEL →
primary explosion blows crankcase relief doors in **seconds**; opening crankcase
doors early admits air → lethal secondary explosion. Intervention: on oil-mist
alarm, **slow down/stop engine immediately** (auto-slowdown typical), keep clear
of relief doors, **wait 20–30 min minimum** before opening, locate hot spot.
Timescale from first mist alarm to explosion: seconds–minutes.

**Scavenge fire (2-stroke).** Signs: high exhaust temp on one unit, `SCAV AIR
TEMP HIGH`, surging turbocharger, sparks/smoke at scavenge drains, paint
blistering on trunking (touch test). Escalation over 5–30 min: unit fire →
distortion, crankcase explosion risk, uptake fire. Intervention: reduce to dead
slow, cut fuel to affected unit, increase cylinder lubrication; small fires
self-extinguish in ~15 min; major fire: stop engine, engage turning gear, close
scavenge space, inject fixed CO2/steam smothering. Prevention: drain scavenge
spaces each watch (oil/carbon buildup is the fuel).

**Turbocharger failure.** Signs: surging ("barking/howling" at load changes),
`T/C VIBRATION HIGH`, `T/C LO PRESS LOW`, exhaust temps rising across all
cylinders, drop in scavenge pressure. Escalation: bearing failure in
minutes–hours → rotor seizure or burst; blade shedding wrecks the casing.
Intervention: reduce load; if severe, stop, lock rotor, run engine derated
(~50–60% power) on remaining T/Cs with blanked ports.

**Fuel injector failure.** Signs: `EXH TEMP DEVIATION HIGH` on one cylinder
(±50°C from average is a classic alarm), knocking, smoke. Escalation over hours:
afterburning → burned exhaust valve, cracked piston crown, scavenge fire.
Intervention: cut out unit or change injector at reduced load (30–60 min job).

**Cracked liner/head.** Signs: level drop in HT expansion tank (`EXP TANK LEVEL
LOW`), water in scavenge drains, pressurized cooling system (combustion gas),
white exhaust. Escalation: water into cylinder → hydrolock on restart (see
Splendor!) or bearing corrosion. Intervention: cut out cylinder, isolate cooling
to that unit, plan repair in port; **always turn engine on gear with indicator
cocks open before restart**.

**Purifier failure → cat fines.** HFO must be centrifuged from ≤60 mg/kg Al+Si
(ISO 8217 bunker limit) to **<15 mg/kg at engine inlet**. Signs: purifier sludge
discharge faults, `PURIFIER WATER CONTENT HIGH`, rising fuel-pump/liner wear on
samples. Escalation is *silent and slow*: days–weeks of abrasive wear → scuffed
liners, seized fuel pumps, blowby — a fleet-killer discovered too late.
Intervention: run purifiers in series, reduce throughput, correct fuel temp
(98°C), drain tank water; ultimately liner renewal.

**Boiler flame failure / furnace explosion.** Alarms: `BURNER FLAME FAIL`,
`BOILER LOW WATER LEVEL` (two independent low-level cutouts). Escalation:
repeated re-fire attempts without purge → unburned vapor accumulates → furnace
explosion in seconds; low water → tube overheat/rupture in minutes.
Intervention: mandatory **pre-purge (30 s–3 min)** before any re-ignition; on
low water, kill burners, never feed cold water to a dry-fired boiler.

**Cooling water loss / overheating.** Alarms: `SW PRESS LOW`, `HT JACKET OUTLET
TEMP HIGH` (~90–95°C alarm), `EXP TANK LEVEL LOW`. Causes: sea-chest strainer
blocked (jellyfish, mussels, ice slush — switch high/low sea chests), pump
failure, pipe burst. Escalation: 5–20 min to piston seizure/auto-shutdown.
Intervention: swap standby pump (auto-cut-in on pressure drop), change sea
suction, reduce power.

**Generator synchronization failure.** Signs: sync-scope spinning fast, breaker
closes out of phase → violent bus transient, `REVERSE POWER TRIP` (device 32),
possible sheared coupling/crankshaft damage, partial blackout. Intervention:
match frequency/voltage, close at 11-to-12-o'clock scope position; use
sync-check relay (25). *Game hook:* a manual-sync minigame under time pressure
during blackout recovery.

**Switchboard / electrical fire.** Signs: burning smell, `EARTH FAULT 440V/220V`
alarms preceding it, arc flash. Escalation: bus-bar arc destroys a whole
switchboard section in seconds; loss of one main board = half the plant.
Intervention: isolate section, CO2 extinguisher (never water), split bus-tie to
save the other board.

**Steering gear failure.** Alarms: `STEERING GEAR HYD LO LEVEL`, `PHASE
FAILURE`, rudder-angle mismatch. Escalation: hardover rudder in traffic within
seconds. Intervention: switch to No.2 pump/system (seconds), then local
emergency steering from the steering flat on bridge orders by phone (SOLAS
requires drills every 3 months).

**Shaft/stern tube/azipod bearings.** Alarms: `INTERM SHAFT BRG TEMP HIGH`
(~65°C alarm, 70–75°C slowdown/trip), `STERN TUBE LO TANK LEVEL LOW` (aft seal
leaking — oil out or water in). Azipods add thrust-bearing vibration monitoring;
historic early-Azipod bearing failures (e.g., QM2) forced speed restrictions and
dry-docking. Escalation: hours from first temp creep to wiped white metal.
Intervention: reduce shaft RPM, boost LO cooling; pod failure = proceed on
remaining pod at reduced speed.

**Hull leaks — bilge flooding.** Alarms: `BILGE WELL LEVEL HIGH` (per
compartment; repeated alarms = rising trend), then `BILGE HIGH-HIGH`.
Escalation: submerged pumps/motors within tens of minutes; free-surface effect
degrades stability. Intervention: identify ingress (sea chest, stern tube,
pipe), start additional bilge pumps, use **direct bilge injection** (main SW
pump suction to bilge) for major flooding, close watertight doors, inform bridge
for list/trim correction.

**Engine-room fire (the master event).** Detection: flame/smoke detectors,
`FIRE ZONE 3 MACHINERY` on the safety panel. Chief Engineer sequence: confirm →
**quick-closing fuel valves** (remote pull) → stop purifiers & fuel pumps →
**stop ventilation fans, close fire dampers/skylights** → evacuate & muster with
headcount → seal space → **release CO2 only after confirmed evacuation** (total
flooding must discharge 85% of gas within 2 min) → boundary cooling → keep
sealed **12–24 h**, monitor boundary temps; premature reopening = reflash.
Hi-Fog local protection can trigger first without evacuation. Both Carnival
fires show: even a "won" fire loses the ship's power if cable runs burn.

**HVAC chiller failure.** No safety alarm — a *hotel* crisis: in the tropics,
cabin temps become intolerable in 2–6 h; galley refrigeration follows. Chillers
are among the largest electrical consumers (hotel load is ~40–70% of a cruise
ship's electrical demand), so shedding them is also the standard
blackout-recovery load-management move.

**Vacuum sewage failure.** Alarms: `VACUUM PRESS LOW` (system needs ~ -0.4 to
-0.5 bar). Causes: vacuum pump failure, pipe blockage (wipes!), collecting-tank
full. Escalation: toilets stop ship-wide within minutes; per Triumph, days
without it = public-health collapse. Intervention: isolate blocked branch,
restore vacuum units, manage tank levels.

## 3. Weather Effects

- **Sea states:** Douglas SS5 (rough, 2.5–4 m) — noticeable motion, secure the
  ER; SS6 (very rough, 4–6 m) — speed reduction, stabilizers deployed, sloshing
  effects on tank suctions (Viking Sky); SS7+ (high, 6–9 m) — course/speed
  changes mandatory, slamming risk. Cruise ships typically avoid > Beaufort 8–9
  via **weather routing** (commercial pressure to keep schedule vs. safety — a
  great game decision).
- **Parametric rolling:** in head/following seas when wave-encounter period ≈
  half the natural roll period; roll amplifies from ~5° to 30–40° within **a few
  cycles (1–2 minutes)**. Countered by changing speed/heading. Flared modern
  hulls are susceptible.
- **Slamming:** bow-flare impacts in head seas; whipping stresses; the classic
  master's remedy is slow down and/or alter heading ("heave to").
- **Stabilizers:** fin stabilizers cut roll up to ~85% *at speed*; nearly
  useless below ~8–10 kt — so a blackout in beam seas (Viking Sky) means violent
  rolling precisely when the plant needs stability.
- **Rogue waves:** rare storm event; momentary extreme roll/pitch + possible
  window/hull breach (QE2, Viking Polaris).

## 4. Blackout / Dead-Ship Recovery (step-by-step, the signature procedure)

1. **T+0 s:** Blackout. Main engines/DGs trip; emergency lighting (battery)
   instantly.
2. **T+≤45 s:** **Emergency DG auto-starts and closes onto the emergency
   switchboard** (SOLAS requirement) — feeds emergency lighting, steering gear,
   one fire pump, bilge pump, comms, and one starting-air compressor.
3. **T+1–5 min:** Establish cause (don't restart into the fault — if it was a
   fire or flooding, fix that first). Verify starting air (receivers hold a
   limited number of starts — nominally 12 for reversible, 6 for non-reversible
   engines) and control power.
4. **T+5–15 min:** Start one standby DG (air or hydraulic start), close its
   breaker onto the **dead main bus**, then sequence-restart essential
   auxiliaries: SW cooling pumps → LT/HT FW pumps → LO pumps → fuel
   supply/booster → compressors.
5. **T+15–30 min:** Start and **synchronize** additional DGs one at a time;
   shed/restore non-essential (hotel) loads progressively — chillers and galley
   last.
6. **T+20–45 min:** Restore propulsion (pods/motors need switchboards and
   drives back up). Viking Sky took ~40 min to partial power with the ship
   drifting toward rocks — that's your game's clock.
7. Failure branches: emergency DG fails to start (Concordia), starting air
   exhausted (must run emergency compressor from emergency board — slow),
   repeated trips because root cause (LO suction, fuel, cooling) persists.

**Abandon-ship engineering duties:** keep emergency DG and fire/bilge pumps
running as long as possible, maintain lighting and PA, transfer ballast to limit
list (lifeboats unusable beyond ~20°), then secure fuel systems and evacuate
machinery spaces on the master's order.

## 5. Design Notes for the Event System

- **Layer causality:** latent defect (weeks) → abnormal parameter (hours) →
  alarm (minutes) → protection trip or casualty (seconds). Let players intercept
  at any stage; earlier = cheaper.
- **"In-limits but unsafe"** (Viking Sky) and **"protection causes blackout"**
  are the most instructive mechanics.
- **Fires are twice-lethal:** the flames, then the cables. Model cable-run
  damage as the thing that turns a local fire into ship-wide power loss.
- **Redundancy erosion:** sailing with one DG down, an unshielded hose, a low
  sump — each is fine alone; the storm finds the combination.
- Realistic alarm vocabulary: `ME1 CYL 4 EXH GAS TEMP HIGH DEV`, `DG3 LO PRESS
  LOW SLOWDOWN`, `OIL MIST DETECTOR ME2`, `BILGE WELL ER AFT HIGH`, `STCP QUICK
  CLOSING VALVES ACTIVATED`, `EMCY GEN RUNNING ON LOAD`, `FO PURIFIER 2 SLUDGE
  DISCHARGE FAIL`, `STEERING GEAR NO1 PUMP PHASE FAIL`.
