# Design Brief: Browser-Based Cruise Ship Chief Engineer Sim — Game Design References

*Agent-researched 2026-07-16; sources at end.*

## 1. The landscape: what works, what fails

**Barotrauma** — the Engineer role works because the player owns a legible
domain (reactor, grid, pumps) whose failures create urgent, physical work, and
skilled manual play beats automation. Failure mode: chaos without crewmates; a
single-player sim must replace crewmates with dispatchable NPC crew.

**FTL** — the canonical crisis-management loop: a calm strategic layer
punctuated by real-time emergencies where the scarce resource is *power and
attention*. Power-rerouting under fire is exactly the diesel-electric
load-shedding decision. Every crisis is a *triage* problem with visible
competing claims on one scarce budget. Skip: permadeath framing.

**Oxygen Not Included** — take the ripple-effect economy (every fix creates a
downstream problem); reject everything-live-from-minute-one onboarding.

**Nucleares / Stationeers** — prove appetite for "you are the plant operator"
fidelity but cap their audience with no-tutorial opacity. Validate
"arcade-ified physics": exaggerated time constants keep a realistic plant
playable.

**Mini Metro** — the abstraction benchmark: borrows a real-world visual language
(transit map) instead of inventing one. Our equivalent: the **single-line
electrical diagram and engine-room mimic panel**. Instant legibility plus
authenticity, plus procedural escalation on a fixed board.

**Stormworks** — the opposite failure: infinite freedom, weak curated goals.
We want authored scenarios on a fixed ship, not a sandbox.

**PC Building Simulator** — the satisfying "job ticket" micro-loop: diagnose →
open → swap → verify → close. Right shape for routine maintenance between
crises. **A Dark Room** — the *reveal* structure: UI elements don't exist until
unlocked; the cleanest tutorialization. **Tycoons** — campaign scaffolding: each
level is a new map + new vehicle + one new mechanic.

### The core loop

Steady state must be *optimization with a scoreboard*, not idle waiting: trim
fuel curves, balance generator load, schedule preventive maintenance against a
finite crew-hours budget. Crises must be *cascades with a triage decision*: a
fouled fuel filter starves DG3 → DG3 trips → grid overload → ~30 s to shed hotel
load (angry passengers) or risk full blackout (much worse). The engagement
engine is the tension between the modes: preventive maintenance you skipped is
exactly what cascades later, so the player authored their own crisis. Causal
legibility — "I gambled on that filter" — makes failure feel fair.

**Recommended loop (one level ≈ one voyage):** Prepare (allocate spares/crew,
1 min) → Steady-state optimize (~60% of playtime) → Crisis (alarm cascade at 1x,
triage, dispatch, ~30%) → Recover & report (star rating, ~10%). Passenger
comfort is the score currency that makes engineering tradeoffs mean something.

## 2. Progression skeleton

Gate systems, not just difficulty numbers; each level a new ship so complexity
feels diegetic. A system that isn't live yet does not appear on the panel at
all. Six levels (level = new ship + route + 1–2 new live systems):

1. **200-pax expedition ship, fjords.** 2 DGs, one switchboard, fuel, cooling.
   Teaches mimic, load balance, alarm-acknowledge. One scripted fault. 8–10 min.
2. **700-pax coastal, Baltic.** Adds hotel load as competing consumer, load
   shedding, maintenance ticket queue. 10 min.
3. **1,500-pax classic liner, Med.** Adds fuel treatment (purifiers — the
   preventive-maintenance gamble), crew dispatch, first two-stage cascade.
   12 min.
4. **2,500-pax, transatlantic.** Adds weather as load/vibration driver,
   redundancy management (N+1, split switchboards, bus-tie), schedule pressure.
   15 min.
5. **4,000-pax, Caribbean 7-day.** Full auxiliary spread (freshwater, HVAC,
   sewage — hotel systems that fail loudly), port transitions, multi-fault
   cascades. 15–18 min.
6. **6,000-pax mega-ship finale.** Everything live; azipods, safe-return-to-port
   fire scenario: run half the plant while keeping 6,000 passengers comfortable.
   The exam. 20 min.

**Scoring:** three stars per level — (1) schedule, (2) comfort, (3) a per-level
engineering-excellence criterion. Stars gate nothing hard (next level at
completion); they feed replay. **Failure states that teach:** blackout is not
game over — it's a recoverable emergency-generator scenario; the post-voyage
report names the causal chain ("Blackout at 14:32 ← DG3 trip ← fuel starvation ←
purifier maintenance deferred on Day 2"). Losing reads like an incident report,
not a slap.

## 3. UI patterns worth stealing

1. **The alarm annunciator grid (the identity of the game).** Fixed grid of
   labelled tiles, real ISA-18.1 sequence: dark = normal; flashing + horn = new
   alarm; ACK → steady lit; cleared → dark. The acknowledge ritual converts
   panic into procedure; one glance reads plant health. Reflows 8×4 desktop →
   4×8 phone with zero redesign.
2. **High-performance HMI grayscale (ISA-101).** Normal state in flat grays;
   color exclusively for abnormal: yellow warning, red critical, blue operator
   action. The "calm screen" measurably reduces missed alarms; visually
   distinctive and credible; trivially dark-mode.
3. **The mimic/single-line diagram as the main playfield.** Generators →
   breakers → main bus → propulsion + hotel feeders in real one-line vocabulary,
   with live power-flow numbers. Interaction happens ON the diagram — click a
   breaker to open it, click a generator to start it. Simultaneously tutorial,
   dashboard, and control surface. Phone: pan/zoom, touch targets ≥ 44 px.
4. **Timestamped event log + engine-order telegraph.** `14:32:07 DG3 TRIP —
   REVERSE POWER` log is the cause-tracing tool and the narrative channel
   (bridge messages arrive in it). The telegraph gives the bridge a personality
   and gives crises a trigger ("bridge demands full power now, mid-repair").

Gauges: analog needles for 2–3 hero instruments only (shaft RPM, bus frequency);
**bar meters with marked normal bands** for everything else. Layout: desktop =
mimic center, annunciator top strip, log right rail; phone = tab bar (Mimic /
Alarms / Log / Crew) + persistent one-line alarm ticker. Design the mimic first
at 390 px wide.

## 4. Session shape

Target **8–20 minutes per level**. Essentials:
- **Time compression 1x/4x/16x + pause; auto-drop to 1x on any new critical
  alarm** (and flash the speed control). Orders issuable while paused
  (pause-and-plan).
- **Autosave to localStorage** on: every 60 game-minutes, crisis start/end,
  `visibilitychange` (more reliable than `beforeunload` on mobile), level end.
  Versioned JSON well under the 5 MB limit; offer export/import of campaign
  save.
- Resume mid-voyage instant and legible: reopen paused with a "situation card".

## 5. Pitfalls (ranked by lethality)

1. **Overwhelming first contact.** Countermeasure is structural: Level 1
   physically contains only two generators and ~8 annunciator tiles. If the
   first screen has more than ~10 interactive elements, cut.
2. **Tutorial walls of text.** Teach via the annunciator itself: mentor Chief's
   messages arrive in the event log, each teaching one action. Never a modal
   essay.
3. **Alarm fatigue.** Keep baseline alarms rare and meaningful; make cascade
   scenarios deliberately floody and give prioritization tools. Never two
   unrelated crises at once in levels 1–3.
4. **Invisible simulation depth.** No state may cause a failure unless it was
   inspectable beforehand and is named in the post-incident report.
5. **Steady state that's actually idle.** Fuel economy, maintenance scheduling
   and load planning must offer real score at 1x attention — the star criteria
   guarantee this.

**Bottom line:** FTL's triage loop inside Mini Metro's borrowed-real-diagram
legibility, skinned in ISA-101 control-room aesthetics, taught by an idle game's
progressive reveal, scored like a tycoon campaign. The annunciator panel is the
signature; the deferred-maintenance-becomes-your-crisis causal chain is the
soul.
