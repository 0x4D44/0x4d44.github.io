# Research Brief: The Cruise Ship Chief Engineer — Role, Plant, and Systems

*For engine-room management simulation design. Agent-researched 2026-07-16;
sources at end.*

## 1. The Chief Engineer's Role and Department

**Position.** The Chief Engineer (CE) is head of the technical department and
one of the ship's three or four senior officers, reporting directly to the
Master (Captain). On a cruise ship the CE is largely a *manager*: budgets,
compliance paperwork, planned maintenance oversight, bunkering sign-off, and
liaison with the shoreside technical superintendent. Hands-on watchkeeping is
delegated.

**Department organization (mega ship, ~80–120 technical crew):**
- **Chief Engineer** — overall command, statutory responsibility.
- **Staff Chief Engineer** — day-to-day operations manager, runs the main
  engineering subdivision; effectively the CE's deputy. (Cruise-specific rank.)
- **First Engineer(s)** — senior operational engineer(s), oversee the watch
  teams and major jobs.
- **Second Engineers (×3)** — senior watchkeepers, one per watch team.
- **Third/Fourth Engineers** — junior watchkeepers.
- **Chief Electrician / ETOs + 4–8 electricians** — HV systems, propulsion
  converters, automation.
- **Hotel Services Engineer & HVAC Engineer** — cruise-specific roles.
- **Environmental Engineer/Officer** — wastewater, garbage, MARPOL records.
- **Motormen/oilers (10–20), fitters, welders, plumbers, refrigeration techs.**

A 200-pax expedition ship runs the whole department with ~6–8 people: CE, 2nd
Eng, 3rd Eng, ETO, 2–3 motormen.

**Watchkeeping.** Unlike cargo ships (often unmanned/UMS at night), cruise ships
keep the **Engine Control Room (ECR) manned 24/7**, typically three teams on
4-on-8-off watches (12–4, 4–8, 8–12), each team = one Second Engineer + a junior
+ a motorman doing physical rounds. The ECR consoles mirror the Integrated
Automation System (~30,000+ I/O tags on a mega ship).

**ISM Code duties.** Under the Safety Management System the CE owns: the
technical planned-maintenance system, the **critical equipment list** (steering,
DGs, fire pumps, emergency generator — tested on schedule), permit-to-work,
non-conformity and near-miss reporting to the Designated Person Ashore, and
drill participation. **SOPEP**: the CE typically commands spill response. The
Oil Record Book is a personal legal liability; falsification ("magic pipe"
cases) has produced multi-million-dollar fines and jail time.

## 2. Power Plant — Real Numbers

**Architecture.** Modern cruise ships are **diesel-electric power stations**:
medium-speed diesels (500–514 rpm) drive alternators onto a 6.6 kV or 11 kV
main bus; everything — propulsion motors, thrusters, chillers, galleys — is an
electrical consumer. Plants are split into **two engine rooms separated by
watertight/fire bulkheads** with independent switchboards (SOLAS "Safe Return to
Port," ships >120 m built after 2010: lose one compartment to fire/flood and
still limp home). Distribution steps down 6.6 kV/11 kV → 690 V → 440 V → 230 V.

**Reference plants (verified):**

| Ship class | Gensets | Total installed | Propulsion | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oasis/Allure of the Seas** (~6,700 pax) | 3× Wärtsilä 16V46D @ ~18.5 MW + 3× 12V46D @ ~13.9 MW | ~97 MW | 3× ABB Azipod, 20 MW each | 4× 5.5 MW bow thrusters |
| **Harmony of the Seas** | 4× Wärtsilä 12V46F @ 14.4 MW + 2× 16V46F @ 19.2 MW | ~96 MW | 3× 20 MW Azipods | ~100 MVA alternator capacity |
| **Icon of the Seas** (7,600 pax, LNG) | 6× Wärtsilä 46DF dual-fuel | ~67.5 MW | 3 Azipods | First RCI LNG ship |
| **Typical mid-size** (~2,000 pax) | 4–5 gensets, 8–12 MW each | 40–60 MW | 2 pods or 2 shaft motors, 14–20 MW each | 2–3× 2.5–3 MW bow thrusters |
| **Expedition** (~200 pax) | 4× 2–3 MW | 8–12 MW | 2× 2.5–3.5 MW | 1× 1–1.5 MW bow thruster |

**Hotel vs propulsion load.** At full sea speed, propulsion dominates (up to
~75–85% of generation on big fast ships); at anchor, hotel load is everything.
Plausible game figures:

| Ship size | Hotel load | Propulsion @ service speed | Total at sea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expedition (200 pax) | 1.5–2.5 MW | 4–6 MW | 6–8 MW |
| Mid (2,000 pax) | 8–12 MW | 20–30 MW | 30–40 MW |
| Mega (6,000+ pax) | 15–25 MW | 40–60 MW | 60–80 MW |

Hotel-load breakdown (mega ship): HVAC chillers 10–15 MW (the single biggest
hotel consumer; 4–6 centrifugal chillers of 3–5 MW cooling each), galleys 2–3 MW
(30,000 meals/day), lighting 2–3 MW, laundry ~1 MW, freshwater plant 1–2 MW,
theatres/casino/pools ~1–2 MW. Hotel load swings with climate (Caribbean noon vs
Alaska) and time of day.

**Power Management System (PMS).** The core game loop lives here:
- **Load-dependent start/stop:** next standby DG auto-starts when bus load
  exceeds ~85% of online capacity; surplus DG stops below ~65–70%. Running more
  engines than needed wastes fuel (SFOC rises steeply below ~50% load); too few
  risks blackout.
- **Spinning reserve:** enough online margin that tripping the largest running
  generator doesn't collapse the bus (N+1; DP/manoeuvring modes demand more).
- **Heavy-consumer request:** bow thrusters and fire pumps must request power —
  PMS starts another DG before allowing the breaker to close.
- **Blackout defense in stages:** (1) fast propulsion load reduction
  (milliseconds), (2) **preferential tripping** of non-essential loads in 2–3
  stages (galley, laundry, some HVAC), (3) blackout: emergency generator +
  batteries carry emergency lighting/steering, then sequential restart. A
  blackout during manoeuvring is the nightmare scenario.

**Fuels & consumption.**
- **SFOC:** medium-speed diesel ~**175–185 g/kWh** at optimum (75–85% load),
  degrading to 200–210+ g/kWh below 40% load.
- **Fuel types:** HFO/HSFO (3.5% S, needs scrubber, heated to ~130–150 °C, 380
  cSt), VLSFO (0.5% S), MGO (0.1% S, unheated), LNG (dual-fuel).
- **Daily burn:** Oasis-class ~**250 t/day** at full speed, ~150–180 t/day
  typical itinerary; mid-size ~70–100 t/day; expedition ~10–20 t/day. Bunker
  capacity: mega ~3,500–4,500 t (~2 weeks endurance).

**Bunkering (rich gameplay scenario).** Pre-transfer meeting and checklist with
barge; scuppers plugged, drip trays set, SOPEP gear staged; agreed pumping
sequence — slow start, full rate, **topping-off at reduced rate to max ~85–90%
tank fill**; continuous manifold watch and tank soundings. **Continuous-drip
sampler at the manifold**; MARPOL sample retained ≥12 months, BDN 3 years; new
fuel held in segregated tanks until lab analysis clears it (2–4 days). Risks:
**overflow/spill**, **off-spec sulfur**, **cat fines** (ISO 8217 allows ≤60
mg/kg, engines want **<15 mg/kg at inlet**; high cat fines destroy liners and
fuel pumps in hours), incompatibility/instability when mixing stems.

**ECA switchover.** Changeover from HFO/VLSFO to 0.1% MGO must **complete before
crossing the ECA boundary**, logged with time/position/tank soundings. It takes
hours: cool the system gradually from ~130 °C to ~40 °C — too fast risks
fuel-pump seizure (thermal shock, MGO viscosity dropping below ~2 cSt). A
botched switchover = engine stall in coastal waters.

## 3. Auxiliary Systems (the CE's whole zoo)

- **Fresh water:** ~250 L/person/day → mega ship needs ~2,000–2,600 t/day.
  **Flash/vacuum evaporators** using jacket-water waste heat (20–25 m³/h each,
  only work at sea with engines loaded) plus **reverse osmosis** (12–15 m³/h
  each, work anytime, ~3–4 kWh/m³). Chlorination + mineralization; evaporators
  must not run in polluted coastal water.
- **Sewage/wastewater:** black ~30–50 L/p/d, grey 150–250 L/p/d → mega ship
  treats 1,000–1,500 m³/day in membrane-bioreactor AWP plants. Discharge rules
  by area (Baltic special area, Alaska).
- **HVAC:** 4–6 centrifugal chillers, chilled-water loops; provision
  refrigeration rooms at different setpoints.
- **Steam:** **exhaust-gas economizers** at sea; 2 **oil-fired auxiliary
  boilers** (~10–16 t/h each) in port — steam heats HFO tanks, laundry, galley,
  evaporators, pools.
- **Fuel treatment:** settling tanks → **centrifugal separators/purifiers**
  (~5–10 m³/h each, 24/7) → day tanks. Purifier failure = cat fines reach
  engines.
- **Lube oil:** LO purifiers run continuously; consumption ~0.5 g/kWh (mega ship
  ~1 t/day); oil analysis (BN, water, wear metals) feeds condition monitoring.
- **Steering/stabilizers:** Azipod ships steer with pods; shaft ships twin
  rudders (SOLAS: 35°→30° in 28 s). Retractable **fin stabilizers** (1–2 pairs).
- **Other:** incinerator (sludge ≈ 1–2% of HFO burned), ballast + BWTS, bilge
  system with **15 ppm oily-water separator**, fire main + Hi-Fog pumps,
  emergency diesel generator (~1–2.5 MW, auto-start in 45 s), shore power
  (6.6/11 kV, 7–12 MW where fitted).

## 4. Emissions & Regulation

- **MARPOL Annex VI sulfur:** **0.50% global (2020); 0.10% in ECAs.** ECAs:
  Baltic, North Sea, North American, US Caribbean, **Mediterranean (from 1 May
  2025)**; Canadian Arctic/Norwegian Sea phasing ~2026–27; NE Atlantic
  ~2027–28.
- **Scrubbers (EGCS):** allow 3.5% HFO. **Open-loop** (banned in many ports),
  **closed-loop** (NaOH dosing, sludge ashore), **hybrid**. Consume 1–2% of
  engine power; washwater monitored. A scrubber fault inside an ECA forces
  immediate MGO switchover — gameplay.
- **NOx tiers:** Tier II ~10.5 g/kWh; **Tier III** (~2.6 g/kWh) in NOx-ECAs —
  SCR catalysts (urea ~15 g/kWh) or LNG.
- **EEXI** (2023) one-time; **CII** annual A–E rating (three D years or one E ⇒
  corrective plan) — a season-long fuel-efficiency score.
- **Port State Control:** inspectors check Oil Record Book vs soundings, fuel
  samples vs BDN, test emergency generator/fire pumps; deficiencies can
  **detain the ship** — missed cruise = millions.

## 5. Maintenance

- **Planned Maintenance System (AMOS/NS5 style):** running-hours or calendar
  jobs; class societies audit. Typical medium-speed genset intervals:
  - Daily/250 h: filters, T/C air filter, soundings.
  - 1,000–2,000 h: injector check, crankcase inspection, crankshaft deflections.
  - 4,000–8,000 h: cylinder-head overhauls, injector nozzle renewal.
  - **~12,000 h: turbocharger overhaul**; piston/ring renewal ~12,000–16,000 h.
  - **~24,000 h+: major overhaul** (liners, bearings). At ~7,000 running
    hours/year per engine, a 6-engine plant has something big due almost
    continuously — engines rotated so overhauls stagger.
- **Condition-based monitoring:** vibration, LO spectrography, cylinder pressure
  indication, thermography, performance trending; OEM remote monitoring.
- **Spares:** critical-spares list; a seized bearing without a spare = engine
  out for weeks; parts logistics to the next port is real CE work.
- **Class & docking:** annual → intermediate (~2.5 yr) → **Special Survey every
  5 years**; dry dock ~every 5 years, 2–4 weeks. Continuous Machinery Survey
  spreads overhaul credits year-round.

**Game-usable scenario seeds:** genset trip at 85% bus load, purifier failure →
cat-fines liner damage, bunker overflow during topping-off, off-spec bunker
quarantined, botched ECA switchover, scrubber outage in the Med, chiller loss in
the Caribbean, blackout during pilotage, PSC detention threat, CII slipping to
D, turbocharger overhaul due with no port time.
