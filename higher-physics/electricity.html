The redstone works · Electricity (E)

Redstone, Leyden jars and the light of an LED

Electricity is the smallest of the three areas, but it is packed with calculations that need several steps, and with graphs you must be able to read and sketch. It builds directly on National 5: V = IR, P = IV, series and parallel circuits. Here you add alternating current, the hidden resistance inside every battery, capacitors, and the semiconductor physics behind LEDs and solar cells.

🎖 Cadet's journal: Cadet Sergeant Rahman

"Somebody has built a redstone workshop on the orlop deck. Redstone dust works like wire, torches like power supplies, and there's a glass jar in the captain's cabin lined with silver foil. The captain says it's a Leyden jar, invented in 1745, and it stores electric charge. We're not to touch it. I'm going to find out why."

1 · Monitoring and measuring AC E · monitoring and measuring AC

A battery gives direct current (DC): it flows one way at a steady value. Alternating current (AC) is a current that changes direction and instantaneous value with time. The mains supply in the UK is AC with a frequency of 50 Hz: it reverses direction 100 times every second.

Peak and r.m.s. values

Because an AC voltage keeps changing, we need a fair way to compare it with DC. The root mean square (r.m.s.) value of an alternating voltage or current is the value of the direct voltage or current that would produce the same power in a resistor. For a sine wave:

Vrms = Vpeak√2

The peak is about 1.4 times the r.m.s. value.

Irms = Ipeak√2

The same rule for current.

T = 1f

Period: the time for one complete cycle.

The UK mains is quoted as 230 V. That is the r.m.s. value, so the peak voltage is 230 × √2 = 325 V. Meters on an AC setting read r.m.s. values.

Reading an oscilloscope

An oscilloscope draws a graph of voltage against time on a grid of squares called divisions. Two settings turn divisions into real values:

  • The Y-gain (volts per division): peak voltage = number of divisions from the centre line to a peak × Y-gain.
  • The timebase (time per division): period = number of divisions for one complete wave × timebase. Then f = 1/T.

The ship's oscilloscope

Change the supply, then the scope settings. Work out the peak voltage, r.m.s. voltage, period and frequency from the trace, then press "Show the answers" to check.

Worked example · reading the trace

An oscilloscope is set to 2.0 V/div and 5.0 ms/div. The trace rises 3.5 divisions above the centre line, and one complete wave spans 4.0 divisions. Calculate (a) the peak voltage, (b) the r.m.s. voltage, (c) the frequency.

Show solution
  1. (a) Vpeak = 3.5 × 2.0 = 7.0 V
  2. (b) Vrms = Vpeak / √2 = 7.0 / √2 → 4.9 V
  3. (c) T = 4.0 × 5.0 ms = 20 ms = 0.020 s. f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.020 → 50 Hz
⚠ From the 2025 course report
  • Markers saw: asked to show that a frequency was 50 Hz, "some did not show where the value of the period came from and so could not access all the marks". Do this: write the division count and the timebase: "4.0 div × 5 ms/div = 20 ms", then f = 1/T.
  • Markers saw: "Few candidates stated and justified what happened to the amplitude of the trace when the switch was closed." Do this: state the change, then link it to the change in voltage across the component the oscilloscope is connected to.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Find peak voltage, period and frequency from an oscilloscope trace (divisions × setting).
  • Use Vpeak = √2 Vr.m.s. and Ipeak = √2 Ir.m.s., and f = 1/T.

2 · Current, potential difference, power and resistance E · current, potential difference, power, and resistance

V = IR

Ohm's law for a resistor.

P = IV = I²R = V²R

Power: choose the version that uses what you know.

RT = R₁ + R₂ + …

Series: total resistance is larger than any one.

1RT = 1R₁ + 1R₂ + …

Parallel: total resistance is smaller than the smallest one.

Remember the rules from National 5: in series the current is the same everywhere and the potential differences add up to the supply voltage; in parallel each branch has the same potential difference and the branch currents add up. At Higher, questions chain several steps together. Draw a quick sketch and label everything you know as you go.

Worked example · a resistor network

A potential difference of 18 V is applied between X and Y. Calculate (a) the total resistance, (b) the current in the 4.0 Ω resistor, (c) the power dissipated in the 12 Ω resistor.

Show solution
  1. (a) Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/12 + 1/4.0 = 0.333 → R = 1 ÷ 0.333 = 3.0 Ω (don't forget to take the reciprocal). Total: RT = 6.0 + 3.0 → 9.0 Ω
  2. (b) Total current: I = V/R = 18 / 9.0 = 2.0 A. The p.d. across the 6.0 Ω resistor is 2.0 × 6.0 = 12 V, so the parallel pair has 18 − 12 = 6.0 V across it. Current in 4.0 Ω: I = 6.0 / 4.0 → 1.5 A
  3. (c) P = V²/R = 6.0² / 12 → 3.0 W

Potential dividers

Two resistors in series across a supply share the supply voltage in proportion to their resistances. This is a potential divider, and it is how sensor circuits turn a change in temperature or light into a change in voltage.

⚓ The redstone analogy, and where it breaks

In Minecraft, a redstone signal starts at strength 15 and drops by one for every block of dust it crosses. That is a good picture of the supply's energy per coulomb being shared out as potential differences across each component, until none is left at the end. A comparator compares two signal strengths and switches its output by the result. In a real sensor circuit the potential divider makes the signal, a voltage that changes with temperature or light, and a transistor or logic gate plays the comparator, switching an alarm when that voltage passes a set value.

Where it breaks: redstone dust loses signal by the block, whatever is attached, so it behaves like a long chain of equal resistors. A real wire has almost no resistance, and the voltage across each resistor depends on its share of the total resistance, not on its length in blocks. And a redstone repeater tops a signal back up to 15 for free; nothing in a real circuit adds energy except the supply.

V₁ = R₁R₁ + R₂ VS

The p.d. across R₁.

V₁V₂ = R₁R₂

The bigger resistor gets the bigger share.

Sharing the signal: a potential divider

Change R₁ and watch the voltages share out. Then make R₁ a thermistor (its resistance falls as it gets warmer) or an LDR (its resistance falls as light increases).

Worked example · a fire alarm sensor

A thermistor and a 1.0 kΩ resistor are connected in series across a 9.0 V supply. At room temperature (20 °C) the thermistor's resistance is 2.0 kΩ. Calculate the p.d. across the thermistor. State what happens to this p.d. as the temperature rises.

Show solution
  1. V₁ = R₁ / (R₁ + R₂) × VS = 2.0 / (2.0 + 1.0) × 9.0 → 6.0 V
  2. As the temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share of the supply: the p.d. across it decreases.

The resistances only need to be in the same units: kilohms in both is fine.

⚠ Exam lookout

In 2025, only some candidates picked the circuit giving the greatest power in a lamp. For "which circuit" questions, work out the current or voltage for the lamp in each option rather than guessing. P = I²R means that doubling the current quadruples the power.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Solve series and parallel resistor networks in several steps.
  • Use P = IV = I²R = V²/R.
  • Use both potential-divider relationships, including sensor circuits with a thermistor or LDR.

3 · Electrical sources and internal resistance E · electrical sources and internal resistance

Connect a bulb to a battery and measure the voltage across the battery's terminals: it is less than the voltage printed on the battery. Some of the energy is used up driving the current through the battery itself, because every real source has some resistance inside it.

📘 Definitions
  • Electromotive force (EMF), E: the energy the source gives to each coulomb of charge that passes through it. It equals the p.d. across the terminals when no current flows. ⚠ Here E is EMF (in volts), not energy; the same letter means energy in E = ½QV below. See same letter, different meaning.
  • Internal resistance, r: the resistance of the source itself.
  • Terminal potential difference (t.p.d.), V: the p.d. across the terminals of the source when a current flows.
  • Lost volts, Ir: the p.d. across the internal resistance: the energy per coulomb turned into heat inside the source.
  • Ideal supply: a source with no internal resistance (r = 0), so V = E whatever the current.
  • Short circuit: the terminals joined by a wire of (almost) zero resistance, so the current is the largest possible, I = E/r.
  • Open circuit: no current flows (I = 0), so there are no lost volts and the t.p.d. equals the EMF.

The energy given to each coulomb (E) is shared between the outside circuit (V) and the inside of the source (Ir):

E = V + Ir

EMF = t.p.d. + lost volts.

E = I(R + r)

Using V = IR for the external resistance R.

So as the current increases (lower external resistance), the lost volts increase and the t.p.d. falls. That is why car headlights dim while the starter motor is turning.

The redstone torch's hidden resistance

This cell has E = 1.5 V and r = 0.50 Ω. Vary R, record five or more readings, and watch the V–I graph appear. Try R = 0 (short circuit).

Experiment: measuring EMF and internal resistance

  1. Connect the cell in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor. Connect a voltmeter across the cell's terminals.
  2. Record the current I and the t.p.d. V. Change the resistance and repeat for a wide range of currents.
  3. Plot a graph of V (y-axis) against I (x-axis). Since V = E − rI, it is a straight line:
    • the y-intercept is the EMF E;
    • the gradient is −r, so r = −gradient;
    • the x-intercept is the short-circuit current, E/r.
  4. Switch off between readings so the cell does not warm up, which would change r.

Worked example · from the graph

A student's V–I graph is a straight line that meets the V axis at 1.60 V and the I axis at 2.00 A. Find the EMF, the internal resistance and the short-circuit current.

Show solution
  1. EMF = y-intercept = 1.60 V
  2. Gradient = (0 − 1.60) / (2.00 − 0) = −0.80 Ω, so r = 0.80 Ω
  3. Short-circuit current = x-intercept = 2.00 A (check: E/r = 1.60/0.80 = 2.00 A ✔)

Worked example · lost volts

A cell of EMF 1.5 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to a 2.5 Ω resistor. Calculate (a) the current, (b) the t.p.d., (c) the lost volts.

Show solution
  1. (a) E = I(R + r) → 1.5 = I × (2.5 + 0.50) → I = 0.50 A
  2. (b) V = IR = 0.50 × 2.5 → V = 1.3 V (unrounded: 1.25 V)
  3. (c) lost volts = Ir = 0.50 × 0.50 → 0.25 V. Check: 1.25 + 0.25 = 1.5 V = E ✔
⚠ From the 2025 course report: evaluating this experiment
  • Markers saw: in assignments on internal resistance, candidates who measured V and I directly "were claiming that the resistance of the wires should have been taken into account. This is not valid for this experimental method." Do this: remember that wire resistance is simply part of the external resistance when you measure the t.p.d. and current directly. Only make evaluative comments that fit the method you actually used.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • State the definitions of EMF, internal resistance, t.p.d. and lost volts.
  • Use E = V + Ir and E = I(R + r).
  • Describe the experiment to find E and r, and read them from the V–I graph (intercept = E, gradient = −r).

4 · Capacitors E · capacitors

A capacitor is two conducting plates separated by an insulator. Connect it to a supply and electrons pile up on one plate and are pulled off the other: the capacitor stores charge, and with it energy. The Leyden jar is a capacitor: metal foil inside and outside a glass jar, with the glass as the insulator.

📘 Capacitance

Capacitance is the charge stored per volt: C = Q / V. A capacitor of 1 farad stores 1 coulomb of charge when the potential difference across it is 1 volt. Practical capacitors are measured in microfarads (μF) or picofarads (pF).

If a capacitor is charged by a constant current, the charge stored after a time t is Q = It.

Keeping the current constant. To charge at constant current, connect the supply, a variable resistor, an ammeter and the capacitor in series, with a voltmeter across the capacitor. As the capacitor charges, its p.d. VC rises, so the p.d. left across the series resistor (VS − VC) falls. To keep the current I = (VS − VC) / R the same, you must reduce the resistance of the variable resistor steadily as the capacitor charges, watching the ammeter. The 2025 examiners asked for exactly this explanation.

Energy stored

The work done in charging a capacitor, which is the energy it stores, is the area under its charge–p.d. graph. That area is a triangle, ½ × base × height:

E = ½QV = ½CV² = ½ Q²C

⚠ Here E is energy (in joules), not EMF.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"W = QV for the electron gun. Why is the capacitor only ½QV?"

Captain Blackwood: "Because the jar fights back as it fills. The first coulomb goes on when the p.d. is nearly zero, so it costs almost nothing. The last coulomb goes on against the full V. The p.d. rises steadily from 0 to V, so on average each coulomb is moved through ½V. In the electron gun every charge falls through the whole V, so there it is the full QV."

Worked example · the camera-flash capacitor

A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V. Calculate (a) the charge stored and (b) the energy stored.

Show solution
  1. (a) Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 12 → Q = 5.6 × 10⁻³ C
  2. (b) E = ½CV² = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 12² → E = 3.4 × 10⁻² J

Worked example · charging at constant current

A capacitor is charged at a constant current of 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ A for 40 s. The p.d. across it is then 6.0 V. Calculate its capacitance.

Show solution
  1. Q = It = 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 40 = 1.2 × 10⁻³ C
  2. C = Q / V = 1.2 × 10⁻³ / 6.0 → C = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ F (200 μF)
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I connect the Leyden jar to a battery, does a steady current flow through it?"

Captain Blackwood: "Only while it fills. There's an insulator between the plates, so no charge ever crosses it. Current flows in the wires as electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other, but as the jar's p.d. rises to match the supply, that current dies away to zero. A charged capacitor blocks a steady (DC) current."

Charging and discharging through a resistor

Charging the Leyden jar

Run it to charge the capacitor, then flip the switch to discharge it. Restart with a bigger R or C and compare the curves. The current axis stays fixed, so you can see how R changes the starting current.

ChargingDischarging
p.d. across the capacitor VCrises quickly at first, then more and more slowly, levelling off at the supply voltagefalls quickly at first, then more slowly, towards zero
Current Ilargest at the start (I = VS/R, since VC = 0), falling towards zerolargest at the start (I = VC/R) but in the opposite direction, falling towards zero
WhyAs charge builds up, VC grows, so the p.d. across the resistor (VS − VC) falls, so the current falls.As charge flows off, VC falls, so the current it drives through R falls.

Effect of R and C: a larger resistance means a smaller current, so the capacitor takes longer to charge or discharge. The starting current is smaller, but the final voltage is the same. A larger capacitance means more charge must flow to reach the same voltage, so it also takes longer. The starting current is unchanged (it depends only on VS and R).

Experiment: current and voltage against time

  1. Set up the circuit above, with a voltmeter across the capacitor and an ammeter in series (or voltage and current sensors connected to a data logger).
  2. Move the switch to A and start a stopwatch. Record VC and I every 5 s (or let the data logger record them) until they stop changing.
  3. Move the switch to B and record VC and I again as the capacitor discharges.
  4. Plot graphs of VC and I against time. Repeat with a different resistor, or a different capacitor, to see the effect on the curves.
⚠ From the 2025 course report
  • Markers saw: "A number of candidates did not draw the correct shape for the curve, and others did not add the correct values to the axes of the graph." Do this: labelled axes with units, the right curve (steep at first, then levelling off), and key values marked: the starting current VS/R and the final voltage VS.
  • Markers saw: "Many candidates stated the correct change for the resistance, but few justified the change correctly." Do this: for constant-current charging, the resistance must be reduced, because as VC rises the p.d. across the resistor falls; a smaller R keeps I = (VS − VC)/R constant.
  • Markers saw: "Most candidates calculated the charge stored on the capacitor correctly" and most found the capacitance. Keep those marks.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Use C = Q/V, Q = It and E = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C.
  • Sketch and explain charging and discharging graphs of current and p.d. against time, with key values marked.
  • Explain the effect of R and C on charging time, and why R is reduced to keep a charging current constant.

5 · Semiconductors and p-n junctions E · semiconductors and p-n junctions

Energy bands

In a single atom, electrons occupy separate energy levels (as in the Bohr model). When atoms come together to form a solid, the levels spread out into energy bands separated by gaps. Two bands matter:

  • The valence band: the highest band that is occupied by electrons.
  • The conduction band: the band above it. Electrons in the conduction band are free to move through the solid and carry a current.

Every solid has its own characteristic band structure. For a solid to conduct, there must be both free electrons and empty states available for them to move into.

Band structure: three materials compared

Raise the temperature and watch the semiconductor (the numbers of electrons are illustrative). Dots are electrons; red rings are the holes they leave behind.

MaterialBand structureConduction
Conductor (metal)One or more bands is only partly filled, or the valence and conduction bands overlap.Plenty of free electrons with empty states to move into: highly conductive.
InsulatorThe valence band is full and the gap to the conduction band is large.At room temperature there is not enough energy to lift electrons across the gap: no conduction.
Semiconductor (silicon, germanium)Like an insulator, but the gap is small.At room temperature some electrons gain enough energy to reach the conduction band, so there is some conduction. Increasing the temperature increases the conductivity, because more electrons cross the gap.

Doping: p-type and n-type

Pure semiconductors conduct poorly. During manufacture they can be doped: tiny amounts of particular impurity atoms are added to increase their conductivity. This makes two types:

  • n-type: the impurity atoms have one more outer electron than silicon (for example phosphorus or arsenic: you need not name them). The extra electrons are free to move in the conduction band, so the majority charge carriers (the carriers there are most of) are negative electrons.
  • p-type: the impurity atoms have one fewer outer electron (for example boron). This leaves gaps, called holes, in the valence band. Neighbouring electrons can move into a hole, so the hole moves the other way, acting like a positive charge carrier.

The p-n junction

When p-type and n-type layers are made next to each other in one crystal, a p-n junction forms. Some free electrons from the n side fill holes on the p side. This leaves a thin region with no free charge carriers (the depletion layer; the name is background, not examined), and there is an electric field in the junction that stops any more charge crossing.

Why a field appears. Electrons that cross leave behind positive ions on the n side and make negative ions on the p side. That charge separation produces an electric field pointing from the n side to the p side. For electrons it is like a hill: an electron on the n side would have to climb it to reach the p side. Forward bias lowers the hill so electrons can roll over; reverse bias makes it steeper.

  • Forward bias (p-type connected to the positive terminal) reduces the electric field in the junction, and current flows.
  • Reverse bias (p-type connected to the negative terminal) increases the electric field, and almost no current flows.

Inside the junction

The coloured lines are the conduction band (top) and valence band (bottom), stepped at the junction. Try each bias, then light the junction up.

LEDs

A light-emitting diode is a forward-biased p-n junction that emits photons. The forward-bias p.d. makes electrons move from the conduction band of the n-type semiconductor towards the conduction band of the p-type. Photons are emitted when electrons "fall" from the conduction band into the valence band (filling holes) on either side of the junction. Each photon's energy is roughly the size of the band gap, so the semiconductor material decides the colour. A blue LED needs a larger band gap than a red LED (higher-energy photons, E = hf), so it needs a larger forward p.d. to light.

Worked example · a red LED

The crow's-nest lookout signals with a red LED lamp. The LED emits light of wavelength 650 nm. Calculate the energy of each photon, which is approximately the band gap.

Show solution
  1. f = v / λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ (6.50 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.62 × 10¹⁴ Hz
  2. E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 4.62 × 10¹⁴ → E = 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Each electron needs about this much energy from the supply, and E = QV gives V = 3.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 1.9 V. That is roughly why red LEDs need about 2 V across them.

Solar cells

A solar cell is a p-n junction designed so that a potential difference is produced when photons are absorbed: the photovoltaic effect. The absorbed photons give electrons enough energy to rise from the valence band into the conduction band. The electric field in the junction pushes the electrons in the conduction band towards the n-type side (and the holes towards the p-type side), so a potential difference appears across the cell, which can drive a current.

⚠ Exam lookout

In 2025 only some candidates chose the correct statement about red and blue LEDs. Keep the chain straight: bigger band gap → higher-energy photons → higher frequency (bluer light) → larger forward p.d. needed. And use the band words precisely: electrons move in the conduction band and fall into holes in the valence band.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Explain conductors, insulators and semiconductors with band theory.
  • Describe n-type and p-type doping, and forward and reverse bias of a p-n junction.
  • Explain how an LED and a solar cell work, and use E = hf with the band gap.
