The quarterdeck · exam drill

Beat to quarters: the question paper

You know the physics. This page is about turning it into marks: how the paper is built, what each command word wants, how calculations are marked, how to tackle the open-ended questions, and what is on the sheets you are given. It finishes with a mixed mock drill.

1 · The paper at a glance

2 h 30 minfor both sections together
130 marksabout 1 minute 9 seconds per mark
20 MCSection 1: 20 questions, options A to E
110 marksSection 2: structured and open-ended questions
  • Section 1: aim for about 25 minutes. Work each question out on the rough-work pages before you look at the options: the wrong options are usually the answers you get from the most common mistakes.
  • Section 2: about 2 hours. Questions are set in a context (a satellite, a fairground ride, a medical scanner) and often mix topics. Every part tells you its marks: a 3-mark calculation needs three steps; a 1-mark "state" needs a word or a phrase.
  • There are usually two open-ended questions (3 marks each) and at least one question about an experiment: describing it, analysing data, or suggesting improvements.
  • You get a data sheet and a relationships sheet. Know what's on them so you never waste time hunting.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Candidates continued to find questions testing recall of facts and definitions challenging." Learn the definition boxes on this site word for word. And if a question asks for a set number of answers (say, two improvements), give exactly that many: extra answers that are wrong can cancel correct ones (general marking principle 21).

2 · Command words

Command wordWhat a full answer does
State, name, give, identifyA short answer is enough: a word, a phrase, a value. No explanation needed.
DescribeGive the features or the steps (for an experiment: apparatus, what you measure and with what, how you get the result).
ExplainLink cause and effect: "…because…, so…". Use the physics words and, where it helps, a relationship.
Calculate, determineGet a number from the information given. Show relationship, substitution and answer with unit.
Show thatUsually 2 marks: 1 for the relationship and 1 for the substitution. Then write the final line, including the value you were given: without it you get at most 1 mark. A bare answer gets nothing.
Sketch, drawSketch: the right shape, with labelled axes (and units) and key values marked; no grid or exact plotting needed. Draw: an accurate diagram or graph, with a ruler for straight lines.
EstimateFind an approximate value, often from a graph.
JustifyGive the reasons for your answer: often "relationship + the effect of changing a variable".
PredictSay what will happen, based on the information given.
SuggestApply physics to a new situation. Several answers can earn the mark if they are physically sound.
Use your knowledge of physics to comment on…An open-ended question: see section 4.

3 · How calculations are marked: the "standard three-marker"

Most calculations are worth 3 marks: 1 for the relationship, 1 for the substitution, 1 for the final answer with its unit. A correct final answer with its unit on its own gets all 3 (except in "show that" questions), but working protects you if you slip.

"Show that" questions are different. They are usually worth 2 marks: 1 for the relationship and 1 for the substitution. The answer is given, so it earns nothing on its own, but you must still write the final line (for example "v = 6.3 m s⁻¹"): if it is missing you get a maximum of 1 mark.

  • The relationship mark is for writing the correct relationship, like V = IR. A "magic triangle" on its own gets nothing.
  • Writing down several relationships without choosing one gets nothing.
  • A wrong answer carried into the next part is marked "follow-on": you can still get full marks for the next part if you use it correctly.
  • Rounding: the final mark is lost if your answer has more than two figures more, or more than one figure fewer, than the data with the fewest significant figures.
  • Spelling is forgiven unless it makes the word ambiguous: "defraction" (reflection? refraction? diffraction?) or "fussion" (fission? fusion?) get no marks.

Try marking these answers yourself. The question: The current in a resistor is 1.5 A when the potential difference across it is 7.5 V. Calculate the resistance of the resistor. (3 marks; correct answer 5.0 Ω)

5.0 Ω
V = IR, 7.5 = 1.5R, R = 5.0
R = V/I = 7.5/1.5 = 4.0 Ω
R = V/I = 1.5/7.5 = 5.0 Ω
R = I/V = 1.5/7.5 = 0.2 Ω
4.0 Ω

4 · Open-ended questions

Two questions (usually 3 marks each) begin "Use your knowledge of physics to comment on…". There is no single right answer. Markers award marks for the depth and breadth of your physics:

MarksThe answer shows…
3A good understanding: the right physics, clearly applied to the situation, with correct statements of principles and/or relationships. Not necessarily long or covering everything.
2A reasonable understanding: at least one relevant piece of physics applied correctly to the situation.
1A limited understanding: some relevant physics, but not really applied, or with errors.
0No relevant physics, or only general (non-physics) comments. Merely restating the physics given in the question also scores 0.

A recipe that works

  1. Spend a couple of minutes thinking: which areas of physics could this touch? Write a list in the margin.
  2. Pick one or two and go deep: name the principle (conservation of momentum, the inverse square law…), quote a relationship, and apply it to the exact situation, with numbers if they help.
  3. Add a second idea only if you are sure it's correct. Wrong physics can drag the mark down.
  4. Diagrams and sketch graphs count if they are labelled.

Practice · "a heavier cannonball would go further"

Cadet Sergeant Rahman says: "If the gun fires a heavier cannonball at the same speed, it will travel further, because heavy things fall more slowly through the air." Use your knowledge of physics to comment on this statement. (3 marks)

Show the kind of physics a good answer uses
  • Projectile motion with no air resistance: the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Every ball has the same vertical acceleration, g = 9.8 m s⁻², whatever its mass (F = ma with F = mg gives a = g). So with the same launch speed and angle, every ball takes the same time and travels the same range. Mass makes no difference.
  • With air resistance: for two balls of the same size, the air resistance at the same speed is the same, but the heavier ball's deceleration (a = F/m) is smaller. So the heavier ball does keep its speed better and goes slightly further. The cadet is right about the result, but for the wrong reason: heavy things don't "fall more slowly"; they are slowed less by the air.
  • Momentum and the gun: but would the gun really fire a heavier ball at the same speed? The same charge of powder gives the ball about the same energy, so by Ek = ½mv², a heavier ball leaves more slowly. And by conservation of momentum, the gun would recoil harder.

Any one of these, explained clearly with its relationship and applied to the cannonball, is a 3-mark answer. You do not need all three.

6 · The relationships sheet

7 · Mock drill

A full Section 1: 20 mixed multiple-choice questions. Start the timer and aim to finish in 25 minutes, working each one out before you look at the options. Then try the structured questions, which are marked like Section 2. For a complete practice paper, use the specimen question paper and its marking instructions on the Qualifications Scotland website.

✔ On the day
  • Read the whole question, including the stem above part (a): information there is needed later.
  • Underline what is actually asked: photon energy or electron energy? Angle to the normal or to the surface?
  • Convert prefixes and units before substituting. Check the powers of ten on graph axes.
  • Use a ruler for every straight line, and write clearly: markers can only mark what they can read.
  • If you are stuck, write down the relevant relationship: it is often a mark on its own.
