The signal deck & the lantern room · Particles and Waves (P&W)

Lanterns, lightning and the smallest things there are

Particles and Waves takes you from the charges inside a particle accelerator to the light from the Sun. Halfway through, it asks a strange question: is light a wave or a stream of particles? The answer is "both", and you will see the evidence for each.

⚓ Ship's log: Flight Lieutenant Murray

"Every signal this ship sends at night goes by lantern, and every lantern is a lesson. Its light thins out with distance by an exact rule. Split it with a prism or a grating and it tells you what it's made of. Shine it on the right metal and it knocks electrons clean out. Up here we read light the way the purser reads a ledger."

1 · Charges in electric fields P&W · forces on charged particles

A charged particle feels a force in an electric field. Electric fields exist around charged particles and between charged plates. We draw them with field lines: the direction of each line is the direction of the force on a small positive charge, and the closer the lines, the stronger the field.

⚠ Sketching field patterns

Markers check four things: lines start on positive and end on negative charges, arrows show the direction, lines never cross, and lines meet a charge's surface at right angles. For parallel plates the lines must be evenly spaced and parallel: use a ruler.

Direction of movement: a positive charge is pushed along the field lines (towards the negative plate). A negative charge, such as an electron, is pushed the opposite way (towards the positive plate).

Potential difference and the energy of a charge

📘 Potential difference

The potential difference (voltage) between two points is the work done in moving one coulomb of charge between those points. 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb.

So when a charge Q moves through a potential difference V, the work done on it is:

W = QV

If the charge starts at rest and nothing else takes energy away (as in a vacuum), all of that work becomes kinetic energy: QV = ½mv².

The electron gun: a charge between parallel plates

Choose a particle and a voltage. The particle accelerates uniformly across the gap and arrives with kinetic energy QV. A proton gains the same energy as an electron but ends up far slower: it is about 1840 times more massive.

Worked example · an electron gun

Electrons are accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Calculate (a) the kinetic energy gained by each electron and (b) its final speed.

Show solution
  1. (a) W = QV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10³ → W = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ J
  2. (b) Ek = ½mv² → 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ = ½ × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × v²
  3. v = 2.7 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹

Charge and mass of the electron are on the data sheet. Use the magnitude of the charge here: you are finding an energy, not a direction.

2 · Charges in magnetic fields, and particle accelerators P&W · forces on charged particles

A moving charge produces a magnetic field: that is why a current in a wire makes a compass needle swing. And a charge moving through a magnetic field feels a force. The force is at right angles both to the field and to the direction of motion.

Finding the direction of the force

Positive charges: left hand

Hold your left hand with the thumb and first two fingers at right angles.

  • First finger: the Field (north to south)
  • seCond finger: the direction the positive charge moves (Current)
  • thuMb: the force, so the Motion it causes

Negative charges: right hand

Use your right hand with exactly the same fingers.

  • First finger: the Field
  • seCond finger: the direction the electron moves (electron flow)
  • thuMb: the force

Either way, a negative charge is pushed the opposite way to a positive charge moving in the same direction. On diagrams, × means a field going into the page (you see the tail of the arrow) and • means a field coming out of the page (you see its point).

Steering the beam: a charge in a magnetic field

Switch between a positive and a negative charge and check the hand rule against the simulation. A stronger field bends the path more tightly.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

Many candidates found the right direction for the force on a proton in a magnetic field. The usual slip is using the wrong hand for the type of charge. Say out loud "positive: left; negative: right" before you lift your hand.

Particle accelerators

Accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider at CERN use all three ideas from this chapter:

  1. Electric fields accelerate the charged particles. Each pass through an accelerating gap does work QV on them.
  2. Magnetic fields deflect them, bending the beam into a circle (or focusing it), so it can pass the accelerating gaps again and again. As the particles gain energy the magnets must be made stronger to keep them on the same path.
  3. Beams are steered into high-energy collisions. Some of the kinetic energy becomes mass (E = mc²), creating new particles, which detectors track and identify.

A ring accelerator, block by block

Two beams circle in opposite directions. Red block: the electric field that speeds them up. Blue blocks: the bending magnets. Gold: the detector, where the beams collide once they reach full energy.

3 · The Standard Model P&W · the Standard Model

The Standard Model is our model of the fundamental particles (particles not made of anything smaller) and the interactions between them. It is the most precisely tested theory in science.

Orders of magnitude

Physics covers an astonishing range of sizes. An order of magnitude is a factor of ten: an atom (10⁻¹⁰ m) is five orders of magnitude bigger than a nucleus (10⁻¹⁵ m).

From quark to cosmos

Slide through 44 orders of magnitude, from the sub-nuclear to the furthest known celestial objects.

The particles

FamilyMembersNotes
Fermions: the matter particlesQuarks (six types: up, down, strange, charm, top, bottom) and leptons (electron, muon and tau, each with its own neutrino)Quarks have fractional charges: +⅔ (up, charm, top) or −⅓ (down, strange, bottom). Quarks are never found alone.
Bosons: the force-mediating particlesPhotons (electromagnetic force), W and Z bosons (weak force), gluons (strong force)Forces act by exchanging these particles. Gravity is not part of the Standard Model.

Hadrons: particles made of quarks

  • Hadrons are composite particles made of quarks.
  • Baryons are made of three quarks. The proton is uud (charge ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1) and the neutron is udd (⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0).
  • Mesons are made of a quark–antiquark pair. For example, the positive pion π⁺ is an up quark and an anti-down quark (⅔ + ⅓ = +1).

The quark crafting table

Combine quarks (dark) and antiquarks (pale) to craft hadrons. The table checks the charge and tells you what you have made.

Evidence for the model

  • Quarks: in high-energy collisions between electrons and nucleons (protons and neutrons) in particle accelerators, some electrons bounce off at large angles, as though they had hit small hard objects inside the nucleon. Those objects are quarks.
  • Antimatter: every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass but opposite charge (the positron is the anti-electron). When a particle meets its antiparticle they annihilate, turning their mass into energy, usually as gamma-ray photons. Detecting the energy from annihilation is evidence that antimatter exists. PET scanners in hospitals use exactly this.
  • The neutrino: in beta decay a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, an electron (the beta particle) and an antineutrino:

    ¹₀n → ¹₁p + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑ

    In the 1920s physicists found that the beta electrons came out with a range of energies, rather than the single energy expected if only two particles were produced. Energy seemed to be going missing. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed a new, almost undetectable, neutral particle carrying away the missing energy. Beta decay was the first evidence for the neutrino, which was finally detected in 1956.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Only some candidates could state the conclusion drawn from studies of beta decay, despite this being mandatory content." The answer they wanted: beta decay provided the first evidence for the existence of the neutrino (because energy appeared to be missing). Many candidates did well on what a fundamental particle is (one that cannot be broken down into smaller particles), that baryons are three quarks, and on working out a quark's charge.

4 · Nuclear reactions and E = mc² P&W · nuclear reactions

A nucleus is written AZX: A is the mass number (protons + neutrons) and Z the atomic number (protons). In every nuclear equation, the mass numbers on each side add up to the same total, and so do the atomic numbers.

ProcessExampleWhat happens
Alpha decay²²⁶₈₈Ra → ²²²₈₆Rn + ⁴₂HeAn alpha particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons) leaves: A falls by 4, Z by 2.
Beta decay¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e + ν̄ₑA neutron becomes a proton: A unchanged, Z rises by 1.
Gamma emission(after alpha or beta decay)An excited nucleus gives out energy as a gamma photon: A and Z unchanged.
Spontaneous fissiona heavy nucleus splits by itselfA large unstable nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei and some neutrons, with no trigger.
Induced fission²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3 ¹₀nA neutron is absorbed and makes the nucleus split. The released neutrons can trigger further fissions: a chain reaction.
Fusion²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀nTwo light nuclei join to make a heavier one. This powers the Sun.

Where the energy comes from

In fission and fusion, the total mass of the products is slightly less than the total mass of what went in. The lost mass has been converted into energy, mostly the kinetic energy of the products:

E = mc²

Here m is the mass lost (in kg) and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Because c² is enormous (9 × 10¹⁶), a tiny mass gives a huge amount of energy.

The purser's mass ledger

Watch the reaction, then compare the mass columns. The bars are zoomed in on their very tops so you can see the mass that goes missing.

Worked example · energy from one fusion

Calculate the energy released when deuterium and tritium fuse: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n.
Masses: ²H 3.344 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; ³H 5.007 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; ⁴He 6.645 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; n 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

Show solution
  1. Mass before = 3.344 + 5.007 = 8.351 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
  2. Mass after = 6.645 + 1.675 = 8.320 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
  3. Mass lost m = 0.031 × 10⁻²⁷ = 3.1 × 10⁻²⁹ kg
  4. E = mc² = 3.1 × 10⁻²⁹ × (3.00 × 10⁸)² → E = 2.8 × 10⁻¹² J

Keep all the figures until you subtract: the mass difference is tiny, so rounding the masses first can wipe it out.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

Most candidates calculated the energy correctly, but "only some candidates explained that mass was being converted to energy". If you are asked why energy is released, say: the total mass after the reaction is less than the total mass before; the lost mass is converted into energy (E = mc²).

Fusion reactors

To fuse, two nuclei must collide fast enough to overcome the electric repulsion between their positive charges. That needs temperatures of over 100 million kelvin. At these temperatures the fuel is a plasma: a gas of charged particles (nuclei and free electrons). No material container could hold it, so the plasma is contained by magnetic fields, which push on the moving charges and keep them away from the walls. This is the job of a tokamak, such as JET in Oxfordshire or ITER in France.

5 · Irradiance and the inverse square law P&W · inverse square law

📘 Irradiance

Irradiance is the power per unit area incident on a surface: I = P / A, measured in W m⁻².

Light from a small point source spreads out in all directions. At twice the distance the same power is spread over four times the area, so the irradiance is a quarter. At three times the distance it is a ninth. Irradiance is inversely proportional to the square of the distance:

I = kd²

k is a constant for a given source.

I₁d₁² = I₂d₂²

The quick way to compare two distances.

The signal lantern and the light meter

Move the meter. The inset shows the patch of light growing: at 2 m it covers 4 squares, at 3 m it covers 9. Then switch the graph to I against 1/d².

Experiment: verifying the inverse square law

  1. In a darkened room, set up a small lamp (as close to a point source as possible) and a light meter.
  2. Measure the distance d from the lamp's filament to the meter with a metre stick, and record the irradiance I.
  3. Repeat for a range of distances (for example 0.20 m to 1.00 m in steps), taking repeated readings at each.
  4. Plot a graph of I against 1/d². A straight line through the origin shows that I ∝ 1/d². (Alternatively, show that I × d² is constant for every result.)
⚠ From the 2025 course report

Asked to suggest two improvements to this experiment, "a number of candidates gave a response that involved darkening the room, despite the question stating that the experiment was carried out in a darkened room", and others gave extra answers that were wrong, which cancels correct ones. Many also forgot to state the relationship after using the data: finish with "so I ∝ 1/d²" or "I × d² is constant". And when asked why the law did not hold for a laser, only some said that a laser is not a point source: its beam does not spread out in all directions.

Worked example · how far can the lantern be seen?

The irradiance from a lantern is 0.80 W m⁻² at 2.0 m. Calculate the irradiance at 5.0 m.

Show solution
  1. I₁d₁² = I₂d₂²
  2. 0.80 × 2.0² = I₂ × 5.0²
  3. I₂ = 0.13 W m⁻² (0.128)

Worked example · a laser beam

A laser beam has a circular cross-section of diameter 4.0 mm. Its irradiance is 1.6 × 10³ W m⁻². Calculate its power.

Show solution
  1. Area of the beam = πr² = π × (2.0 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁵ m². (Radius, not diameter, and in metres.)
  2. I = P / A → 1.6 × 10³ = P / 1.26 × 10⁻⁵ → P = 0.020 W (20 mW)

6 · Wave–particle duality: the photoelectric effect P&W · wave-particle duality

Shine ultraviolet light on a clean zinc plate and electrons fly off it. This is photoemission, the photoelectric effect. Shine bright red light on the same plate, as bright as you like, and nothing happens at all. A wave model of light cannot explain that. A particle model can.

In the particle model, light is a stream of photons, packets of energy. The energy of one photon depends only on the frequency of the light:

E = hf

where h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant. Each photon gives all of its energy to one electron.

📘 Definitions

The threshold frequency f₀ is the minimum frequency of a photon required for photoemission.

The work function hf₀ of a material is the minimum energy of a photon required to cause photoemission.

If a photon has more energy than the work function, the extra becomes the kinetic energy of the electron. The fastest electrons (those that lose nothing on the way out) have:

Ek = hf − hf₀   and   Ek = ½mv²

Use v = fλ (with v = c) when you are given a wavelength instead of a frequency.

Photon bombardment

Lower the frequency below the threshold, then turn the irradiance right up. Then raise the frequency and change the irradiance again: watch what changes and what doesn't.

ChangeNumber of electrons per secondMaximum kinetic energy of each electron
Increase irradiance (above f₀)increases: more photons per secondno change: each photon still has energy hf
Increase frequency (above f₀)no change (same number of photons per second)increases: each photon has more energy

⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Few candidates explained why the photoelectric effect provides evidence supporting the particle model of light." A strong answer: photoemission only happens above a threshold frequency, however great the irradiance. This shows that light energy arrives in packets (photons) whose energy depends on frequency, E = hf. A single photon must have enough energy to free an electron. Also: moving a lamp further away changes the irradiance, so it changes the number of electrons, not their maximum kinetic energy. Many candidates got that right but could not justify it.

Worked example · ultraviolet on zinc

Ultraviolet light of frequency 1.20 × 10¹⁵ Hz falls on a zinc plate. The work function of zinc is 6.9 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Calculate (a) the energy of each photon, (b) the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons and (c) their maximum speed.

Show solution
  1. (a) E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 1.20 × 10¹⁵ → E = 7.96 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  2. (b) Ek = hf − hf₀ = 7.96 × 10⁻¹⁹ − 6.9 × 10⁻¹⁹ → Ek = 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  3. (c) 1.06 × 10⁻¹⁹ = ½ × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × v² → v = 4.8 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹
⚠ Read the question

In 2025 a multiple-choice question asked for the energy of each photon, and some candidates calculated the maximum kinetic energy of the electron instead. Underline what is being asked for.

7 · Interference and diffraction gratings P&W · interference

If the photoelectric effect is evidence that light is a particle, interference is evidence that it is a wave. Only waves can add together to cancel out. Interference is evidence for the wave model of light.

📘 Coherent waves

Coherent waves have a constant phase relationship: the same frequency, and a phase difference that never changes. Only coherent sources give a steady interference pattern.

  • Constructive interference (a maximum): waves meet in phase, crest on crest. The amplitudes add.
  • Destructive interference (a minimum): waves meet exactly out of phase (phase difference 180°), crest on trough. They cancel.

Whether two waves arrive in phase depends on the path difference: how much further one wave has travelled than the other. For two sources in phase:

path difference = mλ

Maximum (constructive). m = 0, 1, 2, …

path difference = (m + ½)λ

Minimum (destructive): an odd number of half-wavelengths.

Two bells in the harbour: a ripple tank

Bright and dark ripples mark the waves; the calm grey bands are the minima. Drag the white ring and read the path difference. Change the wavelength or the separation and watch the bands move.

Worked example · path difference

Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound of wavelength 0.40 m. A microphone is 5.0 m from one speaker and 5.6 m from the other. Is it at a maximum or a minimum, and of which order?

Show solution
  1. Path difference = 5.6 − 5.0 = 0.6 m
  2. In wavelengths: 0.6 ÷ 0.40 = 1.5λ = (1 + ½)λ
  3. A minimum (the second minimum away from the centre: m = 1 in (m + ½)λ).

The diffraction grating

A diffraction grating is a slide ruled with hundreds of fine, equally spaced slits per millimetre. Light diffracts through every slit, and the waves from all the slits interfere. Bright maxima appear only at angles where the path difference between neighbouring slits is a whole number of wavelengths:

d sin θ = mλ

  • d is the grating spacing, the distance between neighbouring slits, in metres. If you are given N lines per millimetre, d = 1 / (N × 1000) m.
  • θ is the angle between the straight-through direction and the maximum; m is the order (0, 1, 2…).

Splitting the lantern light: a grating

Change the number of lines per millimetre: more lines means smaller d, so the maxima spread further apart and fewer orders fit. Then try white light.

Worked example · a laser and a grating

Red light of wavelength 633 nm shines on a grating with 300 lines per millimetre. Calculate the angle to the second-order maximum.

Show solution
  1. d = 1 / (300 × 10³) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m
  2. d sin θ = mλ → 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ × sin θ = 2 × 633 × 10⁻⁹
  3. sin θ = 0.380 → θ = 22° (22.3°)

White light and gratings

  • The central maximum is white: at θ = 0 the path difference is zero for every wavelength, so all the colours arrive in phase and combine to make white light.
  • Each other order is a spectrum. Since sin θ = mλ/d, longer wavelengths are deviated more: red is deviated more than violet, the opposite way round to a prism.
  • A grating with fewer lines per millimetre has a larger d, so the spectra are squashed closer together, which makes it harder to pick out a particular colour.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

Many candidates lost marks converting lines per millimetre into lines per metre. "Few candidates explained why the central maximum is white", and few could explain that a grating with fewer lines per millimetre puts the colours closer together. Some "stated that having fewer lines-per-millimetre on the grating meant that the lines on the grating were closer together", which is backwards.

8 · Spectra and the Bohr model P&W · spectra

In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed a model of the atom in which electrons can only have certain fixed energies, called energy levels. An electron cannot sit between levels.

📘 Bohr model words
  • Ground state: the lowest energy level an electron can occupy.
  • Energy levels: the allowed energies. Electrons in higher levels are excited.
  • Zero potential energy: the energy an electron has when it has just been removed from the atom. Because of this choice, every energy level inside the atom is negative.
  • Ionisation: giving an electron enough energy to escape from the atom completely (reaching 0 J).

When an electron drops from a higher level E₂ to a lower level E₁ it emits one photon whose energy equals the difference. When it absorbs a photon of exactly that energy, it jumps up:

E₂ − E₁ = hf

Hydrogen's energy ladder

Pick a transition. Falls to n = 2 give hydrogen's visible lines (the Balmer series): try 3 → 2, 4 → 2, 5 → 2 and 6 → 2. Falls to n = 1 are ultraviolet. Then switch to absorption.

SpectrumLooks likeHow it is made
Line emissionbright coloured lines on a dark backgroundA hot, low-pressure gas. Excited electrons fall to lower levels, each emitting a photon of energy E₂ − E₁. Only certain energy differences exist, so only certain frequencies are emitted: a pattern unique to each element.
Continuous emissionall colours, with no gapsA hot solid, liquid or dense gas (a lamp filament, a star's surface). The atoms are so close together that their energy levels merge, so electron transitions of every energy are possible and photons of every frequency are emitted.
Absorptiona continuous spectrum crossed by dark linesWhite light passes through a cooler gas. Electrons in the gas absorb photons of exactly the frequencies that match their energy-level differences and jump up. Those frequencies are missing from the light that comes through (the re-emitted photons go off in all directions), leaving dark lines in exactly the places where that element's emission lines would be.

The dark Fraunhofer lines in the spectrum of sunlight are absorption lines. The Sun's hot surface gives a continuous spectrum, and the cooler gases in its outer atmosphere absorb particular frequencies. Matching the lines with laboratory spectra tells us which elements are in the Sun's outer atmosphere. Helium was discovered this way, in the Sun, before it was found on Earth.

Worked example · hydrogen's red line

In a hydrogen atom, E₃ = −2.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and E₂ = −5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron falls from E₃ to E₂.

Show solution
  1. E₃ − E₂ = −2.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ − (−5.45 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
  2. E = hf → f = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz
  3. v = fλ → λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 4.57 × 10¹⁴ → λ = 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m (656 nm: red, as on the data sheet)
⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Few candidates explained the production of absorption lines in the Sun's spectra correctly. A number of candidates answered in terms of emission spectra", having learned a past-paper answer. And when asked which transition produced a line, many gave no direction or the wrong one: emission is a fall (higher → lower); absorption is a rise (lower → higher). Say which levels, and which way.

9 · Refraction of light P&W · refraction of light

Light slows down when it passes from air into glass or water, and if it meets the surface at an angle, it changes direction: refraction. Its frequency does not change (the source sets that), so its wavelength gets shorter in proportion to its speed.

📘 Absolute refractive index

The absolute refractive index n of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to the speed of light in the medium: n = c / v. It has no unit, and it is always greater than 1. (Air is 1.00 to three significant figures.)

All angles are measured from the normal, the line at right angles to the surface. For light passing between air (or a vacuum) and a medium:

n = sin θ₁sin θ₂

where θ₁ is the angle in air and θ₂ the angle in the medium. More generally, between any two media 1 and 2:

sin θ₁sin θ₂ = λ₁λ₂ = v₁v₂

The ray bends towards the normal entering a slower (optically denser) medium and away from it leaving one.

The navigator's prism: a semicircular block

First send the ray into the block and check sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ = n. Then send it out of the block and raise the angle past the critical angle.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Many candidates used the value for the angle the ray of light made with the glass/air interface rather than using the angle between the ray and the normal." If a diagram gives the angle to the surface, subtract it from 90°.

Worked example · into crown glass

Red light (wavelength 633 nm in air) enters crown glass (n = 1.50) at an angle of incidence of 40°. Calculate (a) the angle of refraction, (b) the speed of the light in the glass, (c) its wavelength in the glass.

Show solution
  1. (a) n = sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ → 1.50 = sin 40° / sin θ₂ → θ₂ = 25° (25.4°)
  2. (b) n = c / v → v = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 1.50 → v = 2.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹
  3. (c) λ₁ / λ₂ = n → λ₂ = 633 / 1.50 → λ₂ = 422 nm. The frequency is unchanged; the light is still red.

Refractive index depends on frequency

The refractive index of a medium increases as the frequency of the light increases. Violet light (high frequency) is slowed and bent more than red light. That is why a prism splits white light into a spectrum: dispersion. Tick "show red and violet separately" in the simulation to see it.

Critical angle and total internal reflection

When light passes from a denser medium into a less dense one (glass to air), it bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray swings closer and closer to the surface.

📘 Critical angle

The critical angle is the angle of incidence that produces an angle of refraction of 90°. When the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, total internal reflection occurs: all the light is reflected back into the medium.

sin θc = 1n

For crown glass, θc = sin⁻¹(1/1.50) = 41.8°. For diamond (n = 2.42), θc is only 24.4°, so light that enters a cut diamond bounces around inside it many times before it escapes: that is the sparkle. Optical fibres use total internal reflection to carry light signals for kilometres.

Experiment: determining the refractive index of a medium

  1. Place a semicircular (or rectangular) block on paper and draw round it. Draw the normal at the centre of the flat face.
  2. Aim a narrow ray from a ray box at the centre of the flat face at a measured angle of incidence θ₁. Mark the ray where it leaves the block, then draw the refracted ray.
  3. Measure the angle of refraction θ₂ from the normal with a protractor.
  4. Repeat for a range of angles of incidence.
  5. Plot a graph of sin θ₁ against sin θ₂. It is a straight line through the origin, and its gradient is the refractive index n.

With a semicircular block, aim the ray at the centre of the flat face: the ray then crosses the curved face along a radius, so it does not bend there and only the refraction at the flat face is measured.
