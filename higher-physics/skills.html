The chart room · units, uncertainties, graphs and the assignment

Measure twice, plot once

Every number in physics comes with a unit, a sensible number of significant figures and an uncertainty. These skills are tested all through the question paper and earn a large share of the assignment's 20 marks, so they are worth learning properly.

⚓ Ship's log: Captain Blackwood

"A navigator who writes down a position with no idea how wrong it might be will put this ship on the rocks. Every reading has an error. Know how big it is, and say so."

1 · Units, prefixes, standard form and significant figures Units, prefixes and uncertainties

Standard form (scientific notation) writes a number as a value between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten: 0.000 47 = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴. On a calculator use the ×10ˣ (or EXP) key, not "× 10 ^".

Significant figures

The final answer can have no more significant figures than the data value with the fewest significant figures. So 2.4 m ÷ 0.0315 s = 76 m s⁻¹, not 76.19047…, because 2.4 has only two significant figures. Markers accept answers from one figure fewer to two figures more than that, but a long string of calculator digits loses the mark. Don't round in the middle of a calculation: keep the full value in your calculator until the end.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

Examiners asked candidates not to copy down long calculator answers or ellipses ("…") as a step before the final answer, because it causes transcription errors. Show the relationship, the substitution, then the answer with its unit, rounded sensibly. And watch the powers of ten on a graph's axis: many candidates calculated a gradient but ignored the "× 10⁻³" written on the axis label.

2 · Uncertainties Units, prefixes and uncertainties

Every measurement is uncertain. You need three kinds of uncertainty, and you can express each as an absolute value (± 0.02 s) or a percentage (± 0.8%).

TypeWhat it isHow to deal with it
Scale-reading uncertaintyHow precisely the instrument's scale can be read.Usually ± half the smallest division on an analogue scale (a ruler marked in mm gives ± 0.5 mm), and ± 1 in the last digit on a digital display (a meter reading 2.36 V gives ± 0.01 V). Use a more precise instrument to reduce it.
Random uncertaintyThe scatter when a measurement is repeated: slight variations in reaction time, release, reading.Take repeated readings and use the mean. More repeats reduce its effect. Approximate random uncertainty = (max − min) ÷ number of readings.
Systematic uncertaintyAll the readings are too large, or all too small, because of the technique or the design of the experiment: a meter that reads 0.1 V with nothing connected, measuring a length from the wrong end of a ruler.Repeating does not help: the mean is simply offset. It often shows up as a best-fit line that misses the origin when theory says it should pass through it. Fix the method or correct for the offset.
⚓ Dead reckoning in the chart room

With no stars to steer by, a navigator plots the ship's position from speed, heading and time. Every one of those readings is a little uncertain, so the pencil mark on the chart is drawn as a circle, not a dot, and it grows the further you sail. The navigator's question is the one you will ask in every experiment: which reading is the least certain? Improve that one and the circle shrinks the most.

random uncertainty = max. value − min. valuenumber of values

The mean of repeated readings is the best estimate of the true value. Quote the final result as value ± uncertainty, for example (2.36 ± 0.02) s.

Which uncertainty matters most?

When an experiment measures several quantities, find the percentage uncertainty in each. The largest one dominates, and is a good estimate of the percentage uncertainty in the final result.

Worked example · the dominant uncertainty

A student measures the speed of a trolley: distance (0.800 ± 0.001) m, time (2.36 ± 0.02) s. Calculate the speed with its uncertainty.

Show solution
  1. Speed = 0.800 ÷ 2.36 = 0.339 m s⁻¹
  2. % uncertainty in distance = 0.001 ÷ 0.800 × 100 = 0.13%. % uncertainty in time = 0.02 ÷ 2.36 × 100 = 0.85%.
  3. Time dominates, so the speed is uncertain by about 0.85%: 0.85% of 0.339 = 0.003 m s⁻¹.
  4. Speed = (0.339 ± 0.003) m s⁻¹. To improve the experiment, improve the timing (light gates instead of a stopwatch), not the distance measurement.
✔ Accuracy, precision and reliability

You don't have to use these words, but if you do, use them correctly. Accurate: close to the true value. Precise: repeated readings close to each other (small random uncertainty). A result can be precise but inaccurate if there is a systematic uncertainty. Reliable: when the experiment is repeated (by you or someone else), it gives similar results. Repeated readings and a consistent method make a result more reliable. The 2025 examiners noted that candidates still mix these words up.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Identify scale-reading, random and systematic uncertainties, and say how to reduce each.
  • Calculate a mean and its random uncertainty, and express an uncertainty as absolute or percentage.
  • Identify the largest percentage uncertainty in an experiment.

3 · Graphs and analysis

A good graph earns marks in the paper and three marks in the assignment. Check every one of these:

  • Axes: the quantity you changed on the x-axis and the quantity you measured on the y-axis, each labelled with quantity and unit (V / V, I / A).
  • Scales: linear, easy to read (steps of 1, 2 or 5 units per division, never 3 or 7), and spreading the points over most of the grid. The origin does not have to be on the graph.
  • Points: plotted accurately as small crosses.
  • Line of best fit: a single straight line (or smooth curve, if the points curve) drawn with a ruler, with the points scattered evenly on both sides. Do not force it through the origin, and do not just join the first and last points.
  • Gradient: use two points on your line that are far apart (a large triangle), not two data points. Gradient = rise ÷ run, with units.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

In the paper's data-analysis question, few candidates drew a proper line of best fit. Some forced it through the origin, then claimed a systematic uncertainty because their line went through the origin. Few went on to use their gradient to find the quantity asked for. In assignments, some candidates averaged a value of g calculated from each separate data point, which is invalid. The right method is to find the gradient of the best-fit line and use that. And say "linear relationship" for any straight line; only say "directly proportional" if the line also passes through the origin.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Draw a graph with suitable scales, labels and units, and a line of best fit (not dot-to-dot, not forced through the origin).
  • Calculate a gradient from points on the line, with its unit, and use it to find a quantity.
  • Choose what to plot to get a straight line (for example I against 1/d²).

4 · The assignment: 20 marks, scaled to 33

The assignment is your own experimental investigation, written up as a report. It has two stages:

  1. Research (with some supervision): choose a topic with your teacher's guidance, carry out experimental work that gives measurements (you may work in a group), and find data or information from the internet, books or journals.
  2. Report (a high degree of supervision, at most 2 hours, no internet): write the report on your own. You may take in only the instructions for candidates, your experimental method, your raw data (tabulated, but with no extra columns for means or derived values), your internet/literature data and extracts, and a record of your sources. No drafts, no templates, no marking instructions.

The whole assignment should take no more than about 8 hours. It is marked by Qualifications Scotland. The rules on help (course specification, version 4.1):

  • Your teacher must offer a range of topics, agree your choice with you and advise on whether your aim is suitable.
  • Your teacher can give you a basic list of instructions for the experimental procedure.
  • Your teacher must not give you a list of websites (URLs), books or journals: you find your own sources, using only websites, journals and/or books.
  • Nobody may give you a template or model answers, and your teacher cannot give you feedback on your report or let you redraft it.

Planning an experiment

The question paper can ask you to plan or improve an experiment, and the assignment needs a clear method. A good plan covers:

  1. Aim: what relationship or value you are finding.
  2. Variables: the one you change (independent), the one you measure (dependent), and the ones you keep the same (controlled).
  3. Apparatus and measurements: name every instrument and what it measures. Choose a range wide enough, with at least five different values, and repeat each reading.
  4. Safety: name a real hazard and how you reduce it. For example: a hot lamp (let it cool before moving it), a laser (never look along the beam), mains equipment (check leads and keep water away), falling masses (keep feet clear, use a sand tray).
  5. Analysis: what you will plot to get a straight line, and what its gradient or intercept tells you.

Good Higher topics

Your teacher will offer a range of topics. Ones that work well at Higher, because they give a graph and a value to compare with literature, include: irradiance against distance (inverse square law); refractive index, or critical angle, of a block or liquid; wavelength of a laser using a grating; EMF and internal resistance of a cell; charging or discharging a capacitor through different resistors; the threshold voltage of different coloured LEDs (and an estimate of Planck's constant); a thermistor or LDR in a potential divider; acceleration down a slope at different angles; the Doppler effect for a moving sound source.

✔ Before the report stage
  • Raw data tabulated, with units and consistent precision, and the scale-reading uncertainty of each instrument written down.
  • Secondary data you can genuinely compare with your own, with full reference details and the date you accessed it.
  • An extract that explains the underlying physics (without sample calculations).
  • A brief summary of your method, naming every instrument.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • Plan an experiment: aim, variables, apparatus, range and repeats, safety measures, and analysis.
  • List what you may take into the report stage, and which help is and isn't allowed.
