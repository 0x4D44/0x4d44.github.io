The gun deck & the crow's nest · Our Dynamic Universe (ODU)

Shot, sail and the edge of the Universe

Our Dynamic Universe starts with a cannonball and ends with the Big Bang. On the way it covers how things move, why they move, what happens when they collide, how gravity holds a satellite up, why fast clocks run slow and how we know the Universe is expanding. It is the biggest area of the course, and every part of the question paper draws on it.

⚓ Ship's log: Master Gunner Kerr

"A gun crew that can't do arithmetic is a danger to the ship. How far will the shot carry? How hard will the gun kick? How long will the rope have to hold it? Every one of those is a sum, Cadets, and today you're going to learn them."

What you need from National 5

Speed = distance ÷ time, a = (v − u) / t, W = mg, F = ma, and energy as Ek and Ep. Scalars have size only. Vectors have size and direction. At Higher, directions matter in almost every calculation, so you must choose a positive direction and stick to it.

1 · Motion: the equations of motion ODU · motion — equations and graphs

The equations of motion describe anything moving in a straight line with a constant acceleration: an aircraft on its take-off run, a ball in flight, a trolley rolling down a slope. They use five quantities:

Which equation? The quartermaster's picker

Mark what you know and what you want. The picker shows the equation that skips the quantity you don't need, already rearranged.

Signs: pick a positive direction

Because s, u, v and a are vectors, they carry a sign. Choose one direction as positive, usually up or the direction of the initial motion, and write it down. Anything in the opposite direction is negative. For a shot fired straight up, with up as positive, u is positive and a = −9.8 m s⁻² all the way up and all the way down.

Where s = ut + ½at² comes from

Worked example · the Tutor's take-off run

A Grob Tutor accelerates uniformly from rest along the runway and reaches its take-off speed of 33 m s⁻¹ after travelling 290 m. Calculate (a) its acceleration and (b) the time the take-off run takes.

Show solution

Known: u = 0, v = 33 m s⁻¹, s = 290 m. Wanted: a, which leaves out t, so use v² = u² + 2as.

  1. (a) v² = u² + 2as
  2. 33² = 0² + 2 × a × 290
  3. Rearrange: 1089 = 580 a → a = 1089 ÷ 580
  4. a = 1.9 m s⁻² (the data have 2 significant figures; keep the unrounded 1.877 in your calculator)
  5. (b) v = u + at → 33 = 0 + 1.877 × t → t = 33 ÷ 1.877 → t = 18 s

Notice we carried the unrounded value of a (1.877) into part (b). Rounding to 1.9 first would give 17 s instead of 18 s, which a marker might not accept.

Worked example · a signal flare fired straight up

A flare is fired vertically upwards from the deck at 35 m s⁻¹. Ignore air resistance. Calculate (a) its maximum height above the deck and (b) how long it takes to come back down to deck level.

Show solution

Take up as positive: u = +35 m s⁻¹, a = −9.8 m s⁻². At the top, v = 0.

  1. (a) v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 35² + 2 × (−9.8) × s → 19.6 s = 35² → s = 1225 ÷ 19.6 → s = 63 m (unrounded: 62.5 m)
  2. (b) Back at deck level the displacement is zero: s = ut + ½at² → 0 = 35t + ½ × (−9.8) × t²
  3. So t(35 − 4.9t) = 0, giving t = 0 (the launch) or 35 − 4.9t = 0 → t = 35 ÷ 4.9 → t = 7.1 s.

Check: the trip up takes 35 ÷ 9.8 = 3.57 s, and the trip down takes the same, which gives 7.14 s, or 7.1 s, in total.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"At the very top the flare stops. If v = 0 there, isn't the acceleration zero too?"

Master Gunner Kerr: "No, and the examiners love this one. Acceleration is how fast the velocity is changing. A moment before the top the flare is going up; a moment after, it is coming down. The velocity is still changing at 9.8 m s⁻² every second, because gravity hasn't switched off. If a really were zero at the top, the flare would hang there for ever."

How answers are shown on this site: the bold answer is rounded to a sensible number of significant figures. Where it helps, the unrounded value follows in brackets as "(unrounded: …)". Carry the unrounded value into later parts.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • choose a positive direction and give every vector the right sign;
  • pick the equation of motion that leaves out the quantity you don't need, and rearrange it;
  • carry unrounded values between the parts of a question.

2 · Motion graphs ODU · motion — equations and graphs

Three graphs describe the same motion in different ways, and the exam expects you to move between them:

  • The gradient of a displacement–time graph is the velocity.
  • The gradient of a velocity–time graph is the acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement. Area below the time axis counts as negative displacement (motion in the negative direction).

For problems solved with the equations of motion, the acceleration is constant on each part of the graph, so the v–t graph is made of straight lines and the a–t graph of horizontal lines. The one curved v–t graph you must know is a falling object with air resistance (see terminal velocity).

Motion-graph lab: watch all three graphs grow together

Choose a scenario. The gold dashed line marks "now" on every graph. For the vertical motions, try switching the sign convention and see every graph flip upside down while the physics stays the same.

Thrown upwards and bouncing

Two shapes come up again and again, so know them cold:

  • Thrown upwards (up positive): the v–t graph is one straight line sloping down, crossing the time axis at the top of the flight. The a–t graph is a flat line at −9.8 m s⁻². The acceleration does not become zero at the top: only the velocity does.
  • Bouncing ball (up positive): the v–t graph is a saw-tooth. Each flight is a straight line with gradient −9.8 m s⁻². At each bounce the velocity jumps almost instantly from negative to positive, and the next line starts a bit lower because energy is lost in the bounce. With down as positive, the same graph appears upside down.
  • The s–t graphs follow from these. Thrown upwards, s–t is an upside-down curve: it rises, flattens at the top (gradient zero, so v = 0) and falls back. A ball dropped and bouncing makes a row of arches, each lower than the last, touching zero at every bounce, where the gradient flips sharply from negative to positive.

Displacement and distance from a v–t graph

  • Displacement after 5 s = +9 + (−4) = 5 m forwards of the start.
  • Distance travelled = 9 + 4 = 13 m, because distance is a scalar and ignores direction.
  • Acceleration = gradient = (−4 − 6) ÷ 5 = −2 m s⁻². The same value applies at every instant, including at t = 3 s when the trolley is momentarily at rest.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

In paper 1, question 2, "only some candidates identified the correct acceleration-time graph", and some selected the option that showed the line for the velocity–time graph. Read the axis labels before you read the shape.

Experiment: measuring the acceleration of a trolley down a slope

  1. Measure the length L of the card on the trolley with a ruler.
  2. Release the trolley from rest so that the card passes through both light gates.
  3. The timer records t1, the time the card takes to pass through gate 1, t2, the time it takes to pass through gate 2, and t3, the time taken to travel from gate 1 to gate 2.
  4. Calculate u = L / t₁ and v = L / t₂, then a = (v − u) / t₃.
  5. Repeat several times from the same release point and find the mean acceleration and its random uncertainty.

Alternatives that also earn the marks: a single light gate with a double-segment card (the timer measures the two segment times and the time between them), or a motion sensor connected to a computer, which plots a v–t graph whose gradient is the acceleration.

⚠ "Describe an experiment" questions

The course specification names this experiment, so you can be asked to describe it. A full answer names the apparatus, the measurements taken (with the instruments used to take them), and how the result is calculated. The 2025 examiners noted that some candidates "had little or no experience of practical work" and could not answer questions on the experiments listed in the course specification.

  • draw the a–t graph that matches a v–t graph (including thrown-up and bouncing objects);

3 · Forces, vectors and Newton's laws ODU · forces, energy and power

📘 Newton's laws

First law: an object stays at rest, or moves at a constant velocity in a straight line, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.

Second law: an unbalanced force causes an acceleration: F = ma, where F is the unbalanced (resultant) force.

Third law: if object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

Weight is the force of gravity on a mass: W = mg, with g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹ on Earth. (⚠ Same letter, different meaning: in section 4, W is also used for work done. See the symbol clash table.) Friction always acts to oppose the motion of a moving object. It turns kinetic energy into heat, so a moving object with no driving force slows down.

Adding vectors

Forces, velocities and displacements add nose to tail. If two forces act at right angles, use Pythagoras for the size of the resultant and trigonometry for its direction. (A scale drawing also earns the marks if it is accurate and has a stated scale.)

The resultant of 3.0 kN east and 4.0 kN north:

F = √(3.0² + 4.0²) = 5.0 kN

tan θ = 4.0 / 3.0 → θ = 53°

So the resultant is 5.0 kN at 53° north of east (a bearing of 037°). A vector answer needs both size and direction.

Resolving a vector into components

The reverse job: any vector can be split into two perpendicular components that together have the same effect.

A cadet drags a crate along the deck with a rope at 30° above the horizontal. The tension in the rope is 50 N.

Horizontal component = 50 cos 30° = 43 N (this pulls the crate along).
Vertical component = 50 sin 30° = 25 N (this partly lifts it, which reduces the push between crate and deck).

Use cos for the component next to the angle and sin for the one opposite it. Don't just learn "horizontal is cos": in the slope problems below, the angle sits differently.

Forces on a slope

For an object on a slope at angle θ, resolve its weight into two components:

  • parallel to the slope (down the slope): mg sin θ
  • normal (perpendicular) to the slope, into the surface: mg cos θ. This is balanced by the normal reaction force from the slope.

Then apply F = ma along the slope, with friction acting in the direction that opposes the motion.

The slipway: a block of oak on a slope

Change the angle, mass and friction. Then switch to "towed up the slope" and watch friction turn round.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

In a paper 1 question on a slope, "some candidates who did not arrive at the correct response had subtracted friction rather than adding it."

✔ Do this

When an object moves up a slope, friction acts down the slope, alongside mg sin θ, so the two add. Draw the free-body diagram first, every time.

Worked example · a crate slides down the slipway

A 12 kg crate is released on a slope at 20° to the horizontal. The friction force on it is 15 N. Calculate its acceleration.

Show solution
  1. Component of weight down the slope: mg sin θ = 12 × 9.8 × sin 20° = 40.2 N
  2. Unbalanced force down the slope: 40.2 − 15 = 25.2 N
  3. F = ma → 25.2 = 12 × a → a = 25.2 ÷ 12 → a = 2.1 m s⁻² down the slope

Tension: the powder hoist

Below decks, gunpowder is lifted from the magazine in a cage on a rope, like a lift on a cable. The cage has two forces on it: the tension T upwards and its weight mg downwards. Taking up as positive, the unbalanced force is T − mg, so:

T − mg = ma  →  T = m(g + a)

A 250 kg cage (mg = 2450 N), up taken as positive.

Worked example · two minecarts on a rope

A cadet pulls two coupled minecarts along a level track with a force of 270 N. The front cart has a mass of 40 kg and the rear cart 20 kg. Friction is 20 N on the front cart and 10 N on the rear cart. Calculate (a) the acceleration and (b) the tension in the coupling.

Show solution
  1. (a) Treat both carts as one object. Unbalanced force = 270 − (20 + 10) = 240 N.
  2. F = ma → 240 = 60 × a → a = 4.0 m s⁻²
  3. (b) Now look at the rear cart alone. The only forces along the track are the tension T forwards and friction 10 N backwards.
  4. T − 10 = 20 × 4.0 → T = 90 N

Check with the front cart: 270 − 20 − 90 = 160 N, and 40 × 4.0 = 160 N. ✔

Terminal velocity

Air resistance increases as an object moves faster. A falling object therefore goes through three stages, and a parachutist goes through two more when the canopy opens:

  1. At first, air resistance is small. The unbalanced force is nearly mg, so the acceleration is about 9.8 m s⁻².
  2. As it speeds up, air resistance increases, the unbalanced force decreases, so the acceleration decreases. The v–t graph curves and becomes less steep.
  3. Eventually air resistance equals the weight. The forces are balanced, so by Newton's first law the object moves at a constant velocity: its terminal velocity.
  4. The parachute opens. Its large area makes the air resistance suddenly much bigger than the weight. The unbalanced force is now upwards, so the parachutist decelerates (still moving downwards, but slowing). As the speed falls, air resistance falls too, so the deceleration decreases and the graph levels off.
  5. Air resistance falls until it equals the weight again. The forces are balanced once more, giving a new, much lower terminal velocity, slow enough to land safely.

Cadet parachute descent

Press Jump, wait for terminal velocity, then open the parachute. The forces and the v–t graph update as you fall. (This is an illustration: the cadet's weight is 784 N.)

⚠ Exam lookout

"Explain, in terms of forces, why the parachutist reaches terminal velocity" is a favourite. You need both ideas: air resistance increases with speed until it is equal to the weight, and the forces are then balanced, so the velocity is constant (Newton's first law). Never say the forces are balanced "so there is no motion".

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • add two perpendicular vectors and resolve one into components;
  • apply F = ma along a slope (mg sin θ) and in lift-and-rope problems (tension);
  • explain the stages of a parachute jump, and the shape of its v–t graph, in terms of forces.

4 · Work, energy and power ODU · forces, energy and power

Ew = Fd

Work done (J) by a force F (N) moving through distance d (m) in the direction of the force. Some books write W = Fd: ⚠ there W means work, not weight.

Ep = mgh

Gravitational potential energy gained when mass m is lifted through height h.

Ek = ½mv²

Kinetic energy. Doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy.

P = Et

Power (W): energy transferred per second.

The principle of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form into another. In problems, the energy you start with equals the energy you finish with plus any work done against friction (which ends up as heat).

Worked example · a minecart on a roller-coaster track

A minecart and its crew, total mass 450 kg, start from rest at the top of a track 12 m high. At the bottom they are moving at 14 m s⁻¹. The track is 80 m long. Calculate the average friction force.

Show solution
  1. Ep lost = mgh = 450 × 9.8 × 12 = 52 920 J
  2. Ek gained = ½mv² = ½ × 450 × 14² = 44 100 J
  3. Work done against friction = 52 920 − 44 100 = 8820 J
  4. Ew = Fd → 8820 = F × 80 → F = 110 N

Worked example · weighing anchor

The crew at the capstan raise the 1500 kg anchor through 20 m at a steady speed in 60 s. Calculate the useful power they produce.

Show solution
  1. At steady speed the work done goes into potential energy: Ep = mgh = 1500 × 9.8 × 20 = 294 000 J
  2. P = E / t = 294 000 / 60 → P = 4900 W (4.9 kW: about 50 sailors each producing roughly 100 W)
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • use Ew = Fd, Ep = mgh, Ek = ½mv² and P = E/t;
  • apply conservation of energy, including work done against friction;
  • state the principle of conservation of energy.

5 · Collisions, explosions and impulse ODU · collisions, explosions, and impulse

Momentum is mass × velocity: p = mv, measured in kg m s⁻¹. It is a vector: a minecart moving left has negative momentum if right is positive.

📘 Conservation of linear momentum

The total momentum before a collision (or explosion) is equal to the total momentum after it, in the absence of external forces.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

Asked to state this law, "a number of candidates did not state it was the total momentum that was conserved, and others did not mention the absence of external forces." Both are needed. Learn the box above word for word.

Elastic, inelastic and explosions

InteractionTotal momentumTotal kinetic energy
Elastic collisionconservedconserved
Inelastic collision (they may bounce apart or stick together)conserveddecreases: some becomes heat and sound
Explosion (objects start together and push apart)conserved (often zero before, so zero after)increases: stored energy (chemical, or in a spring) becomes kinetic

To show a collision is inelastic, calculate the total Ek before and after and show that it has decreased. Just saying "they stuck together" is not enough.

Minecart crash lab

Set the masses and velocities (right is positive), choose the type of interaction and watch the bars. Total momentum never changes. Kinetic energy only stays the same in the elastic case.

Worked example · head-on minecarts

Minecart A (400 kg) moves right at 4.0 m s⁻¹. It collides head-on with minecart B (200 kg) moving left at 2.0 m s⁻¹. They lock together. (a) Calculate their velocity after the collision. (b) Show that the collision is inelastic.

Show solution

Take right as positive, so B's velocity is −2.0 m s⁻¹.

  1. (a) Total momentum before = total momentum after
  2. mAuA + mBuB = (mA + mB)v
  3. (400 × 4.0) + (200 × −2.0) = 600 × v
  4. 1600 − 400 = 600v → v = 2.0 m s⁻¹ to the right
  5. (b) Ek before = ½ × 400 × 4.0² + ½ × 200 × 2.0² = 3200 + 400 = 3600 J
  6. Ek after = ½ × 600 × 2.0² = 1200 J. The total kinetic energy has decreased, so the collision is inelastic.

Forget the minus sign on B's velocity and you get 3.3 m s⁻¹: the most common way to lose these marks.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"The cannon is far heavier than the shot, so surely the shot gets more momentum?"

Master Gunner Kerr: "Weigh it up properly. Before firing, gun and shot are at rest: total momentum zero. The powder pushes them apart with equal and opposite forces for the same time, so they get equal and opposite momentum. The shot is light, so it needs a big speed to carry that momentum; the gun is heavy, so a small recoil speed does it. Equal momentum, very unequal speeds."

Newton's third law and the gun's recoil

When a gun fires, the expanding powder gases push the shot forwards and push the gun backwards with a force of the same size for the same time (Newton's third law). So the shot and the gun receive equal and opposite changes in momentum. That is why total momentum is conserved, and it is why guns recoil.

Worked example · the 32-pounder's kick

A 32-pounder fires a 14.5 kg shot at 450 m s⁻¹. The gun and its carriage have a mass of 3300 kg and start at rest. Ignore the mass of the powder gases. (a) Calculate the recoil velocity of the gun. (b) The breeching rope brings the gun to rest in 0.40 s. Calculate the average force the rope exerts.

Show solution

Take the direction the shot travels as positive. Before firing, the total momentum is zero.

  1. (a) 0 = (14.5 × 450) + (3300 × v) → v = −2.0 m s⁻¹: 2.0 m s⁻¹ backwards
  2. (b) Ft = mv − mu → F × 0.40 = 3300 × 0 − (−6525) (the gun's momentum after firing is −14.5 × 450 = −6525 kg m s⁻¹ exactly, which avoids rounding the velocity)
  3. F = 6525 ÷ 0.40 → F = 1.6 × 10⁴ N (unrounded: 16 313 N), acting forwards on the gun (which is why the rope had to be so thick)

Impulse and force–time graphs

Rearranging F = ma with a = (v − u)/t gives:

Ft = mv − mu

The quantity Ft is the impulse of the force (unit N s, which is the same as kg m s⁻¹). Impulse equals the change in momentum, and it equals the area under a force–time graph. Real forces in collisions are not constant: they rise and fall. The average force is the constant force that would give the same area in the same time.

Oak hull or sandbags? Stopping a spent 14.5 kg shot at 20 m s⁻¹, near the end of its flight

Stretch the contact time. The area (the impulse) stays the same, because the change in momentum is fixed, but the peak force drops dramatically. Then make the shot bounce back.

This is why crumple zones, airbags, cycle helmets and the soft mats under a high jump work: they increase the time over which the momentum changes, which reduces the average force.

Worked example · a rebound (watch the vectors)

A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall at 12 m s⁻¹ and rebounds along the same line at 8.0 m s⁻¹. It is in contact with the wall for 0.050 s. Calculate the average force the wall exerts on the ball.

Show solution

Take the direction towards the wall as positive, so u = +12 m s⁻¹ and v = −8.0 m s⁻¹.

  1. Ft = mv − mu
  2. F × 0.050 = (0.20 × −8.0) − (0.20 × 12) → F × 0.050 = −4.0
  3. F = −4.0 ÷ 0.050 → F = −80 N: 80 N directed away from the wall

Forgetting that velocity is a vector gives (0.20 × 8.0 − 0.20 × 12)/0.050 = −16 N, which is far too small.

Worked example · reading a force–time graph

On the parade ground a cadet kicks a 0.45 kg football from rest. The graph shows the force of the boot on the ball. Calculate (a) the impulse on the ball, (b) the speed of the ball as it leaves the boot and (c) the average force on the ball.

Show solution
  1. (a) Impulse = area under the graph = area of a triangle: ½ × 0.050 × 400 → 10 N s
  2. (b) Impulse = change in momentum: 10 = 0.45 × v − 0.45 × 0 → v = 10 ÷ 0.45 → v = 22 m s⁻¹
  3. (c) Ft = 10 → F × 0.050 = 10 → F = 200 N

The average force is half the peak here because the graph is a triangle: the dashed line at 200 N encloses the same area in the same time. Don't use the peak force in Ft = mv − mu.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

"Few candidates calculated the mean force correctly. Many candidates did not take the vector nature of the velocities into account." If something bounces back, its change in velocity is the sum of the two speeds.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • state the law of conservation of linear momentum, word for word;
  • solve collision and explosion problems with signs, and show by calculating Ek whether a collision is elastic or inelastic;
  • find impulse from the area under a force–time graph and use Ft = mv − mu to find an average force.

6 · Projectiles: the bow chaser ODU · gravitation

A projectile is anything moving freely under gravity alone: a cannonball, a thrown ball, a parcel dropped from an aircraft. The single most important idea is this:

📘 Key idea

The horizontal motion and the vertical motion of a projectile are independent of each other. Ignoring air resistance, the horizontal velocity stays constant (no horizontal force), while the vertical motion has a constant acceleration of 9.8 m s⁻² downwards.

So you solve a projectile problem as two separate one-dimensional problems linked only by the time:

HorizontalVertical
Acceleration09.8 m s⁻² downwards
Velocityconstant, uh = u cos θstarts at uv = u sin θ, changes by 9.8 m s⁻¹ every second
Used = v̄t (here v̄ = uh)the equations of motion

The bow chaser: projectile range

Fire horizontally (0°) with "drop a second ball" ticked. The red ball is simply dropped at the moment of firing. It hits the sea at exactly the same time as the cannonball, whatever the launch speed. That is independence. Then try angles above and below the horizontal.

Worked example · fired horizontally from the gun deck

A gun on the lower deck, 4.9 m above the sea, fires a shot horizontally at 450 m s⁻¹. Ignore air resistance. Calculate (a) the time the shot is in the air, (b) the horizontal distance it travels, (c) its vertical velocity just before it hits the water.

Show solution

Vertically, take down as positive: uv = 0, a = 9.8 m s⁻², s = 4.9 m.

  1. (a) s = ut + ½at² → 4.9 = 0 + ½ × 9.8 × t² → t² = 4.9 ÷ 4.9 = 1.0 → t = 1.0 s
  2. (b) Horizontally: d = v̄t = 450 × 1.0 → 450 m
  3. (c) v = u + at = 0 + 9.8 × 1.0 → 9.8 m s⁻¹ downwards

A ball simply dropped from the same height would also take 1.0 s. The 450 m s⁻¹ horizontal speed makes no difference to the time of flight.

Worked example · fired at an angle

A shot is fired from sea level at 25 m s⁻¹ at 40° above the horizontal. Ignore air resistance. Calculate (a) the components of its initial velocity, (b) the time to reach maximum height, (c) the range, (d) the maximum height.

Show solution
  1. (a) uh = 25 cos 40° = 19.2 m s⁻¹; uv = 25 sin 40° = 16.1 m s⁻¹
  2. (b) Vertically, up positive: at the top vv = 0. v = u + at → 0 = 16.1 + (−9.8)t → t = 16.1 ÷ 9.8 → t = 1.6 s (unrounded: 1.64 s)
  3. (c) It lands at the same height, so total time = 2 × 1.64 = 3.28 s. Range = 19.2 × 3.28 → 63 m
  4. (d) v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 16.1² + 2(−9.8)s → 19.6 s = 16.1² → s = 259 ÷ 19.6 → s = 13 m

Check with the lab above: set 25 m s⁻¹, 40° and a height of 0 m.

⚠ Exam lookout

At the top of the flight the velocity is not zero: the vertical component is zero, but the horizontal component is still u cos θ. And on a v–t graph for the vertical motion, the gradient is −9.8 m s⁻² all the way.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • resolve a launch velocity into horizontal and vertical components;
  • treat the two motions separately, with the time of flight linking them;
  • explain why the horizontal velocity is constant and the vertical acceleration is 9.8 m s⁻² downwards.

7 · Gravity, satellites and universal gravitation ODU · gravitation

Satellites are falling

Isaac Newton imagined a cannon on a mountain so tall that it was above the air. Fire a shot horizontally and it falls to the ground. Fire faster and it lands further away. Fire fast enough, and the curved Earth drops away beneath the shot as fast as the shot falls: it never lands. It keeps falling around the planet. That is an orbit.

📘 Key idea

A satellite is in free fall around the planet (or star). The only force acting on it is gravity. Its velocity at right angles to the pull of gravity carries it sideways while gravity accelerates it towards the centre.

Newton's cannon on a block-built planet

Raise the launch speed slowly from 0.3 to 1.5 times orbital speed. The red arrow is gravity: it never switches off, and it always points to the centre.

Astronauts on the International Space Station feel "weightless" for the same reason passengers float during a parabolic "zero-g" training flight: they and their craft are all falling together, so nothing pushes up on them. Gravity at the ISS's height is still about 90% of its strength at the surface.

Newton's law of universal gravitation

Every mass attracts every other mass. The force between two masses is:

F = G m₁m₂r²

where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² (on the data sheet) and r is the distance between the centres of the two masses. The force acts on both masses, equal and opposite (Newton's third law). It is an inverse square law: double the separation and the force falls to a quarter.

Worked example · the Earth and the Moon

The Earth has a mass of 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg and the Moon a mass of 7.3 × 10²² kg. The distance between their centres is 3.84 × 10⁸ m. Calculate the gravitational force between them.

Show solution
  1. F = Gm₁m₂ / r²
  2. F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.0 × 10²⁴ × 7.3 × 10²² / (3.84 × 10⁸)²
  3. F = 2.0 × 10²⁰ N

Worked example · two ships of the line

Two first-rates, each of mass 3.5 × 10⁶ kg, lie 100 m apart (centre to centre). Calculate the gravitational force between them.

Show solution
  1. F = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 3.5 × 10⁶ × 3.5 × 10⁶ / 100²
  2. F = 0.082 N, about the weight of an 8 g object: less than the weight of a single musket ball. Gravity is very weak unless at least one of the masses is enormous.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

Asked whether the force on a more distant planet would be smaller, many candidates said "less" but only some justified it. A full answer refers to the relationship: F is inversely proportional to r², so a larger r gives a smaller F. A quick calculation also counts as justification.

Experiment: measuring the acceleration of a falling object

  1. A steel ball is held by an electromagnet above a trapdoor (or above a light gate). Switching off the magnet releases the ball and starts an electronic timer.
  2. The ball falls through a measured height h (metre stick) and opens the trapdoor, which stops the timer, giving the time of fall t.
  3. Since the ball starts from rest, s = ut + ½at² becomes h = ½ g t², so g = 2h / t².
  4. Repeat for several heights. Plot h against t²: the graph is a straight line through the origin whose gradient is g/2. Using the gradient is better than averaging separate values, because it uses all the data and reveals any systematic offset.

Other acceptable versions: two light gates and a card (as on the slope, but vertical), or a ball dropped through a single light gate with a known diameter, measuring its speed after falling a known height and using v² = u² + 2as.

  • explain that a satellite is a projectile in free fall, with gravity as the only force on it;
  • use F = Gm₁m₂/r² with r measured between centres, and justify how F changes with r (inverse square);
  • describe an experiment to measure g, naming the apparatus, the measurements and how g is found.

8 · Special relativity ODU · special relativity

In 1905 Albert Einstein built a new theory of space and time on a simple postulate, since confirmed by every experiment:

📘 Key idea

The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all observers, whatever their own motion, and whatever the motion of the light source: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

That sounds harmless, but it isn't. If a cadet in a minecart rolling at 6 m s⁻¹ throws a ball forward at 10 m s⁻¹, a cadet standing ahead sees the ball arrive at 16 m s⁻¹. If the rider shines a torch forward instead, the standing cadet does not measure c + 6 m s⁻¹. They measure c, exactly as the rider does. For both of them to measure the same speed for the same light, their measurements of time and distance must differ.

Time dilation

Imagine a light clock: a pulse of light bouncing between two mirrors. Each round trip is one tick. On board the squadron's fast courier craft the pulse simply goes up and down. Seen from HMS Redstone, the clock is moving, so the pulse travels along a longer, slanting path. Light has the same speed for both observers, so the longer path must take longer. The moving clock ticks slowly.

Two light clocks

Raise the speed of the craft. Count the ticks: the moving clock falls behind. Watch the hull too: the moving craft is shorter in the direction it travels (length contraction, coming up next).

t′ = t√1 − (v/c)²

  • t is the time on a clock that travels with the thing being timed: the courier's own clock. (Formally: measured by an observer at rest relative to the event.)
  • t′ is the time for the same event measured by someone the courier whizzes past at speed v. It is always longer: this is time dilation.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"How do I remember which one is t and which is t′?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "The primed quantity, t′ (say 't-dash'), belongs to the observer watching it move, and the watcher always gets the bigger time and the shorter length. Ask yourself: whose clock is riding along with the event? That's t, and it's the smaller number."

Optional: where the formula comes from

On board, one half-tick takes t/2 = D/c. Seen from the ship, during half a tick (t′/2) the light travels c t′/2 along the diagonal while the clock moves v t′/2 sideways. Pythagoras gives (c t′/2)² = D² + (v t′/2)². Rearranging: t′²(c² − v²) = 4D², and since 2D/c = t, this gives t′ = t / √(1 − v²/c²). You don't need this for the exam, but it shows the formula is nothing more than Pythagoras plus "light has the same speed for everyone".

Length contraction

l′ = l √1 − (v/c)²

  • l is the length measured by an observer at rest relative to the object.
  • l′ is the length measured by an observer for whom the object is moving at speed v, along its direction of motion. It is always shorter: length contraction.

Only the length along the direction of motion contracts. A craft flying left to right gets shorter, not thinner.

Worked example · the fast courier

The squadron's courier spacecraft (a thought-experiment craft: nothing in 1805 flies this fast) flashes past HMS Redstone at 0.80c. The journey between two beacons takes 3.0 hours on the craft's own clock. (a) How long does the journey take as measured from HMS Redstone? (b) The craft is 60 m long when at rest. How long is it as measured from HMS Redstone?

Show solution
  1. √(1 − 0.80²) = √0.36 = 0.60
  2. (a) t′ = t / √(1 − v²/c²) = 3.0 / 0.60 → t′ = 5.0 hours
  3. (b) l′ = l √(1 − v²/c²) = 60 × 0.60 → l′ = 36 m

The times can stay in hours: the formula only needs t and t′ in the same unit. The ratio v/c has no unit at all.

Worked example · the muons that shouldn't reach the ground

Muons are created about 10 km up, when cosmic rays strike the top of the atmosphere. In their own frame of reference they last (on average) 2.2 μs. They travel at 0.995c. Explain why a large fraction of them reach the ground.

Show solution
  1. Without relativity: distance = speed × time = 0.995 × 3.00 × 10⁸ × 2.2 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 660 m. They should all decay long before the ground.
  2. From the ground, their lifetime is dilated: t′ = 2.2 × 10⁻⁶ / √(1 − 0.995²) = 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ s (ten times longer).
  3. In that time they travel 0.995 × 3.00 × 10⁸ × 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ 6.6 km.
  4. But 2.2 μs is only the average lifetime, and decay is random, so some muons last much longer. The 10 km trip takes about 34 μs. Without relativity that is about 15 average lifetimes, and almost none survive. With the dilated lifetime it is only about 1.5, so a large fraction (roughly 1 in 5) reach the ground, as is observed.

From the muon's point of view, its lifetime is normal but the atmosphere is length-contracted to about 1 km: again about 1.5 average lifetimes of travel. Both views agree on what happens.

Why don't we notice any of this? At the Tutor's cruising speed of about 60 m s⁻¹, v/c is 2 × 10⁻⁷ and the effect is about one part in 5 × 10¹³. Relativity matters for particles in accelerators, for cosmic rays, and for GPS satellites, whose clocks must be corrected: without that, relativistic effects (mostly the gravitational ones of general relativity) would make positions drift by kilometres a day.

⚠ From the 2025 course report

Most candidates identified the effect as time dilation, but "a significant number of candidates did not spell dilation correctly, with 'dilatation' being a common error." It is dilation. The most common slip in calculations is mixing up t and t′: the moving clock's own reading is the shorter one.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • state that the speed of light is the same for all observers;
  • decide which time is t and which is t′ (and l and l′), then use the relationships;
  • explain the muon observation using time dilation or length contraction.

9 · The Doppler effect and redshift ODU · the expanding Universe

When a source of waves moves, the waves it sends forwards are squashed together and the waves it sends backwards are spread out. A listener ahead hears a higher frequency; a listener behind hears a lower one. This is the Doppler effect. You hear it as a siren, a racing car or a low-flying aircraft goes past: "neeee-owww".

The ship's bell, rowed past at speed

The rings are wavefronts. They all travel at the speed of sound, 340 m s⁻¹, but each one starts from wherever the boat was when it was sent out. Press 🔊 to hear the pitch drop as it passes. (No rower could manage these speeds: they are exaggerated so the bunching is easy to see.)

For a source moving with speed vs towards or away from a stationary observer, with waves of speed v:

fo = fs vv ± vs

  • ⚠ Here v is the speed of the waves (sound, 340 m s⁻¹), not a velocity from the equations of motion.
  • Source moving towards the observer: use v − vs (smaller denominator, so fo is bigger).
  • Source moving away: use v + vs (fo is smaller).

Check your answer makes sense: approaching always means a higher pitch.

Worked example · the Tutor's flypast

The Tutor flies directly towards a cadet at 55 m s⁻¹. Its engine produces a note of 160 Hz. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. Calculate the frequency the cadet hears (a) as it approaches and (b) after it has passed.

Show solution
  1. (a) Approaching, so use v − vs: fo = 160 × 340 / (340 − 55) = 54 400 ÷ 285 → 190 Hz (unrounded: 191 Hz)
  2. (b) Moving away, so use v + vs: fo = 160 × 340 / (340 + 55) = 54 400 ÷ 395 → 140 Hz (unrounded: 138 Hz)
⚠ From the 2025 course report

Many candidates explained well, "in terms of wavefronts", why a moving buzzer sounded higher. A good answer: the source moves towards the observer between emitting one wavefront and the next, so the wavefronts arrive closer together; the wavelength is shorter and, since the speed of sound is unchanged, the frequency heard is higher. The examiners noted that a labelled diagram made answers clearer.

Redshift

Light from a source moving away from us is Doppler-shifted to longer wavelengths: towards the red end of the spectrum. This is redshift. (Light from an approaching source is blueshifted.) Astronomers measure it using the dark absorption lines of elements such as hydrogen, which appear at precisely known wavelengths in the laboratory.

📘 Redshift

The redshift z of a galaxy is the change in wavelength divided by the emitted (rest) wavelength:

z = λobserved − λrestλrest

For slowly moving galaxies (v much less than c), redshift is also the ratio of the galaxy's recessional velocity to the speed of light:

z = vc

z is a ratio, so it has no unit.

The captain's telescope: a galaxy's spectrum

Raise the redshift. Every hydrogen line moves to a longer wavelength by the same fraction, so the gap grows for the longer wavelengths.

Worked example · how fast and how far?

The red hydrogen line (rest wavelength 656 nm, from the data sheet) is observed at 689 nm in the light from a galaxy. Calculate (a) the redshift, (b) the recessional velocity and (c) the distance to the galaxy. (H₀ = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.)

Show solution
  1. (a) z = (689 − 656) / 656 → z = 33 ÷ 656 → z = 0.050 (unrounded: 0.0503)
  2. (b) z = v / c → v = 0.0503 × 3.00 × 10⁸ → v = 1.5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹
  3. (c) v = H₀d → d = 1.51 × 10⁷ ÷ (2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸) → d = 6.6 × 10²⁴ m (about 700 million light-years)
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • choose the sign in fo = fs v/(v ± vs) and check the answer makes sense;
  • explain, in terms of wavefronts, why an approaching source sounds higher;
  • calculate redshift from wavelengths, and recessional velocity from z = v/c.

10 · The expanding Universe ODU · the expanding Universe

The Hubble–Lemaître law

In the 1920s Georges Lemaître and Edwin Hubble found that the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. The recessional velocity is proportional to the distance:

v = H₀d

H₀ is the Hubble constant, about 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (data sheet). Measurements of the velocities of galaxies (from redshift) and their distances led to the theory of the expanding Universe. Galaxies are not flying apart through space like shrapnel: space itself is stretching, carrying the galaxies with it, and every galaxy sees the others receding.

Stretching the map: there is no centre

The block grid of galaxies grows. Pick any galaxy as "home": from there, every other galaxy moves away at a speed proportional to its distance.

The age of the Universe

Run the expansion backwards. If a galaxy at distance d has always moved away at speed v = H₀d, it left "here" a time t = d/v = 1/H₀ ago:

t = 1H₀ = 12.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 4.3 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years

About 14 billion years. That is why the Hubble–Lemaître law lets us estimate the age of the Universe. (Precise measurements give 13.8 billion years.)

Weighing galaxies: dark matter

Stars orbit the centre of their galaxy, held by the gravity of all the mass inside their orbit. Measure how fast stars orbit (from the Doppler shift of their light) and you can estimate the mass of the galaxy. The results are startling: stars far from the centre orbit far too fast for the visible matter to hold them. There must be extra mass that we cannot see.

This invisible mass is called dark matter. The evidence for dark matter comes from estimates of the mass of galaxies: the mass needed to explain the orbital speeds is several times the mass of all the stars, gas and dust we can see.

The accelerating Universe: dark energy

Gravity should slow the expansion down. In 1998, observations of distant exploding stars (type Ia supernovae) showed the opposite: the rate of expansion of the Universe is accelerating. Something is working against gravity on the largest scales. We call it dark energy. (Beyond the course, background only: current estimates are that ordinary matter makes up only about 5% of the Universe, dark matter about 27% and dark energy about 68%.)

Temperature and colour of stars

Every hot object emits radiation over a wide range of wavelengths. For stars:

  • The temperature of a star is related to the distribution of the radiation it emits across wavelengths.
  • The peak wavelength of the distribution is shorter for hotter objects. Hot stars look blue-white; cooler stars look orange-red.
  • Hotter objects emit more radiation per unit surface area per unit time, at every wavelength.

Starlight through the ship's prism

Raise the temperature. The peak moves left (shorter wavelength) and the whole curve rises above the Sun's.

⚠ From the 2025 course report
  • Markers saw: hotter-star sketches with a shorter peak wavelength, but "fewer candidates drew a curve that had a higher energy emitted per second per unit area at all wavelengths". Do this: draw the hotter curve above the cooler one everywhere, peaking further left.
  • Markers saw: "a number of candidates wrongly attempted to explain the difference in colour in terms of redshift". Do this: say colour tells you temperature: a redder star is cooler, so its peak wavelength is longer.

Evidence for the Big Bang

The Big Bang theory says that the Universe began about 14 billion years ago in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. You need four pieces of evidence:

EvidenceWhat it isWhy it supports the Big Bang
Cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiationFaint microwave radiation arriving from every direction in the sky, with almost exactly the same intensity everywhere.It is the heat glow of the early Universe, released when it had cooled enough to become transparent (about 380 000 years after the Big Bang: background, not examined) and stretched by the expansion from visible and infrared wavelengths into microwaves (about 2.7 K).
Abundance of hydrogen and heliumAbout three-quarters of the ordinary matter in the Universe (by mass) is hydrogen and about one-quarter helium.The Big Bang theory predicts very closely these proportions, made by nuclear fusion in the first few minutes while the Universe was hot enough.
The darkness of the sky (Olbers' paradox)If the Universe were infinite, unchanging and infinitely old, every line of sight would end on a star and the night sky would be bright.A dark night sky means the Universe has a finite age (light from far enough away has not reached us yet) and is expanding (distant light is redshifted and weakened).
Redshift, not blueshiftThe light from the large majority of galaxies is redshifted.Almost all galaxies are moving away from us, as expected in an expanding Universe.
⚠ From the 2025 course report

Many candidates could pick out correct statements about the CMB, and many could say that dark energy is thought to be responsible for the accelerating expansion. Learn the four pieces of evidence as a list. Questions often ask for two of them.
