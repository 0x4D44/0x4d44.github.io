What actually is a black hole?

Here is the shortest honest definition. A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape — not matter, not light, not information. Its edge is a one-way surface called the event horizon: cross it travelling inward and you can never send anything back out, because doing so would mean travelling faster than light, which nothing can. A black hole is not a thing so much as a place, defined entirely by the way it bends the paths available to everything around it.

Astonishingly, you need almost no ingredients to make one. You do not need exotic matter or unknown physics. You need only to take an ordinary amount of mass and squeeze it small enough. Every object has a critical size — its Schwarzschild radius — below which its own gravity wins and no light can leave. For the Earth that size is about a centimetre; for the Sun, about three kilometres; for you, far smaller than a proton. Nothing squeezes an everyday object that hard — but a dying star's core has no such luxury, and gravity finishes the job (that's Chapter 1).

The one number to remember

A black hole's size is set almost entirely by its mass, through one clean formula, the Schwarzschild radius:

rs = 2GM / c²

Double the mass, double the size. In friendly units, a black hole is about 3 km across for every Sun's worth of mass — so a 10-solar-mass hole is a 30-km ball of pure "no", and the four-million-Sun monster at our galaxy's centre is about the width of Mercury's orbit.

Once you accept that squeezing is possible, everything else in this guide follows from a single deeper idea, due to Einstein: gravity is not a force pulling on things — it is the curvature of spacetime, and things simply follow the straightest available paths through that curved geometry (Chapter 2). A black hole is just the most extreme curvature there is: a funnel so steep that every path, even a light ray's, leads inward.

Three sizes of hole

Nature builds black holes in (at least) three wildly different weight classes, and they are made in different ways:

The same physics governs all three: a one-kilogram black hole (if one existed) and the 6.5-billion-Sun giant in galaxy M87 obey the identical equations, scaled by mass. That universality is what makes the subject so satisfying — learn the rules once and they apply from the quantum to the cosmic.

A very short history

The idea is older than you'd think. In 1783 the English clergyman John Michell reasoned that a star 500 times the Sun's diameter would have an escape velocity exceeding light's, making it a "dark star." The modern version arrived in 1915, weeks after Einstein published general relativity, when Karl Schwarzschild — computing on the Russian front in the First World War — found the exact solution that now bears his name. For decades most physicists, Einstein included, thought such objects were mathematical curiosities that nature would never actually build. They were wrong.

How to read this guide

Start here and go in order — each chapter leans on the one before — or jump straight to whatever pulled you in. Play with every interactive; they do the explaining that words can't. And a promise about honesty: popular accounts of black holes tend to drift into either mysticism ("a tunnel to another universe!") or false cosiness ("just a very heavy star"). Black holes are neither. They are ferociously precise objects that general relativity describes to many decimal places — and they also mark the exact spot where that same theory admits it breaks down. This guide keeps those two things apart: it calculates first, and flags clearly where it steps into the genuinely unknown.
