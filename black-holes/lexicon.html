Chapter 8

The Lexicon

The reference shelf: the working vocabulary of black-hole physics, the constants that build every formula in this guide, a scale table of real black holes from city-sized to solar-system-sized, and the two-and-a-half-century story of how we came to believe in the impossible.

A–Z Working glossary

Accretion disk
A flattened, orbiting disk of gas spiralling toward a compact object, heated by internal friction until its inner edge glows in X-rays. The main way black holes announce themselves.
Chandrasekhar limit ≈ 1.4 M☉
The maximum mass a white dwarf can have before electron degeneracy pressure fails and it collapses. Above it, a stellar core becomes a neutron star or black hole.
Cosmic censorship
The unproven conjecture (Penrose, 1969) that singularities are always hidden behind an event horizon — no "naked" singularities exposed to the outside universe.
Ergosphere
The region outside a spinning hole's horizon where frame-dragging is so strong that nothing can remain at rest. Rotational energy can be extracted here (the Penrose process).
Event horizon r = rs
The one-way boundary of a black hole: the surface from which no signal can ever escape. Not a physical membrane — a boundary in spacetime.
Frame-dragging Lense–Thirring effect
The twisting of spacetime around a rotating mass, which drags nearby space (and orbits, and light) around with the spin. Measured for the Earth by Gravity Probe B.
Geodesic
The straightest possible path through curved spacetime — the route a freely falling body or light ray follows when no force acts on it. Orbits are geodesics.
Hawking radiation
The faint thermal glow a black hole emits due to quantum effects at the horizon, at temperature TH = ħc³/8πGMkB. Causes evaporation over vast timescales.
Innermost stable circular orbit ISCO
The closest stable circular orbit around a black hole (3 rs when non-spinning). Sets the inner edge of an accretion disk and the efficiency of accretion.
Kerr metric
The exact solution (Roy Kerr, 1963) describing a rotating, uncharged black hole — the kind that actually exists. Has a ring singularity and an ergosphere.
No-hair theorem
The result that a black hole is fully described by just three numbers — mass, spin and charge — with every other detail of what formed it erased.
Photon sphere r = 1.5 rs
The radius where gravity bends light into a circular orbit. Produces the bright "photon ring" seen in black-hole images.
Quasar
A supermassive black hole feeding so voraciously that its accretion disk outshines the entire surrounding galaxy — among the most luminous objects in the universe.
Schwarzschild radius rs = 2GM/c²
The radius of the event horizon of a non-spinning black hole; about 3 km per solar mass. Karl Schwarzschild's 1915 solution.
Singularity
The point (or ring) at the centre where the classical equations give infinite curvature — the marker for where general relativity breaks down and quantum gravity must take over.
Spaghettification
The stretching (and lateral pinching) of an object by tidal forces near a black hole, which grow as 1/r³. Lethal near small holes, gentle at the horizon of large ones.
Tidal force
The difference in gravitational pull across an extended object, because the near side is pulled harder than the far side. The cause of spaghettification and of Earth's ocean tides.

§8.3 Timeline

How we came to believe in the impossible
YearEvent
1783John Michell describes "dark stars" whose escape velocity exceeds light's.
1915Einstein completes general relativity; Karl Schwarzschild finds the exact solution weeks later, on the WWI front.
1931Chandrasekhar derives the 1.4 M☉ white-dwarf limit.
1939Oppenheimer & Snyder model the complete gravitational collapse of a star.
1963Roy Kerr solves the metric for a rotating black hole.
1965Penrose proves singularities are unavoidable (Nobel 2020).
1967John Wheeler popularises the term "black hole."
1971Cygnus X-1 identified as the first strong black-hole candidate.
1974Hawking shows black holes radiate and slowly evaporate.
2015LIGO detects gravitational waves from a black-hole merger (Nobel 2017).
2019The Event Horizon Telescope photographs M87*.
2020Genzel & Ghez win the Nobel for the stars orbiting Sgr A*.
2022The EHT images Sagittarius A* at our own galaxy's heart.

§8.4 Where to read next

  • Kip Thorne — Black Holes & Time Warps. The definitive popular history, by a physicist who was there for most of it (and won the Nobel for LIGO).
  • Leonard Susskind — The Black Hole War. The information paradox as a gripping intellectual thriller.
  • Sean Carroll — Spacetime and Geometry. The graduate textbook, if you want to do the general relativity properly.
  • Misner, Thorne & Wheeler — Gravitation. The legendary 1200-page "telephone book," the reference the equations in this guide were checked against.
