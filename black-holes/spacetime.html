Chapter 2

Curved Spacetime

To go any further we have to give up an idea you have carried your whole life: that gravity is a force that pulls. Einstein's staggering insight was that gravity is not a force at all. It is the shape of spacetime — and things, including light, simply coast along the straightest paths available through that shape. A black hole is what happens when the shape becomes a cliff.

Einstein called it "the happiest thought of my life." It sounds like nothing: a person in free fall does not feel their own weight.

Step off a diving board and, for the moment you fall, you feel weightless — the same floating you have seen astronauts enjoy. Einstein realised this is not a coincidence but a clue to the deepest nature of gravity. A freely falling frame feels no gravity at all. If you were in a windowless lift and it fell freely, nothing inside — no experiment you could do — would reveal that you were falling rather than drifting in deep space. And conversely, standing in that lift on a launch pad accelerating upward at g would feel exactly like standing on Earth.

The Equivalence Principle

Locally, gravity and acceleration are indistinguishable. There is no experiment you can perform in a small enough sealed box that tells you whether you are being pulled by gravity or pushed by a rocket. Gravity is not a substance acting on you; it is a property of the reference frame you are in.

Bob, in free fall

Wait — so when I'm falling toward the black hole, I don't feel any pull at all? I just feel… nothing?

Exactly. You feel weightless the whole way down. Gravity isn't tugging on you — it has bent the roads, and you're just following the only road there is.

§2.1 Straight lines in a bent world

If gravity is not pulling the planets around the Sun, what keeps them in orbit? Einstein's answer: the Sun's mass curves the spacetime around it, and the planets simply travel in straight lines — but straight lines through a curved geometry come back on themselves. A "straight line" in curved spacetime is called a geodesic: the locally straightest possible path, the one you follow when no force acts on you. On the curved surface of the Earth the geodesics are great circles; that is why long-haul flights arc toward the pole even though the map makes it look like a detour. Around a mass, the geodesics are orbits.

Where the analogy lies to you

The sheet needs gravity "underneath" to work, which is circular, and it shows only space bending. In truth, for slow-moving things like planets and apples, the dominant effect is the bending of time: clocks run slower deeper in a gravity well, and objects "fall" toward where their own clock ticks slowest. We will make that concrete in Chapter 5. Keep the marble picture for intuition; trust the equations for truth.

§2.2 Light has to bend too

Here is the prediction that made Einstein world-famous overnight. If mass curves spacetime, then light must follow the curves as well — a passing star should visibly deflect a ray of starlight, bending its path. Newton's gravity, treating light as fast particles, predicts a small bend too; general relativity predicts exactly twice as much, because in Einstein's theory both space and time are curved and light feels both.

The deflection of light

A light ray passing a mass M at closest distance b (the "impact parameter") is bent through an angle

α = 4GM / c²b = 2rs / b (2.1)

— twice the Newtonian value. In 1919 Arthur Eddington sailed to the island of Príncipe, photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun, and measured the tiny shift. It matched Einstein, not Newton. The next morning's Times ran the headline "Revolution in Science."

Far from a black hole the bend is gentle. But bring the ray closer and equation (2.1) runs away — at a certain radius, the photon sphere at 1.5  rs, light can circle the hole entirely, and any ray aimed closer than a critical impact parameter is captured, never to leave. Fire beams past the hole below and watch it happen. The rays that skim the capture radius wind around and paint the bright photon ring you saw on the front page.

In parallel beam mode, slide aim so the beam grazes the hole: the rays nearest the edge swing hard and some spiral in (red). Switch to point star and move aim until the star sits directly behind the hole — its light wraps around both sides at once and you get a ring. That is a real Einstein ring, and it is how we weigh distant galaxies.

Go deeper — the geodesic these rays actually followthe orbit equation for light

Writing u = 1/r and φ for the angle around the hole, a light ray in the Schwarzschild geometry obeys a deceptively simple equation (here M ≡ GM/c² = rs/2, in length units):

d²udφ² + u = 3Mu² (2.2)

The left side alone (u'' + u = 0) gives a straight line — Newton's world. The whole novelty of general relativity for light is the little 3Mu² on the right. It is negligible when r is large (weak bending, equation 2.1) and it takes over completely near the hole, producing the photon sphere at r = 3M = 1.5  rs and capture for any ray with impact parameter below the critical bc = 3√3 M ≈ 2.6  rs. §2.3 The field equation, in one breath

All of this — the orbits, the bending, the black hole itself — is encoded in a single tensor equation Einstein published in 1915, the crowning achievement of classical physics:

Gμν = 8πGc⁴ Tμν

The physicist John Wheeler compressed its meaning into nine words that are hard to improve on: "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve." The left side, Gμν, is the curvature of spacetime; the right side, Tμν, is the matter and energy present. Feed in a single spherical mass and solve, and out comes the Schwarzschild solution — the exact description of the spacetime around any non-rotating black hole. Its geometry is the map for the rest of this guide. In the next chapter we walk its territory, ring by ring, from the photon sphere down to the horizon.
