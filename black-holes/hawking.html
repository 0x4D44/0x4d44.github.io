Chapter 6

Hawking Radiation

In 1974 a 32-year-old Stephen Hawking proved something nobody expected and Hawking himself initially disbelieved: black holes are not perfectly black. They glow, faintly, with a temperature set by their mass — and glowing means losing energy, and losing energy means shrinking. Given enough time, every black hole evaporates. The result tied gravity, quantum theory and thermodynamics into a single knot that physics is still trying to untangle.

The trick is that empty space is not empty. At the smallest scales the vacuum seethes.

Quantum theory forbids perfect stillness. Even in the emptiest vacuum, fields flicker with irreducible "zero-point" jitter, constantly producing fleeting virtual particle pairs — a particle and its antiparticle — that appear, exist for a borrowed instant, and annihilate back to nothing before the universe can notice. This is not speculation; the tiny forces these virtual pairs exert (the Casimir effect, the Lamb shift) are measured to exquisite precision. Now bring this restless vacuum right up to the edge of a black hole, and something can go wrong with the usual disappearing act.

The heuristic picture (handle with care)

Imagine a virtual pair flickering into being straddling the horizon. One partner falls in; the other, now with no partner to annihilate, is stranded in the real world and flies off as a genuine particle — Hawking radiation. To balance the books, the partner that fell in must carry negative energy inward, so the black hole's mass ticks down by exactly the escaping particle's worth. Hawking used this cartoon himself, but warned it is only a mnemonic. The rigorous version is subtler: the hole's intense gravity mixes the quantum vacuum's notions of "positive" and "negative" frequency, so that what one observer calls empty space, a distant observer sees as a warm thermal glow.

§6.1 A temperature, and everything that follows

Hawking's calculation delivered a shockingly clean result. A black hole radiates like a warm body — a perfect thermal spectrum — at a temperature that depends only on its mass:

The Hawking temperature
TH = ħc³8πGMkB (6.1)

Look at where M sits: on the bottom. The temperature is inversely proportional to mass. A bigger black hole is colder; a smaller one is hotter. This single fact drives the entire strange story of evaporation below. Notice, too, that this one little formula contains ħ (quantum mechanics), c and G (relativity and gravity), and kB (thermodynamics) — four pillars of physics that meet nowhere else.

Take a black hole's temperature yourself. The result for any ordinary hole is almost comically cold — far colder than the 2.7-kelvin afterglow of the Big Bang that fills all of space. That matters: because real black holes are colder than their surroundings, they currently absorb more than they emit, and grow. Evaporation only wins once the universe itself cools further, or for holes born tiny.

Pip

Wait, so a black hole the mass of the Sun is colder than deep space? Then how does anything ever evaporate?

It mostly doesn't — yet. Big holes grow for now. But run the clock forward far enough, past when the last stars die and the cosmic background fades below the holes' own temperature, and even the giants begin to shrink.

§6.2 The runaway end, and the longest wait

Here is the twist hidden in equation (6.1). As a black hole radiates, it loses mass; as it loses mass, it gets hotter; hotter means it radiates faster; which makes it lose mass faster still. This is a runaway with a strange name — negative heat capacity — the opposite of a normal object, which cools as it loses heat. So evaporation starts as an imperceptible trickle over eons and ends in a fraction of a second: the final moments are a blinding flash as the last of the mass converts to a burst of high-energy radiation. The lifetime scales as the cube of the mass:

tevap ≈ 5120 π G²M³ħc⁴ (6.2)

For a stellar black hole this is around 1067 years — so far beyond the current age of the universe (1.4×1010 years) that the number is essentially meaningless on any human or cosmic scale. A supermassive hole takes 1090 years or more. These are the last objects that will exist in the universe, presiding over an all-but-empty cosmos long after the last star has died, before finally winking out. A black hole formed in the Big Bang with the mass of an asteroid, on the other hand, would be finishing its evaporation right about now — which is why astronomers hunt for the tell-tale gamma-ray flashes of primordial black holes.

§6.3 Black holes have entropy — and a paradox

If a black hole has a temperature, thermodynamics insists it must also have entropy — a measure of hidden disorder, of how many ways its interior could be arranged. Jacob Bekenstein guessed this before Hawking's proof, and the answer is one of the most beautiful equations in physics:

S = kBc³A4Għ (6.3)

The entropy is proportional to the area A of the horizon — not its volume. This is staggering: it says the maximum information that can be stored in any region of space is set by the area of its boundary, as if the three-dimensional interior were a projection of data written on a two-dimensional surface. That insight grew into the holographic principle, now a central idea in quantum gravity. A black hole the size of the one in M87 has an entropy so vast it dwarfs the entropy of all the ordinary matter in the observable universe combined.

The information paradox

Here is the unsolved knot. Quantum mechanics is built on a rule that information is never truly destroyed — the present always encodes a recoverable past. But Hawking radiation, in his original calculation, is perfectly thermal: random, featureless, carrying no trace of what fell in. So when a black hole evaporates completely, what happens to the information about everything it swallowed? If it is gone, quantum mechanics is broken. If it is somehow imprinted on the outgoing radiation, then Hawking's own calculation is incomplete. This is the black hole information paradox, and after fifty years it is only partly resolved — recent work on "islands" and entanglement entropy strongly suggests information does escape, but exactly how the bits get out remains one of the deepest open problems in physics.

Hawking radiation remains, for now, entirely theoretical — no black hole is hot enough for us to detect its glow against the cosmic background. Yet almost no physicist doubts it is real: it falls out of the mathematics too cleanly, and laboratory "analogue" black holes made of flowing fluids and light have shown the same effect on tabletop scales. To actually find black holes, though, we rely not on their whisper of radiation but on the violence of the matter falling in — which is where we turn next.
