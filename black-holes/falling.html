Chapter 5

Falling In

Two people, one black hole. Bob straps in and falls; you stay behind with a telescope. You are both right about what happens — and you will disagree completely. Bob sails across the horizon in seconds and notices nothing. You watch him slow, redden, dim, and freeze at the edge forever, never quite arriving. This is not an illusion or a paradox. It is what it means for time itself to bend.

The disagreement comes from one fact you can measure on Earth today: clocks run slower where gravity is stronger.

This is gravitational time dilation, and it is not science fiction — your phone's GPS corrects for it every second. Clocks on the satellites, higher in Earth's weak gravity, tick faster than clocks on the ground by about 38 microseconds a day; ignore it and GPS would drift kilometres off within hours. Near a black hole the same effect goes from a rounding error to the whole story. The deeper into the gravity well a clock sits, the slower it runs as seen from outside — and at the event horizon, seen from outside, it stops altogether.

Hit drop Bob. Watch his own clock (cyan) tick steadily all the way across the horizon and down — a few seconds, start to finish, and he feels fine the whole way. Now watch the amber image, which is what your telescope actually receives: it slows, piles up against the horizon, reddens toward infinity, and fades to black. You will wait literally forever to see Bob cross.

§5.1 Bob's story: a quick, unremarkable trip

From Bob's point of view, almost nothing dramatic happens. He is in free fall, so — by the equivalence principle (Chapter 2) — he feels weightless the entire way. His own clock ticks one second per second, as everyone's always does. He crosses the event horizon without any local sign at all: no wall, no barrier, no jolt, no flash. For a supermassive hole the tides are gentle (Chapter 4), so he could cross the most important surface in the universe and not notice the moment it happened. Only afterwards does the trap spring shut: now every direction leads inward, and a short, fixed span of proper time later — measured in seconds for a stellar hole, hours for a giant one — he reaches the singularity. Nothing he does can slow that arrival.

Bob, crossing the horizon

…huh. Was that it? My clock says nine seconds. I didn't feel a thing. Are you still getting my signal out there?

We are — sort of. Your last "hello" is going to take the rest of eternity to finish arriving.

§5.2 Your story: Bob freezes at the edge

From your telescope, the same trip looks utterly different. As Bob falls, each light signal he sends has to climb out of a deeper part of the gravity well, so each one takes longer to reach you than the last, and each is stretched to longer, redder wavelengths. His movements appear to slow. His colours slide from white to orange to deep red to infrared to radio to nothing. His image dims exponentially. As he approaches the horizon, the light he emitted right at the edge takes an infinite time to crawl out to you — so you see him slow asymptotically and freeze, hovering just above the horizon, fading forever but never quite crossing.

Why the Soviets called them "frozen stars"

For exactly this reason, Soviet physicists led by Yakov Zel'dovich originally called these objects frozen stars (zamyorzshiye zvyozdy): from outside, the collapsing star appears to freeze at its horizon, its surface hanging motionless and reddening for eternity. The American name "black hole," popularised by John Wheeler in 1967, won out — but the Russian one captured the outside view perfectly. Both descriptions are correct; they are simply told by clocks that no longer agree.

The resolution — there is no paradox

So does Bob cross the horizon or not? Yes. He does, in finite time, on his own perfectly good clock. The "freezing" is not what happens to Bob; it is what the outside sees, distorted by the ever-growing climb his light must make. There is no contradiction because the two observers can never compare notes again: once Bob is inside, no signal of his can reach you, and once you have waited "forever," Bob is long since gone. Each is right within their own slice of spacetime. Relativity does not promise everyone the same story — only that each story is self-consistent.

Go deeper — the redshift factorwhere "freezes and reddens" comes from

For a clock at rest at radius r outside a non-spinning hole, the ratio of its tick rate to a faraway clock's is the square root of the metric's time coefficient:

dτdt = √1 − rs/r (5.1)

As r → rs, this ratio → 0: the local clock's ticks arrive arbitrarily far apart. The wavelength of light stretches by the reciprocal factor, so the redshift is

1 + z = 1√1 − rs/r (5.2)

which diverges at the horizon. A falling emitter like Bob adds an ordinary Doppler redshift on top (he is moving away as he falls), making the dimming even faster — the received brightness of an infalling object drops off exponentially with a time constant of only a few times rs/c, which for a stellar hole is well under a millisecond. In practice Bob doesn't linger visibly; he simply winks out.

Bob's fate raises an uncomfortable question we have so far dodged. If nothing ever truly escapes, and infalling things freeze at the edge, is a black hole utterly, permanently black — a perfect one-way trap for all eternity? For sixty years the answer was assumed to be yes. Then a young Stephen Hawking did a calculation that changed everything.
