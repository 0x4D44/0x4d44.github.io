Chapter 7

Seeing the Unseeable

A black hole emits no light — so how do we know they are out there, and even weigh and photograph them? The answer is that a black hole is a spectacularly bad neighbour. It heats the gas it eats until it outshines galaxies, whips nearby stars into giveaway orbits, shakes spacetime itself when two of them collide, and casts a shadow we have now, at last, photographed. Four independent methods — and each one won a Nobel Prize.

You never see the hole. You see what it does to everything around it. Start with the brightest clue: matter screaming as it falls.

§7.1 Accretion — the brightest things in the sky

Gas doesn't fall straight into a black hole; it has sideways motion, so it spirals in and piles up into a flattened, orbiting accretion disk — the glowing band you saw on the front page. Friction and magnetic stress in the disk heat the inner edge to millions of degrees, hot enough to blaze in X-rays. This process is ferociously efficient: a non-spinning hole converts up to 6% of infalling mass to energy, and a fast-spinning one up to 42% — against a mere 0.7% for the nuclear fusion that powers stars. Pound for pound, accretion onto a black hole is the most efficient steady energy source in the universe. When a supermassive hole feeds hard, its disk becomes a quasar, outshining its entire host galaxy of a hundred billion stars and visible clear across the observable universe.

Cygnus X-1 — and a famous bet

The first widely accepted black hole was Cygnus X-1, a bright X-ray source where a ~21-solar-mass invisible object orbits a blue supergiant, dragging gas off it into a searing disk. It was the subject of a 1974 wager between Stephen Hawking and Kip Thorne — Hawking betting against it being a black hole as an "insurance policy" against his life's work being wrong. He conceded in 1990. It was a black hole.

§7.2 Jets — beams across a galaxy

Some of the infalling matter never makes it in. Twisted magnetic fields anchored in the spinning disk and the hole's own rotation fling a fraction of it back out along the spin axis in two thin relativistic jets — beams of plasma moving at over 99% of light speed, punching hundreds of thousands of light-years out of the galaxy. The Blandford–Znajek mechanism that powers them is, essentially, a black hole working as a colossal electromagnetic dynamo, extracting energy from its own spin. When a jet happens to point at Earth, we call the source a blazar, and it flickers with the violence of the innermost light-hours around the hole.

§7.3 Watching stars orbit nothing

The cleanest proof of all sits at the centre of our own galaxy. For nearly three decades, two teams tracked individual stars looping around an invisible point in Sagittarius. One star, S2, swings around every 16 years on a tight ellipse, reaching 3% of the speed of light at closest approach. From its orbit — pure Kepler and Newton, then Einstein for the fine details — the mass of the central object is pinned at 4.3 million Suns, packed into a region smaller than S2's closest approach. Nothing but a black hole can be that heavy and that small and that dark. The measurement earned Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize.

§7.4 Hearing them collide — the chirp

In September 2015, a century after Einstein predicted them, the LIGO detectors caught a ripple in spacetime itself: two black holes, 36 and 29 solar masses, spiralling together 1.3 billion light-years away and merging into one. As they whirled closer they radiated gravitational waves — stretching and squeezing space — rising in pitch and volume until they slammed together, releasing three Suns' worth of energy as pure spacetime vibration in a fraction of a second. Its signature is the famous chirp: frequency and amplitude sweeping up, then a sharp merger, then the "ringdown" as the newborn hole settles. Play one below.

Hit replay and watch the two dots wind inward, faster and faster, while the waveform below sweeps up in pitch — that's the chirp. Slide total mass down: lighter holes are smaller and merge at a higher frequency (a higher-pitched chirp); heavier ones boom low and briefly. LIGO literally converts these waves to audio — and they sound like a bird's chirp.

§7.5 Photographing a shadow

Finally, in April 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope did the thing that was supposed to be impossible: it took a picture. By linking radio dishes across the whole Earth into a single virtual telescope the size of the planet, it resolved the glowing ring and dark central shadow of M87*, the 6.5-billion-solar-mass hole in the galaxy M87, 55 million light-years away. In 2022 the same team imaged Sgr A* at our own galaxy's centre. The dark patch is the shadow — light-paths swallowed by the horizon; the ring is exactly the lensed, Doppler-beamed emission you bent into shape back in Chapter 2 and met on the front page. The image matched general relativity's prediction, made decades earlier, to within its error bars. A theory born in 1915 had told us precisely what a photograph taken in 2019 would show.

Professor Nova

Four completely independent windows — X-rays, radio jets, stellar orbits, gravitational waves, and now a direct image — and every one tells the same story, with the same masses, the same geometry. That is what it looks like when a hundred-year-old prediction turns out to be simply, thoroughly true.

We have come the whole way down and back out: from a thrown ball, through curved spacetime, past the photon ring and horizon, to the singularity and the faint glow of evaporation, and out again to the telescopes that catch them.
