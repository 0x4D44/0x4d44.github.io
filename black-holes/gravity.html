Chapter 1

Escape & Collapse

Throw a ball hard enough and it never comes back. That single everyday idea — escape velocity — contains the whole seed of a black hole. Push it to its limit and you find that any object, squeezed small enough, will trap even its own light. Then we ask the real question: what could possibly squeeze that hard? A dying star, it turns out, with nothing left to hold gravity off.

Start with something you already know in your bones: to leave, you have to be going fast enough.

Throw a ball upward and Earth's gravity slows it, stops it, and pulls it back. Throw it faster and it goes higher before returning. Throw it fast enough — about 11.2 km/s, or 40,000 km/h — and it never returns at all: it has enough speed to climb out of Earth's gravity forever. That threshold is the escape velocity, and it depends on just two things: how much mass is pulling you back, and how close you start to it.

Escape velocity

To escape an object of mass M starting at distance R from its centre, you need a speed of at least

vesc = √2GM / R

Notice what happens as you shrink R: the same mass, packed into a smaller ball, has a higher escape velocity at its surface. Compress it enough and vesc climbs all the way to the speed of light, c. At that point nothing at all can leave — because nothing can travel faster than light.

1783 · the first black hole

This is exactly the reasoning of the Reverend John Michell, a Yorkshire clergyman and geologist, in a paper read to the Royal Society in 1783. Setting escape velocity equal to the speed of light, he concluded that a body with the Sun's density but 500 times its diameter would be invisible: "all light emitted from such a body would be made to return towards it." He called them dark stars. He even suggested we might detect one by the motion of a companion star orbiting it — which is very nearly how we found the first real black hole two centuries later. Pierre-Simon Laplace reached the same conclusion independently in 1796.

§1.1 Make a black hole with a slider

Below is a star with a fixed mass. The only thing you control is how tightly it is packed: drag the compression slider and watch its own light try to leave. When the star is large, photons fly off into the night unimpeded. As you squeeze it, escape velocity climbs; light leaves more and more steeply until, at a critical radius, it can no longer get away at all. The star turns black. You have just made an event horizon.

Push compression all the way up. Watch the escape-velocity gauge cross 1.00 × c — the instant it does, every photon curves back and the disk goes black. Now switch the mass to 1 Earth: the black hole is still there, but its horizon is the size of a marble. Mass sets the size; anything can become a black hole if you squeeze it hard enough.

Zip the photon

Hey — I was leaving! Why does it get harder the smaller you make it? I'm going the same speed either way.

Because you start deeper in the gravity well. The mass didn't change, but the surface moved closer to it. Same climb, taller cliff.

Go deeper — where rs comes froma one-line "derivation" that gets the right answer

Set the escape velocity equal to the speed of light and solve for the radius. Starting from vesc = √(2GM/R), put vesc = c:

c² = 2GM / rs ⟹ rs = 2GM / c² (1.1)

This is the Schwarzschild radius. The delicious part: this Newtonian hand-wave gives exactly the same answer as Schwarzschild's full solution of Einstein's equations in 1915 — the factor of 2 and all. That is a coincidence of the non-rotating case (two errors cancelling), but a very useful one. Plugging in the constants, a clean rule of thumb falls out:

rs ≈ 2.95 km × (M / M☉)

About 3 kilometres of horizon per solar mass. The Sun: 3 km. The Earth: 9 mm. A human: 10−25 m, a hundred-millionth the width of a proton — which is why you are not, at this moment, a black hole.

§1.2 What could ever squeeze that hard?

The catch, of course, is that nothing in everyday life squeezes matter anywhere near this hard. Atoms resist. Push on a rock and its electrons push back; that resistance — electromagnetic, and later something subtler — holds up everything from a table to a planet. To make a black hole you need to defeat every form of resistance matter can offer. There is exactly one thing in the universe strong enough to try: gravity itself, given enough mass and taken away from anything that might hold it off.

That is why black holes are born from dying stars. For its whole life a star is a standoff between two titanic forces in near-perfect balance:

  • Gravity pulls every gram of the star inward, trying to crush it to a point.
  • Pressure from nuclear fusion in the core pushes outward. Fusing hydrogen into helium releases energy, the core stays hot, and the outward push exactly balances the inward pull.

A star is a controlled bomb held together by its own weight — for millions or billions of years, a draw. But fusion needs fuel, and fuel runs out. When the core has fused its way up to iron, the game is over: fusing iron absorbs energy rather than releasing it, so the outward push vanishes in seconds. Gravity, which never tires, wins.

3

4
What's left behind is crushed past every limit — a black hole.

§1.3 The last stands of matter

When fusion stops, gravity doesn't win instantly — matter makes two more desperate stands, each using a strange quantum rule called the Pauli exclusion principle (no two identical particles may share the same state; crowd them and they push back ferociously). Which stand — if any — succeeds is decided entirely by mass:

What a dying stellar core becomes
Core mass leftWhat holds it upResultDensity
below ~1.4 M☉Electron degeneracy pressure White dwarf — Earth-sized ember~10⁹ kg/m³
~1.4 – ~2.3 M☉Neutron degeneracy pressure Neutron star — a city-sized nucleus~10¹⁷ kg/m³
above ~2.3 M☉Nothing known Black hole→ ∞

The first threshold is the celebrated Chandrasekhar limit, ≈ 1.4 solar masses — computed by a 19-year-old Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar in 1930 on the boat from India to England. Above it, electrons can no longer hold the core up and it collapses until the electrons are crushed into the protons, making a ball of almost pure neutrons: a neutron star, so dense a sugar-cube of it would weigh as much as a mountain. The second threshold — the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit, around 2 to 2.3 solar masses — is where even neutron pressure fails. Past it, physics knows of nothing that can stop the fall. The core collapses without limit, and an event horizon closes over it.

The point of no return, literally

Once the collapsing core shrinks inside its own Schwarzschild radius, the escape velocity at the surface exceeds c, and the outcome is sealed. No force, no pressure, no cleverness can now halt the collapse — not because we haven't found the right material, but because stopping would require signalling faster than light. The matter continues inward toward a singularity (Chapter 4), but the horizon — the black hole as the outside universe knows it — is already complete.

So a black hole is not made of any special stuff. It is ordinary matter — the same protons and neutrons that make you — that lost its lifelong argument with gravity. The horizon is not a surface you could touch; it is a boundary in spacetime, the last sphere from which light can still, just barely, escape. To understand what that boundary really is, we have to stop thinking of gravity as a force at all. That is the next chapter.
