DelticBritish Rail Class 55 · 1961–1982

Twenty-two locomotives, each carrying two triangular diesel engines — and named, half after Derby winners and half after Army regiments. For twenty years the Deltics were the fastest thing on the East Coast Main Line. The name comes from the shape of the engine: a delta, Δ.

01 · THE ENGINEWhy it’s called a Deltic

The Napier Deltic was a two-stroke diesel built as an inverted triangle: three banks of cylinders forming the three sides, and a crankshaft at each of the three corners. There is no cylinder head. Instead each cylinder holds two pistons that race toward each other — an opposed-piston design — so the combustion space is the gap between them at the middle of the bore. One piston uncovers the exhaust ports, the other the inlet ports, and the charge is swept straight through. The three crankshafts are tied together by phasing gears and drive a single output shaft; one crank runs slightly ahead so the exhaust piston always leads for clean scavenging.

Napier borrowed the opposed-piston trick from the wartime Junkers Jumo aircraft diesel, then did something new with it — folding three banks into that Δ. The Greek letter gave the engine, and later the locomotive, its name. It was first built for fast naval craft; in its 18-cylinder railway form each engine gave 1,650 hp, and English Electric hung two of them in every Class 55.

02 · THE LOCOMOTIVEMasters of the East Coast

English Electric built the 22 production Deltics at the Vulcan Foundry in 1961–62, after a single powder-blue prototype (DP1, 1955, now in the Science Museum) proved the idea. They took over the crack King’s Cross–Edinburgh expresses on the East Coast Main Line, where their sustained power let just 22 machines cover work that had needed 55 Gresley Pacifics in steam.

They were the most powerful single-unit diesel locomotives in the world when new, and held the East Coast’s fastest schedules until the High Speed Train arrived. Withdrawn in 1981–82 as the HST took over, six survived into preservation — and one, 55022, still runs on the main line. The Deltic Preservation Society keeps three more alive.

03 · THE NAMESRacehorses & regiments

How a Deltic was named depended on which depot it belonged to. Finsbury Park in London kept the old LNER tradition and named its engines after racehorse classic winners. Gateshead chose regiments of the North-East of England, and Haymarket in Edinburgh chose Scottish regiments. The eight racehorse names below are the complete set — there were exactly eight — the other fourteen honour the British Army.

04 · CODAThe names ride on

The locomotives are gone from traffic, but the names keep turning up. One place they live on is software: the author keeps a small fleet utility named — of course — Deltic, which christens each cluster of build machines after one of these locomotives. It picks the name deterministically, so every machine in a cluster agrees on it with no coordination: take the cluster’s anchor name, hash it with SHA-256, read the first eight hex digits as a number, and index into a fixed list of ten.

Worked example

A cluster whose anchor is WORKSHOP resolves like this — the same arithmetic the tool runs, and the same answer on every machine:

sha256("WORKSHOP") = e7fec7a8…
0xe7fec7a8 = 3,892,234,152
3,892,234,152 mod 10 = 2 → Pinza

Ten names, indexed 0–9: all eight racehorse Deltics, plus two of the best-loved regiment names — Royal Scots Grey (that’s 55022, the preserved pioneer) and Gordon Highlander. A tiny homage, hashed into a build fleet.
