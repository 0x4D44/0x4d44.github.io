Chapter 2

The Machinery

Strip away the history and quantum mechanics is a short list of rules about vectors in complex spaces — rules you can write on an index card and spend a lifetime unpacking. This chapter builds the machine piece by piece: amplitudes, state vectors, operators, the Born rule, the commutator that hides the uncertainty principle, and the one equation that turns all of it in time.

2.1The strange new bookkeeping

Classical probability adds like common sense. If an event can happen two ways, its probability is the sum of the probabilities of each way: P = P1 + P2. Quantum mechanics replaces this with a deeper rule, and every quantum phenomenon in this almanac is a corollary of it. To each way a process can happen, nature assigns not a probability but a probability amplitude — a complex number, an arrow in the plane with a length and a direction. The rules of the game:

  1. Alternatives add as amplitudes. If the ways are indistinguishable in principle — nothing anywhere records which occurred — add the arrows tip-to-tail: ψ = ψ1 + ψ2.
  2. Probability is the squared length of the final arrow: P = |ψ|². This is the Born rule.
  3. Distinguishable alternatives add as probabilities, classically: P = |ψ1|² + |ψ2|². The moment a record of “which way” exists, rule 1 is off.
  4. Sequential steps multiply amplitudes, as independent probabilities would.

Because arrows have direction, two ways can conspire (aligned: |ψ1+ψ2|² up to four times either alone) or cancel (opposed: exactly zero — a place the particle simply cannot land, though either route alone would happily take it there). That is interference, and it is why the double slit in the Overview does what it does — and why the fringes die when the detector switches on: the which-path record converts rule 1 into rule 3. No force is exerted; a bookkeeping condition changes, and the world changes with it.

2.2States are vectors

The amplitude rules become a theory once you organise the amplitudes. The complete catalogue of amplitudes for a system — one for each possible outcome of a maximal measurement — is its state, and the deep structural fact of quantum mechanics is that states behave like vectors: they can be scaled by complex numbers and added, and the result is another legal state. This is the superposition principle, and it is the load-bearing axiom of the theory.

Dirac's notation, universally used, writes a state as a ket |ψ⟩. For a system with n distinguishable outcomes, kets live in ℂn — lists of n complex numbers; for a particle on a line the list becomes a continuum, the wavefunction ψ(x) of Chapter 3. In both cases the arena is a Hilbert space ℋ: a complex vector space equipped with an inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩ (and, in infinite dimensions, complete — every Cauchy sequence converges, the technical clause that keeps the calculus honest). The inner product is the master operation:

⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ∑k φk* ψk  or  ∫ φ(x)*ψ(x) dx INNER PRODUCT

It is linear in the ket, conjugate-linear in the bra ⟨φ| (a bra is the conjugate-transpose row vector of its ket), and ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ‖ψ‖² ≥ 0. Physical states are normalised, ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1, because the probabilities of a complete set of outcomes must total one; and the global phase of a state is unobservable — |ψ⟩ and eiα|ψ⟩ describe the same physics, though relative phases inside a superposition are the whole point, as the figure above shows.

Choose an orthonormal basis {|k⟩} — physically, the outcomes of some maximal measurement, ⟨j|k⟩ = δjk — and any state expands as

|ψ⟩ = ∑k ck|k⟩,  ck = ⟨k|ψ⟩,  ∑k |ck|² = 1 EXPANSION

The component ck = ⟨k|ψ⟩ is the amplitude of Section 2.1 — the amplitude that a system prepared in |ψ⟩ is found, on measurement, in |k⟩ — and |ck|² is its probability. A superposition is not ignorance of which basis state the system is “really” in: the relative phases among the ck produce interference that no mixture of definite alternatives can imitate. That distinction — superposition versus mixture — is made precise with density matrices in Chapter 5.

Notational dividend

Dirac's brackets make the bookkeeping self-checking. |k⟩⟨k| is the projector onto basis direction k; summing them gives the resolution of the identity Σk|k⟩⟨k| = 𝟙, which you can insert anywhere — the algebraic engine behind “change of basis”, and the discrete ancestor of the Feynman path sum in Chapter 6.

2.3Observables are operators

Measurable quantities — position, momentum, energy, spin along an axis — are represented by linear operators on ℋ: machines that eat a ket and return a ket, respecting superposition. The physically meaningful ones are Hermitian (self-adjoint): Â = Â†, equal to their own conjugate-transpose. Hermiticity buys, via the spectral theorem, exactly the two properties a measurable quantity needs:

  • all eigenvalues are real — these are the possible measurement results;
  • eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and together the eigenvectors furnish a complete basis — the measurement's outcome states.

The eigenvalue equation Â|a⟩ = a|a⟩ thus carries the whole physical interpretation: measure A on a system in state |ψ⟩ and you get eigenvalue a with probability |⟨a|ψ⟩|². Statistics follow compactly; the mean of many runs is the expectation value

⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|Â|ψ⟩ = ∑a a |⟨a|ψ⟩|² EXPECTATION

and the spread is ΔA = √⟨A²⟩ − ⟨A⟩². An eigenstate of Â is precisely a state with ΔA = 0 — a state in which the quantity A has a definite value. Most states are not eigenstates of most observables; that a quantity can simply fail to have a value in a given state is quantum mechanics' sharpest break with classical thinking, and Chapter 5 returns to it armed with Bell's theorem.

2.4The rules on one card

Postulate I — states

An isolated system is completely described by a normalised ket |ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space ℋ. Superpositions of states are states.

Postulate II — observables

Every measurable quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator Â on ℋ; the possible results of a measurement are its eigenvalues.

Postulate III — the Born rule

Measuring A on |ψ⟩ yields eigenvalue a with probability P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|² (more generally ⟨ψ|P̂a|ψ⟩ with P̂a the projector onto the eigenspace).

Postulate IV — collapse

Immediately after that result, the state is the (normalised) projection of |ψ⟩ onto the eigenspace of a: measurement is repeatable, and it updates the state. Whether this “collapse” is physics or bookkeeping is the measurement problem — stated honestly in Chapter 5.

Postulate V — dynamics

Between measurements the state evolves deterministically and linearly under the Schrödinger equation, iħ ∂t|ψ⟩ = Ĥ|ψ⟩, where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian — the energy observable.

Add one clause for composite systems (Chapter 4: the joint space is the tensor product ℋ1 ⊗ ℋ2) and one for identical particles, and this card is the entire theory. Everything else — wavefunctions, spin, entanglement, quantum fields, quantum dots — is these rules applied to particular Hilbert spaces and particular Hamiltonians.

2.5Incompatibility, made quantitative

Two observables can share a full set of eigenvectors — then a state can have definite values of both. The test is algebraic: complete compatibility holds exactly when the commutator vanishes,

[Â, B̂] ≡ ÂB̂ − B̂Â COMMUTATOR

When it does not vanish, no state is sharp in both, and the trade-off is exact. For any state, a short Cauchy–Schwarz argument (Robertson, 1929) gives

ΔA ΔB ≥ ½ |⟨[Â, B̂]⟩| ROBERTSON BOUND

Feed in the fundamental commutation relation inherited from Born and Jordan, [x̂, p̂] = iħ, and out drops Heisenberg's principle in its exact modern form:

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 UNCERTAINTY

Note what this is not. It is not about clumsy measuring instruments disturbing the particle — it is a theorem about the state itself, exactly as a sharply localised pulse of sound necessarily contains a broad band of frequencies. Position and momentum are Fourier-conjugate descriptions of one wavefunction (Chapter 3), and the bound is saturated only by Gaussian wave packets. The energy–time relation ΔE Δt ≳ ħ/2 is subtler — time is a parameter, not an operator, so there Δt means the timescale over which the state appreciably changes: sharp energies mean slow change, which is why long-lived atomic levels emit narrow spectral lines and short-lived ones emit broad “natural-linewidth” smears.

Why ħ runs the universe

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a structural law, and it does real load-bearing work: squeeze an electron into an atom-sized Δx ≈ 0.1 nm and it must carry momentum spread — hence kinetic energy of electron-volt scale. That zero-point pressure is what stops the electron spiralling into the nucleus, sets the size of atoms, and (Chapter 7) is exactly the effect that a quantum dot's size dials up and down to choose its colour.

2.6Motion: the state turns

Postulate V is a first-order, linear differential equation, so its solutions superpose and its evolution preserves inner products. Formally, for a time-independent Hamiltonian,

|ψ(t)⟩ = Û(t)|ψ(0)⟩,  Û(t) = e−iĤt/ħ,  Û†Û = 𝟙 UNITARY EVOLUTION

Evolution is a rigid rotation of the state vector in Hilbert space: lengths and angles — hence all probabilities — are shuffled but never leaked. (Unitarity is the quantum conservation of probability, and its apparent violation by black holes is one of the deepest open arguments in physics.) Expand in energy eigenstates Ĥ|En⟩ = En|En⟩ and the whole of dynamics becomes phases:

|ψ(t)⟩ = ∑n cn  e−iEnt/ħ|En⟩ PHASE CLOCKWORK

Each energy component spins its own little clock at rate En/ħ. An energy eigenstate only acquires a global phase — nothing observable moves, which is why they are called stationary states and why atoms in them do not radiate: Bohr's mysterious postulate, now a two-line theorem. All change, anywhere, is the drift of relative phase between energy components — the two-level beat in Chapter 3's box, the Larmor precession of a spin in Chapter 4, chemistry itself.

Two consequences knit the theory to older physics. Ehrenfest's theorem: expectation values obey Newton-like equations, d⟨x⟩/dt = ⟨p⟩/m and d⟨p⟩/dt = −⟨∂V/∂x⟩ — so well-localised wave packets ride classical trajectories, and the classical world survives as the theory's coarse-grained shadow. And conservation laws are commutators: any observable with [Ĥ, Â] = 0 has frozen statistics, d⟨A⟩/dt = (i/ħ)⟨[Ĥ,Â]⟩ = 0. Symmetries generate the conserved quantities — momentum generates translations, angular momentum rotations, the Hamiltonian time itself (Noether's insight, wearing quantum clothes; made exact by Wigner and Stone). Heisenberg's original formulation stands to Schrödinger's as two views of one rotation: states fixed and operators turning, dÂ/dt = (i/ħ)[Ĥ, Â] — identical predictions, and the form that survives into quantum field theory.

2.7The hydrogen atom of the formalism: the qubit

The smallest quantum system has a two-dimensional Hilbert space — a qubit. Its basis kets |0⟩ and |1⟩ might be spin up/down (Chapter 4), a photon's two polarisations, or the ground and excited states of an atom. The most general state, with the useless global phase spent, is

|ψ⟩ = cos (θ/2) |0⟩ + eiφ sin (θ/2) |1⟩ THE QUBIT

Two real angles: a point on a sphere (the Bloch sphere — poles are |0⟩ and |1⟩, the equator holds the equal superpositions, differing only in phase φ). Every statement in this chapter can be tried out here by hand, which is what the apparatus below is for: set the dial, predict |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = cos²(θ/2), then let the measurements fall where they may — singly random, collectively lawful.

Notice what the phase slider does not do: φ leaves P(0) and P(1) untouched. In the measurement basis, phase is invisible. But it is not idle — rotate the measurement (or evolve the state; they are the same rotation seen from opposite sides) and φ becomes the difference between constructive and destructive interference. A qubit's phase is where quantum computers hide their work, which is the subject of this almanac's companion, The Interference Engine.
