Chapter 2

The Machinery

Strip away the history and quantum mechanics is a surprisingly short list of rules — rules you could write on an index card and spend a lifetime unpacking. This chapter builds the machine one plain piece at a time: states pictured as arrows, measurements as questions, the rule for turning arrows into odds, why some questions can't both be answered at once, and the single equation that sets it all turning. (The full mathematics rides along in “Go deeper” panels whenever you want it.)

2.1The strange new bookkeeping

Classical probability adds like common sense. If an event can happen two ways, its probability is the sum of the probabilities of each way: P = P1 + P2. Quantum mechanics replaces this with a deeper rule, and every quantum phenomenon in this almanac is a corollary of it. To each way a process can happen, nature assigns not a probability but a probability amplitude — a complex number, an arrow in the plane with a length and a direction. The rules of the game:

  1. Alternatives add as amplitudes. If the ways are indistinguishable in principle — nothing anywhere records which occurred — add the arrows tip-to-tail: ψ = ψ1 + ψ2.
  2. Probability is the squared length of the final arrow: P = |ψ|². This is the Born rule.
  3. Distinguishable alternatives add as probabilities, classically: P = |ψ1|² + |ψ2|². The moment a record of “which way” exists, rule 1 is off.
  4. Sequential steps multiply amplitudes, as independent probabilities would.

Because arrows have direction, two ways can conspire (aligned: |ψ1+ψ2|² up to four times either alone) or cancel (opposed: exactly zero — a place the particle simply cannot land, though either route alone would happily take it there). That is interference, and it is why the double slit in the Overview does what it does — and why the fringes die when the detector switches on: the which-path record converts rule 1 into rule 3. No force is exerted; a bookkeeping condition changes, and the world changes with it.

Drag the phase δ to 180° with the arrows equal. Two perfectly good routes, each likely on its own — and together they add to exactly nothing. That impossible-in-everyday-life cancellation is interference. Now switch the alternatives to “recorded”: the moment a which-way record exists, the arrows stop adding and you get boring classical odds.

2.2A state is an arrow

Here is the single most useful picture in all of quantum mechanics. Take everything there is to know about a system — every amplitude, for every possible outcome — and bundle it into one object. That object behaves exactly like an arrow pointing somewhere in an abstract space of possibilities. That arrow is the system's state. Learn to think in arrows and half the theory becomes geometry.

Two things you can do with arrows, you can do with states, and both matter:

  • Stretch or shrink an arrow (here, by a complex number — a length and a twist).
  • Add two arrows tip-to-tail. Add two states and you get another perfectly good state — a superposition. This one rule, that states add, is the beating heart of the whole theory.
What's a bra and a ket?

Physicists write a state — our arrow — inside this bracket-and-bar: |ψ⟩. Read it aloud as “the state psi.” That's all it means: it is a name for an arrow, dressed in Paul Dirac's 1939 shorthand. The half-bracket is pure notation, no maths hiding in it.

The payoff comes when you want to compare two states. Dirac writes the mirror-image half, ⟨φ|, and calls it a bra (so |ψ⟩ is a ket — together they spell “bra-ket,” his little joke). Snap a bra onto a ket and the two halves close into a full bracket, ⟨φ|ψ⟩, which is a single number. That number answers one question: how much does the arrow ψ point along the arrow φ? — the overlap of the two states. If they're the same, it's 1. If they're completely different (“at right angles”), it's 0. In between, it's the amplitude from Section 2.1 — the arrow you square to get a probability.

That's the entire trick. A measurement offers a set of possible answers, and each answer is itself a state — a reference direction, like the axes of a graph. Ask “is the system in answer-state |k⟩?” and the amplitude is the overlap ⟨k|ψ⟩; the probability is that overlap squared. Line up all the answer-directions and any state is just a weighted sum of them:

|ψ⟩ = ∑k ck|k⟩,  ck = ⟨k|ψ⟩ = amplitude for answer k,  ∑k |ck|² = 1 A STATE, IN COMPONENTS

The weights ck are the amplitudes; their squares are the probabilities, and — since some answer must come up — the squares add to one. Crucially, a superposition is not a confession of ignorance (“it's really in one answer, I just don't know which”). The relative twists between the arrows produce genuine interference — the cancellation you watched in the figure above — which no “secretly one definite answer” story can fake. Nailing that difference down is a job for Chapter 5.

Made precise: the arrows live in a Hilbert space ℋ — a complex vector space with an inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩ (and, in infinite dimensions, “complete”: every convergent-looking sequence really converges, the fine print that keeps the calculus of Chapter 3 honest). For an n-outcome system a ket is a column of n complex numbers in ℂn; for a particle on a line it becomes the continuous wavefunction ψ(x). The inner product is the master operation:

⟨φ|ψ⟩ = ∑k φk* ψk  or  ∫ φ(x)*ψ(x) dx

A bra ⟨φ| is literally the conjugate-transpose (row) of its ket; the product is linear in the ket, conjugate-linear in the bra, and ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = ‖ψ‖² ≥ 0. Real states are normalised, ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1, so the probabilities of a complete set of answers total one. An overall phase is invisible — |ψ⟩ and eiα|ψ⟩ are the same physics — but relative phases inside a superposition are everything. A measurement's answer-directions form an orthonormal basis, ⟨j|k⟩ = δjk, and the object |k⟩⟨k| is the projector onto direction k. Summing them, Σk|k⟩⟨k| = 𝟙, gives the resolution of the identity — a “1” you can slot in anywhere to change basis, and the discrete ancestor of Feynman's path sum in Chapter 6.

2.3Every measurement is a question with special answers

Anything you can measure — position, momentum, energy, a spin's direction — comes with a menu of allowed answers, and each answer has its own state (its own arrow-direction). Physicists package a measurable quantity as an operator, written with a little hat: Â. Don't be spooked by the word — an operator is just a machine that takes a state in and gives a state out. What makes it a measurement is a special set of states it leaves pointing the same way, merely stretched. Those special states are the answer-states (the jargon is eigenstates), and the amount of stretch is the answer you'd read off the dial (the eigenvalue):

Â|a⟩ = a|a⟩  — “on answer-state |a⟩, measuring A gives the number a, guaranteed” THE ANSWER EQUATION

Now the payoff. Prepare any state |ψ⟩ and measure A. The possible readings are the answer-numbers a; the chance of each is the overlap-squared |⟨a|ψ⟩|² — the Born rule again, wearing the arrow picture. Two consequences worth pinning up:

  • If |ψ⟩ is one of the answer-states, that answer is certain — the quantity has a definite value. This is as classical as it gets.
  • If |ψ⟩ is a mix of answer-states, the quantity has no value at all until you measure — only odds. A property that simply fails to exist until asked for is quantum mechanics' rudest break with common sense, and Chapter 5 shows (via Bell) that it's really true, not just ignorance.

Average many runs and you get the expectation value ⟨A⟩ — the long-run mean reading — with a spread ΔA around it. That spread is exactly zero for an answer-state and grows the more mixed the state is; it's the star of the next section.

2.4The rules on one card

Everything so far can be written as five short rules. This really is the whole theory — memorise the card and the rest of physics is just working out what it implies for particular systems. In plain terms: (I) a system is an arrow; (II) each measurement is a question with a menu of answer-states; (III) the odds of each answer are the overlap-squared; (IV) asking the question snaps the arrow onto the answer you got; (V) left alone, the arrow turns smoothly and predictably. The formal versions:

Postulate I — states

An isolated system is completely described by a normalised ket |ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space ℋ. Superpositions of states are states.

Postulate II — observables

Every measurable quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator Â on ℋ; the possible results of a measurement are its eigenvalues.

Postulate III — the Born rule

Measuring A on |ψ⟩ yields eigenvalue a with probability P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|² (more generally ⟨ψ|P̂a|ψ⟩ with P̂a the projector onto the eigenspace).

Postulate IV — collapse

Immediately after that result, the state is the (normalised) projection of |ψ⟩ onto the eigenspace of a: measurement is repeatable, and it updates the state. Whether this “collapse” is physics or bookkeeping is the measurement problem — stated honestly in Chapter 5.

Postulate V — dynamics

Between measurements the state evolves deterministically and linearly under the Schrödinger equation, iħ ∂t|ψ⟩ = Ĥ|ψ⟩, where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian — the energy observable.

Add one clause for composite systems (Chapter 4: the joint space is the tensor product ℋ1 ⊗ ℋ2) and one for identical particles, and this card is the entire theory. Everything else — wavefunctions, spin, entanglement, quantum fields, quantum dots — is these rules applied to particular Hilbert spaces and particular Hamiltonians.

2.5Why you can't know everything at once

Some pairs of questions get along: they share the same answer-states, so a system can have a definite value of both at once. Others don't — and quantum mechanics is precise about which. Position and momentum are the famous quarrelling pair. There is no state in which a particle has both a sharp position and a sharp momentum; pin one down and the other blurs, on a strict budget:

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 HEISENBERG'S UNCERTAINTY PRINCIPLE

Here Δx is the spread in position and Δp the spread in momentum; their product can never dip below half of ħ. The crucial thing to get right: this is not about clumsy instruments knocking the particle as they measure it. It is a fact about the state itself — the very same fact that a musical note played for an instant has no definite pitch. A pure tone needs time to be a pitch; a sharply-located particle simply has no single momentum, in the same way. (Position and momentum turn out to be two views of one wavefunction, related by the same maths that ties a sound's timing to its pitch — Chapter 3.)

The order of position and momentum matters by exactly [x̂, p̂] = iħ (the one relation Born and Jordan distilled from Heisenberg's matrices in 1925), and dropping that in gives ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2, saturated only by Gaussian wave packets. The energy–time version ΔEΔt ≳ ħ/2 is subtler — time is a parameter, not an operator, so Δt means the timescale over which the state actually changes: sharp energies mean slow change, which is why long-lived atomic levels emit razor-thin spectral lines and fleeting ones emit broad smears.

Why ħ runs the universe

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 is a structural law, and it does real load-bearing work: squeeze an electron into an atom-sized Δx ≈ 0.1 nm and it must carry momentum spread — hence kinetic energy of electron-volt scale. That zero-point pressure is what stops the electron spiralling into the nucleus, sets the size of atoms, and (Chapter 7) is exactly the effect that a quantum dot's size dials up and down to choose its colour.

2.6Motion: the arrow turns

Leave a system alone and its arrow doesn't sit still — it turns, smoothly and predictably, like the hand of a clock. There's no randomness here at all: between measurements, quantum mechanics is perfectly deterministic (all the chance is in Postulate IV, the measurement step). And the turning never changes the arrow's length, so the probabilities always keep adding to one — the theory can neither leak certainty away nor conjure it from nowhere.

Go deeperunitary evolution, and why probability is conserved

Postulate V is a first-order, linear equation, so for a constant Hamiltonian the solution is a single “turn” operator applied to the starting state:

|ψ(t)⟩ = Û(t)|ψ(0)⟩,  Û(t) = e−iĤt/ħ,  Û†Û = 𝟙

Û is unitary — a rigid rotation of the arrow that preserves all lengths and angles, hence all probabilities. This is quantum mechanics' law of conservation of probability; whether black holes secretly break it is one of the deepest open questions in physics.

The easiest way to see the turning: sort the state into its pure-energy pieces — states of one definite energy En. Each piece simply spins in place, at a rate set by its energy:

|ψ(t)⟩ = ∑n cn  e−iEnt/ħ|En⟩ PHASE CLOCKWORK

Each energy component spins its own little clock at rate En/ħ. An energy eigenstate only acquires a global phase — nothing observable moves, which is why they are called stationary states and why atoms in them do not radiate: Bohr's mysterious postulate, now a two-line theorem. All change, anywhere, is the drift of relative phase between energy components — the two-level beat in Chapter 3's box, the Larmor precession of a spin in Chapter 4, chemistry itself.

This same turning is the hidden bridge back to ordinary physics — and it's where symmetries turn into conservation laws.

Two results knit quantum mechanics to older physics. Ehrenfest's theorem: the average position and momentum obey Newton-like equations, d⟨x⟩/dt = ⟨p⟩/m and d⟨p⟩/dt = −⟨∂V/∂x⟩ — so a well-localised wave packet rides a classical trajectory, and the everyday world survives as the theory's coarse-grained shadow. And conservation laws are commutators: any observable that commutes with the Hamiltonian, [Ĥ, Â] = 0, has statistics frozen in time. Symmetries generate the conserved quantities — translations give momentum, rotations give angular momentum, time-shifts give energy (Noether's insight in quantum dress). Finally, Heisenberg's original matrix mechanics is just the same rotation seen from the other side: hold the state still and turn the operators instead, dÂ/dt = (i/ħ)[Ĥ, Â] — identical predictions, and the form that carries into quantum field theory.

2.7The simplest quantum system: the qubit

The smallest quantum system of all has just two answer-states. Call them |0⟩ and |1⟩ — they could be a spin pointing up or down (Chapter 4), a photon polarised two ways, or an atom in its ground or excited state. It's called a qubit, and it's the fruit fly of quantum mechanics: simple enough to hold in your head, rich enough to show off everything in this chapter. Its most general state is a blend of the two, with a size for each and a relative phase between them:

|ψ⟩ = cos (θ/2) |0⟩ + eiφ sin (θ/2) |1⟩ THE QUBIT

Two real angles: a point on a sphere (the Bloch sphere — poles are |0⟩ and |1⟩, the equator holds the equal superpositions, differing only in phase φ). Every statement in this chapter can be tried out here by hand, which is what the apparatus below is for: set the dial, predict |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = cos²(θ/2), then let the measurements fall where they may — singly random, collectively lawful.

Set the angle θ, then hit measure ×1 a few times — pure coin-flip, you can't predict any single click. Now hit measure ×100 and watch the tally lock onto the predicted odds. Single events are lawless; crowds of them are exact. Then drag the phase φ: the odds don't budge — phase hides from this measurement, biding its time until interference calls on it.

Notice what the phase slider does not do: φ leaves P(0) and P(1) untouched. In the measurement basis, phase is invisible. But it is not idle — rotate the measurement (or evolve the state; they are the same rotation seen from opposite sides) and φ becomes the difference between constructive and destructive interference. A qubit's phase is where quantum computers hide their work, which is the subject of this almanac's companion, The Interference Engine.
