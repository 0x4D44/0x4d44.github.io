Chapter 1

The Crisis, 1859–1927

Quantum theory was not an idea anybody wanted. It was forced, concession by concession, out of three stubborn experimental facts that classical physics could not digest: the colour of hot things, the way light knocks electrons off metal, and the barcode of lines in the light of atoms. This chapter follows the retreat from certainty — from a curve-fit made in a single evening in 1900 to the complete, strange, working theory of 1927.

1.1The colour of heat

Every warm body glows. A poker in the fire goes dull red, then orange, then — if the smith is showing off — white. By the mid-nineteenth century this glow was a precision industry: the German Bureau of Standards (the PTR in Berlin) wanted a rigorous standard for the new electric lamps, and physics wanted to know why the glow of everything, iron or clay or platinum, follows the same universal curve once you correct for surface emissivity.

Gustav Kirchhoff proved in 1859 that it must. For a perfect absorber — a black body, realised in the laboratory as a small hole in the wall of a heated cavity — the emitted spectrum can depend only on temperature, not on material. Finding that universal function became one of the sharpest, cleanest problems in physics, precisely because there was nothing material to hide behind: any correct theory of heat and light had to produce it.

The thermodynamicists got impressively far without knowing what light was made of. Josef Stefan (1879, empirically) and Ludwig Boltzmann (1884, from Maxwell's radiation pressure) fixed the total output:

P/A = σT4,  σ = 5.670 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4 STEFAN–BOLTZMANN

— double a body's absolute temperature and it radiates sixteen times the power. Wilhelm Wien (1893) proved with a thought experiment about slowly compressed cavities that the peak of the spectrum slides with temperature:

λmaxT = 2.898 × 10−3 m·K WIEN DISPLACEMENT

which is why the 5,772 K Sun peaks in the green-yellow around 500 nm while a 300 K human peaks at 10 μm, in the far infrared. But the shape of the curve refused to yield. Wien guessed a distribution (1896) that fitted the blue side beautifully and sagged in the infrared. Worse, the honest classical calculation — count the electromagnetic standing-wave modes in the cavity, give each the equipartition energy kBT — gives the Rayleigh–Jeans law:

Bλ(T) = 2c kBTλ4 RAYLEIGH–JEANS

The λ4 in the denominator is a catastrophe. Ever-shorter wavelengths pack ever more modes into the cavity, each demanding its kBT; the predicted energy diverges without limit in the ultraviolet. Taken seriously, classical physics says a warm oven should be a lethal source of X-rays, and that matter in thermal equilibrium with radiation cannot exist at all. Paul Ehrenfest later christened this the ultraviolet catastrophe — not a fringe puzzle but a proof, from within, that classical physics was broken.

1.2Planck's act of desperation

Max Planck was forty-two, a thermodynamicist of deep conservatism who had spent years trying to derive the blackbody curve from entropy arguments alone. On 7 October 1900 the experimenters Rubens and Kurlbaum told him over Sunday tea that his latest formula (and Wien's) failed in the far infrared. That evening, by what he called “a fortunate guess”, he interpolated between the two known limits and mailed the result on a postcard. It fitted everything, at every wavelength, to within experimental error. It still does:

Bλ(T) = 2hc2λ5 1ehc/λkBT − 1 PLANCK, 19 OCT 1900

A formula that fits is not a theory. Planck spent the next eight weeks — “the most strenuous work of my life” — trying to derive it, and found only one route through: treat the energy of the charged oscillators in the cavity walls not as a continuum but as a stack of discrete parcels, each of size

E = hν,  h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s THE QUANTUM, 14 DEC 1900

Count the ways of distributing P such parcels among N oscillators (Boltzmann's combinatorics, which Planck had distrusted all his life), maximise the entropy, and Planck's law falls out. The high-frequency modes are rescued because an oscillator whose minimum parcel hν far exceeds kBT almost never gets funded at all: the exponential Boltzmann factor e−hν/kBT freezes the ultraviolet out. Equipartition — each mode an equal share — was the broken assumption.

Planck presented the derivation to the German Physical Society on 14 December 1900, the conventional birthday of quantum theory. He regarded the quantisation as “a purely formal assumption” — bookkeeping to be dissolved later — and described the whole thing as “an act of desperation”. He spent years afterwards trying to smuggle continuity back in. The constant he had wrung from the data, however, was now loose in the world, and it appears in essentially every equation for the rest of this almanac. Its small size is the reason the quantum world was hidden for so long: h sets the scale at which action comes in grains, and 10−34 J·s is invisible at the scale of pendulums and planets.

Two constants for the price of one

Fitting his law to the data gave Planck not just h but kB — the first accurate value of Boltzmann's constant, hence of Avogadro's number, hence of the charge on the electron (via Faraday's constant), five years before Einstein's Brownian-motion paper made atoms respectable. Planck considered this chain of numbers, not the quantum, the great result of his work.

1.3Einstein takes the quantum seriously

For five years nobody — including Planck — believed energy itself was grainy. The revolutionary step came in March 1905, from a 26-year-old examiner at the Bern patent office. Einstein's “heuristic” paper (the one he himself called revolutionary, in a year that also produced special relativity) argued from the entropy of high-frequency radiation that light in a cavity behaves thermodynamically as if it consisted of independent quanta of energy hν — not as a wave that merely exchanges energy in lumps, but grains of light. G. N. Lewis would later (1926) name them photons.

The test case was the photoelectric effect, discovered by Hertz (1887) and mapped by Lenard (1902): ultraviolet light ejects electrons from metal. Classically, a brighter wave carries more energy, so brighter light should eject faster electrons — and very dim light should need hours to pool enough energy to eject any. Neither is true. Einstein's photon picture makes the accounting trivial: one photon in, one electron out. The photon pays the metal's exit toll — the work function W — and the electron keeps the change:

Kmax = hν − W EINSTEIN, 1905

  • Below the threshold ν₀ = W/h, no electrons — however intense the light. The individual photon simply cannot pay the toll.
  • Above it, the maximum electron energy climbs linearly with frequency, with universal slope h — the same constant from the oven curve, in a completely different experiment.
  • Intensity buys more photons, hence more electrons — but not faster ones. And emission is immediate, even in near-darkness.

Robert Millikan found the photon idea so “reckless” that he spent ten years building what he described as a machine shop in vacuum to disprove the linear law — and in 1916 confirmed it exactly, measuring h to half a percent. (He still called the underlying theory untenable; the data did not care.) Einstein's 1921 Nobel citation names the photoelectric law, not relativity.

1.4The atom gets a ladder

The second classical scandal was the atom itself. Rutherford's scattering experiments (1911) had revealed a miniature solar system: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with electrons somewhere outside. Classically this atom is a death trap. An orbiting electron accelerates; accelerating charges radiate; the electron should spiral into the nucleus in about 16 picoseconds, chirping a continuous smear of radiation as it falls. Matter should not exist, and its light should have no lines.

But atomic light is nothing but lines. Heat hydrogen and it emits a precise barcode — red at 656.3 nm, blue-green at 486.1, violet at 434.0 and 410.2 — whose wavelengths a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, had fitted in 1885 with a formula involving integers. Generalised by Rydberg:

1λ = RH ( 1nf2 − 1ni2),  RH = 1.0968 × 107 m−1 RYDBERG, 1888

Integers, in a spectrum. Niels Bohr, working in Rutherford's Manchester department in 1913, made the leap: perhaps the electron may only occupy orbits in which its angular momentum is a whole multiple of Planck's grain, L = nħ. In those orbits — postulate, not explanation — it does not radiate. Combining the quantisation rule with ordinary Coulomb mechanics gives allowed radii rn = n2a0 (with a0 = 0.0529 nm, the Bohr radius) and allowed energies

En = − mee48ε02h2 1n2 = −13.606 eVn2 BOHR, 1913

Light is emitted only when the electron jumps, and the photon carries exactly the difference: hν = Ei − Ef. Rydberg's formula falls out with RH computed correctly from me, e, ε0 and h — no fitting. When Bohr then explained a mystery series in the star ζ Puppis as helium ions (with the nuclear charge and reduced mass scaled correctly), Einstein called it “one of the greatest discoveries”. Franck and Hertz clinched the discreteness in 1914 by driving electrons through mercury vapour: the current dips every 4.9 V, the atoms accepting energy only in exact level-sized bites, and the vapour glows at precisely the ultraviolet line that a 4.9 eV drop predicts.

Yet the Bohr model is a half-way house — a classical planet with quantum bolt-ons. It fails for helium's two electrons, says nothing about why some lines are bright and others faint, and its sharp orbits will turn out to violate the very theory it midwifed. It earns its place here as the decisive proof that the states of matter are discrete: atoms have ladders, and spectroscopy is the sound of electrons falling down them.

1.5Waves of matter

Two more dominoes fell before the real theory arrived. In 1923 Arthur Compton scattered X-rays off electrons in graphite and found the scattered light shifted to longer wavelength by exactly the recoil formula for a billiard-ball collision with a massless particle of energy hν and momentum h/λ:

Δλ = hmec(1 − cos θ) COMPTON, 1923

with h/mec = 2.43 pm, the Compton wavelength of the electron. After this, the photon's momentum was not negotiable.

Then, in his 1924 Paris thesis, Louis de Broglie ran the logic backwards with aristocratic audacity: if waves of light are also particles, perhaps particles of matter are also waves, with the same dictionary,

λ = hp DE BROGLIE, 1924

The idea instantly demystifies Bohr's rule: an electron wave wrapped around a circular orbit must close on itself, nλ = 2πr — which is L = nħ. The examining committee, unsure whether the thesis was genius or nonsense, sent it to Einstein, who replied that de Broglie had “lifted a corner of the great veil”. Proof came in 1927 from two directions at once: Davisson and Germer in New York diffracted slow electrons off a nickel crystal, and G. P. Thomson in Aberdeen diffracted fast ones through metal foil, both finding Bragg peaks at exactly h/p. (Physics enjoys the symmetry: J. J. Thomson won the Nobel for showing the electron is a particle; his son George for showing it is a wave.) A 100 eV electron has λ ≈ 0.12 nm — atomic spacing — which is why electron microscopes resolve what light never can, and why fifty-kilodalton molecules have since been interfered through gratings.

1.6Five feverish years

By 1924 the “old quantum theory” was a heap of brilliant patches — quantisation rules grafted onto classical orbits, each success shadowed by a failure. The real theory arrived in a rush between 1925 and 1927, built twice over by two different routes that turned out to be the same thing.

Matrix mechanics — Helgoland, June 1925

Werner Heisenberg, twenty-three, exiled to the grassless North Sea island of Helgoland by hay fever, resolved to expel everything unobservable from atomic physics — above all the electron's orbit, which no experiment had ever seen. What experiments see are transitions: frequencies and intensities, indexed by two states, initial and final. So Heisenberg replaced each classical quantity by an array of transition amplitudes — x becomes a table xmn — and worked out how such tables must multiply for the physics to be consistent. Around 3 a.m. one June morning, with the energy theorem checking out line by line, he was, he wrote, too excited to sleep and climbed a rock at the island's tip to wait for sunrise. Back in Göttingen, Max Born recognised Heisenberg's rule as matrix multiplication, and with Pascual Jordan distilled the theory's entire novelty into one line — position and momentum matrices that do not commute:

x̂ p̂ − p̂ x̂ = iħ BORN–JORDAN, 1925

Order of operations now matters in nature's arithmetic — the mathematical seed of the uncertainty principle, and (as the next chapter shows) very nearly the whole theory. Wolfgang Pauli proved the machinery real by grinding out the full hydrogen spectrum from it within months.

Wave mechanics — the Alpine Christmas, 1925–26

Erwin Schrödinger, nearly forty and sceptical of Göttingen's austere matrices, took de Broglie's waves seriously instead: if the electron is a wave, there should be a wave equation. Over Christmas 1925 in the Alps (and in a legendary burst of six papers in six months) he found it. For a particle of mass m in a potential V:

iħ ∂ψ∂t = − ħ22m ∂2ψ∂x2 + Vψ SCHRÖDINGER, 1926

Solve it for the Coulomb potential and the hydrogen levels −13.6 eV/n2 emerge not from postulated orbits but as the discrete frequencies of a confined wave — quantisation as resonance, integers appearing for the same reason a violin string has harmonics. Physicists embraced the familiar differential equation with relief; Schrödinger himself hoped ψ was a real physical wave, matter smeared smoothly through space, and that the granular “jumps” would dissolve. Then Schrödinger (and Pauli, and Eckart) proved the awkward thing: wave mechanics and matrix mechanics are mathematically identical — one theory in two costumes. Whatever ψ was, it was not an ordinary wave: for two particles it lives in six-dimensional configuration space, and it is irreducibly complex-valued.

What the wave is — Born, 1926

The answer that stuck came from Max Born, in a paper on collisions, almost as a footnote: ψ is a wave of probability amplitude. Its squared magnitude |ψ(x)|² is the probability density of finding the particle at x — the particle itself is always found whole, at one place, like the dots on the screen in the Overview. Nature, on this reading, is lawful about statistics and silent about individuals. Born's rule completed the working theory; it also opened the interpretational argument that Chapter 5 inherits, and earned Born his Nobel only in 1954, twenty-eight years late.

Uncertainty and Solvay — 1927

In February 1927 Heisenberg, walking at night in the park behind Bohr's institute, saw what the non-commuting matrices meant physically: no state of affairs can make both terms of x̂p̂ − p̂x̂ = iħ sharp at once. Position and momentum are not merely unknowable together; they are not jointly defined,

Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 HEISENBERG, 1927

(the modern tight form; the derivation from commutators is in Chapter 2). That October the new physics assembled at the fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels — seventeen of the twenty-nine attendees held or would hold Nobel prizes — and the famous duel began over the breakfast table: each morning Einstein produced a thought experiment to beat the uncertainty principle; each evening Bohr found the flaw. “God does not play dice,” Einstein grumbled; “Einstein, stop telling God what to do,” Bohr — by most accounts — replied. Einstein never disputed that the theory worked; his real objection, sharpened over years into the EPR argument of Chapter 5, was that it could not be the whole story. The theory shipped anyway. By 1928 Dirac had married it to special relativity and predicted antimatter; by 1930 the textbooks were being written; and physics began the long project — still running — of finding out what it had built.
