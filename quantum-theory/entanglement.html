Chapter 5

Measurement & Entanglement

Here the theory turns and looks at itself. Its dynamics are smooth, deterministic and linear; its measurements are abrupt, random and unique. Its states for two particles can encode correlations that no story of pre-existing local facts can reproduce — a claim that stopped being philosophy in 1964 and became an experiment, run in this chapter, live. This is where quantum mechanics is strangest, and where it has been tested hardest.

5.1The seam in the theory

Chapter 2 left a seam showing. Postulate V says states evolve unitarily — smoothly, reversibly, superpositions preserved. Postulate IV says that when a measurement happens the state jumps, once, irreversibly, to a single outcome. But a measuring device is itself a swarm of atoms obeying Postulate V; run the unitary rules on system-plus-apparatus and you get no jump at all — only an apparatus steered into superposition along with the system it measured: “pointer left” + “pointer right”. Where, then, is the line past which one outcome becomes the outcome? This is the measurement problem, and it is not a student's confusion: it is an open structural question about which serious physicists still disagree (§5.7).

Schrödinger dramatised the seam in 1935 with his notorious cat — sealed with a radioactive trigger so that the unitary rules, applied all the way up, produce a superposition of dead-and-alive. His point, usually mangled: not that cats are half-dead — he called the case “ridiculous” — but that the formalism, unamended, refuses to stop the superposition anywhere on the way from atom to cat. The same 1935 paper coined the word for this chapter's real subject: Verschränkung — entanglement — which Schrödinger judged “the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought.”

5.2Superposition is not ignorance

To even state these questions cleanly you need the distinction the density matrix makes precise. A system genuinely in the superposition (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and a system that is either |0⟩ or |1⟩ with even odds give identical statistics if you only ever measure in the {|0⟩,|1⟩} basis — and completely different statistics the moment you measure anything diagonal to it. The bookkeeping object ρ = Σkpk|ψk⟩⟨ψk| carries exactly this difference in its off-diagonal elements — the coherences:

The left one interferes; the right one is a classical coin already tossed. Two facts about ρ do heavy lifting below. First, a subsystem of an entangled pair, described alone (by the partial trace), is a mixture — maximally entangled pairs have subsystems that are pure static, all correlation and no local content. Second, “losing the phases to the environment” means exactly: the off-diagonals of the system's ρ decay. That is decoherence (§5.6), and it will get us most — but provably not all — of the way across the seam.

5.3EPR: Einstein's best shot

Einstein's discomfort, honed for years after Solvay, landed in May 1935 as the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paper. In Bohm's sharper spin version: prepare two electrons in the singlet state of Chapter 4,

|Ψ⁻⟩ = (|↑↓⟩ − |↓↑⟩)/√2 THE SINGLET

and fly them to laboratories a light-hour apart. Measure either spin along any axis: the results are perfectly anticorrelated, always. Now, says Einstein: measuring particle 1 cannot physically disturb distant particle 2 (locality). Yet by choosing my axis at station 1, I can predict with certainty either the z-spin or the x-spin of particle 2 without touching it. Anything predictable-with-certainty without disturbance must correspond to something real already at particle 2 — an “element of reality”. But quantum mechanics says no state carries definite z-spin and x-spin at once (§4.2). Therefore, EPR concluded, the quantum description is incomplete — there must be deeper variables the theory omits, and the spooky correlations (“spukhafte Fernwirkung”) are no spookier than finding one glove in each of two parcels: determined at the source, revealed at the bench.

It is a beautiful argument, and for thirty years it read like untestable metaphysics: hidden determinism versus irreducible chance, matter of taste. The plot twist is that it is testable — the gloves picture makes a quantitative commitment, and the quantum singlet breaks it.

5.4Bell's theorem: the universe takes sides

John Bell's 1964 insight: any theory of the EPR type — outcomes fixed by local variables λ carried from the source, each station's result depending only on its own setting and λ — obeys strict bounds on how strongly the two stations' statistics can correlate across several setting choices at once. In the CHSH form (Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt, 1969): each station picks between two analyser angles (a or a′ here; b or b′ there); outcomes are ±1; E denotes the average product. Local realism demands, for every such theory,

S = E(a,b) + E(a′,b) + E(a′,b′) − E(a,b′)  |S| ≤ 2 CHSH BOUND

(The proof is two lines: for fixed λ, B + B′ and B − B′ are ±2 and 0 in some order, so A(B+B′) + A′(B−B′) = ±2; averaging over λ cannot exceed what every term respects.) Quantum mechanics, for polarisation-entangled photons, predicts E = cos 2(a−b) — and at the canny angles 0°, 45°, 22.5°, 67.5° that stacks up to

SQM = 2√2 ≈ 2.828 TSIRELSON'S MAXIMUM

Not a paradox — a fork in the road, decidable by counting coincidences. Run the experiment below and watch which way the universe votes.

The laboratory verdict is unambiguous and now spans half a century: Freedman and Clauser (1972, calcium cascade photons), Aspect and colleagues (1982, settings switched while the photons were in flight, closing the locality loophole in spirit), and in 2015 three loophole-free tests at once — Delft (entangled electron spins 1.3 km apart), NIST and Vienna (photons, with detection efficiency high enough to close the fair-sampling loophole, and setting choices by independent fast random-number generators). All violate Bell's bound by dozens of standard deviations, in exquisite agreement with 2√2's predictions at the chosen angles. The 2022 Nobel Prize went to Clauser, Aspect and Zeilinger for precisely this programme. The conclusion is not “quantum mechanics is weird” but a theorem about the world, independent of quantum theory: no theory in which measurement outcomes are fixed by local pre-existing facts can reproduce the observed counts. Einstein's gloves do not exist. Something in locality, or in pre-existing values, or in our notions of inference itself, has to give.

5.5What the magic cannot do

Entanglement looks like a telegraph and is not one. The no-signalling theorem: nothing Alice chooses to do alters Bob's local statistics — his ρ is untouched by her choice of measurement (the correlations are visible only when the two records are brought together, at light speed or slower, and compared). Relativity and quantum mechanics dovetail exactly where they seem to grind. The no-cloning theorem (Wootters–Żurek, 1982; provable in three lines from linearity) forbids copying an unknown quantum state — which is why quantum information behaves like nothing classical: it cannot be backed up, eavesdropping necessarily leaves fingerprints (the basis of quantum key distribution, deployed commercially today), and “reading” a state generally destroys it. What entanglement does enable, with a classical side-channel: teleportation of an unknown state (Bennett et al. 1993, demonstrated from ground to satellite in 2017), dense coding, and the correlation fuel of quantum computing. Entanglement is a resource — a strictly non-classical one — not a telephone.

5.6Decoherence: why the world looks classical

Why do we never meet a cat in two states, if the linear rules allow it? The modern answer is quantitative. A macroscopic superposition does not live in a vacuum: air molecules, thermal photons, cosmic microwaves scatter off it, and every scattering event carries away which-branch information — the environment is a which-path detector nobody installed (Zeh, Żurek, from 1970 on). Trace the environment out and the system's off-diagonal terms are multiplied by overlap factors that crash toward zero at astonishing speed:

Decoherence also picks out which basis survives — the pointer states, selected by what the environment couples to (usually position: that is why the classical world is a world of places). This explains with full quantitative success why interference between macroscopically distinct alternatives is unobservable, why bigger is more classical, and where the quantum/classical boundary sits in practice: it is not a boundary but an exponential gradient. What decoherence does not do, by itself, is choose an outcome — it converts “coherent both” into “statistical either” with perfect grace, and leaves the final “this one, in fact” exactly where Postulate IV left it. The seam is narrowed, relocated, illuminated — not sewn.

5.7The interpretations, priced honestly

All of the following reproduce every confirmed experiment above. They differ in what they charge you for it. (Bell's theorem is the menu's fine print: each must give up locality, definite pre-existing values, a single outcome, or observer-independence of facts — something.)

Where this leaves the reader

Nothing in the rest of this almanac depends on the choice. The formalism's predictions — including every device in Chapters 6 and 7 — are interpretation-proof. That a theory can be this precise about experience and this contested about reality is itself the deepest datum we have about the world; hold the question open, and hold the calculations tight.
