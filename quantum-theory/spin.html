Chapter 4

Spin & Identity

The electron carries a property with no classical ancestor: an intrinsic angular momentum that comes in exactly two values along any axis you choose to ask about, and that needs two full turns to come back to itself. Combine spin with the perfect indistinguishability of identical particles and you get the deepest sorting of matter there is — bosons and fermions — and, from one austere rule, the periodic table, the rigidity of your desk, and the fate of dying stars.

4.1The experiment that split the beam

In Frankfurt in 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach fired a beam of silver atoms through a strongly non-uniform magnetic field. A magnetic moment in such a field feels a net force proportional to its component μz along the gradient. Classically the atoms' moments should point every which way, so the beam should smear into a continuous band. What appeared on the glass plate — legend says only after Stern's sulphurous cigar smoke helped develop the faint silver deposit — was two sharp spots. Not a smear; not three or five spots; two.

The measured component came out as ±ħ/2 and nothing else, along whatever axis the magnet defined. This is space quantisation at its starkest: ask any direction, and the answer is one bit. The effect does not come from orbital motion — silver's single valence electron sits in an s-state with zero orbital angular momentum (§3.5). It took until 1925 for two Dutch students, Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck, to name the culprit: the electron possesses an intrinsic angular momentum, spin, of magnitude s = ½. The word is a trap and everyone falls into it once: nothing rotates. A spinning charged ball of any plausible electron size would need its equator moving faster than light to make the measured moment; the electron is, as far as every experiment can tell, a point. Spin is angular momentum the particle simply has, as it has charge — and in Dirac's 1928 relativistic equation it emerges automatically, unasked, with the right magnitude.

4.2Spin is the electron's private qubit

Two answers, up or down, along any axis you pick — that's exactly the two-answer system, the qubit, from §2.7. So spin is the electron's built-in qubit, and everything you learned at the qubit dial applies. Write |↑⟩ and |↓⟩ for up and down along the vertical (z) axis. A general spin state is a blend of the two — and here's the twist that makes spin interesting.

The three axes — x, y, z — are quarrelling questions in the sense of §2.5: no state can have a definite spin along two of them at once. An electron that is definitely up along z is a perfect 50/50 blur along x. In symbols, “pointing right” is an equal superposition of up and down: |→⟩ = (|↑⟩ + |↓⟩)/√2. Ask such an electron whether it's up or down and it's a coin flip. The apparatus below turns that arithmetic into beams of real atoms you can split and re-split.

Go deeperspinors, the Pauli matrices, the rotation algebra

The spin state is a two-component complex vector, a spinor, and the spin observables are Ŝi = (ħ/2)σi built from the Pauli matrices:

They obey [Ŝx, Ŝy] = iħŜz and cyclic — the algebra of rotations itself — so no two components commute, and the Robertson bound (§2.5) forbids any definite spin direction in the classical sense. The right-pointing states are |±x⟩ = (|↑⟩ ± |↓⟩)/√2, and measuring σz on them gives ±1 with even odds.

Two more properties complete the electron's dossier. First, the magnetic moment tied to spin is μ = g(eħ/4me) with g ≈ 2 — twice the classical gyromagnetic ratio, again delivered free by Dirac's equation (its measured excess over 2, one part in 800, is Chapter 6's crown jewel). In a magnetic field the spin's phases drift (§2.6) and ⟨S⟩ precesses at the Larmor frequency — for protons, 42.58 MHz per tesla, and that precession, mapped in water, is the entire physical basis of MRI. Second, and stranger: rotating a spinor by 360° multiplies it by −1; only after 720° does it return. A phase of −1 is invisible on one particle but real in interference — neutron interferometers have rotated one arm's spin through 360° and watched the fringes flip, exactly as the half-integer mathematics demands.

Set the three magnets to z → x → z. The first filters to spin-up. The last asks “up or down?” again — and half fail. The middle magnet, by pinning down the sideways spin, wiped out the up-ness you'd just established. Now set the middle one to “pass all (no record)” and the up-ness survives intact. Measuring isn't peeking — it changes the thing.

The cascade teaches the theory's rudest lesson. Filter spins to +z, then to +x, then ask about z again — and half come out −z. The middle measurement did not merely find the x-value; by rendering it definite it erased the previously definite z-value, because no state has both (the operators do not commute). And if the middle analyser passes both beams without recording which, the erasure never happens: recombine the paths coherently and the original polarisation marches through untouched. Measurement is not peeking. It is participation.

4.3Two of a kind: the exchange rule

Now the deepest fact about matter — and it comes from an idea so simple it sounds like a technicality: all electrons are exactly alike.

Every electron in the universe is identical to every other — same mass, same charge, same spin, to the last measured decimal. In everyday life, even “identical” twins can be told apart by watching which is which. But quantum particles have no trajectories to follow (§3.1), so there is genuinely no fact about which electron is which. Swapping two of them can't change anything you could ever measure. That single requirement has a razor-sharp consequence: swapping two identical particles may only multiply their shared state by +1 or −1 (swap twice and you must be back where you started). Nature uses both options — and sorts every particle in existence into two great families by which sign it takes:

The symmetrisation postulate

Bosons (integer spin: photons, pions, ⁴He atoms, the Higgs) take the +: the many-particle state is symmetric under any exchange, Ψ(2,1) = +Ψ(1,2).

Fermions (half-integer spin: electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks, ³He) take the −: the state is antisymmetric, Ψ(2,1) = −Ψ(1,2).

That spin decides the sign — the spin–statistics theorem — is provable only in relativistic quantum field theory (Pauli, 1940), one of the deepest “why”s the deeper theory answers.

The minus sign is the most consequential sign in science. Put two fermions in the same single-particle state and antisymmetry demands Ψ = −Ψ = 0: the configuration does not exist. This is the Pauli exclusion principle (1925): no two identical fermions may occupy one quantum state. Fermions are nature's individualists, stacking one per state up the energy ladder. Bosons are the opposite — the symmetric sum actually amplifies amplitudes for bunching (a factor √n+1 for joining n others), so bosons herd.

4.4One rule, one universe of consequences

Fermions build structure…

  • The periodic table is exclusion plus hydrogen's ladder. Each hydrogen-like orbital (n, l, m) takes two electrons — one per spin. Shells hold 2n²: 2, 8, 18, 32. Electrons stack upward, and the outermost, part-filled shell does all the chemistry: the rows and columns of Mendeleev's table are the bookkeeping of exclusion. Without the minus sign every electron would sink into the 1s state; every element would be a slight variation on hydrogen, and there would be no chemistry worth the name.
  • Solidity. Push two atoms together and their electron clouds are forced toward already-occupied states; exclusion resists with an effective pressure far stronger than electrostatics alone. That your hand does not pass through the table is, at bottom, antisymmetry.
  • Dead stars. Compress matter until electrons form a degenerate gas and exclusion supplies pressure even at absolute zero. That pressure holds up white dwarfs — to a point: Chandrasekhar (1930) showed relativity caps the trick at 1.4 solar masses. Beyond, collapse squeezes electrons into protons and neutron degeneracy pressure makes a last stand at nuclear density — a city-sized nucleus of a star — before gravity wins outright and a black hole forms. The tombstones of stars are classified by which fermion was holding the roof.

…bosons build intensity

  • The laser works because photons are bosons: stimulated emission is the √n+1 herding factor turned into an avalanche of identical, in-phase photons.
  • Bose–Einstein condensation: cool bosonic atoms below a microkelvin and a macroscopic fraction drops into the single ground state — a wavefunction you can photograph, first achieved with rubidium in 1995, seventy years after Einstein predicted it.
  • Superfluid helium and superconductivity: ⁴He below 2.17 K flows without viscosity; in metals, electrons — fermions — pair up (Cooper pairs, BCS 1957) into composite bosons and condense, conducting without resistance. The fermion/boson divide is so fundamental that ³He (fermion) and ⁴He (boson), which are chemically identical, superflow by completely different mechanisms a thousand times apart in temperature.

Composite identity

Whether a composite object herds or excludes depends on its total spin: count the half-integer constituents. Hydrogen (p + e⁻: two fermions) is a boson; deuterium (p + n + e⁻: three) is a fermion. Two isotopes of one element can thus obey opposite statistics — and measurably do, in their spectra and their condensates. Identity in quantum mechanics is arithmetic, not philosophy.

One loose end. The singlet combination of two spins-½, (|↑↓⟩ − |↓↑⟩)/√2 — total spin zero, antisymmetric, the state helium's two 1s electrons are locked in — has a property Einstein considered a reductio ad absurdum of the whole theory: neither spin has any state of its own, while the pair's anticorrelation is perfect along every axis at once. Whether that is absurdity or the fabric of the world is the next chapter.
