Chapter 7

Quantum Dots

A quantum dot is a semiconductor crystal so small — a few thousand atoms, a few nanometres — that the particle-in-a-box of Chapter 3 stops being a textbook exercise and becomes a manufacturing specification. Squeeze the crystal and its energy levels spread; its colour is set not by what it is made of but by how big it is. This is quantum mechanics you can hold up to a UV lamp: the same physics from Planck's ovens, now glowing in television screens, surgical theatres and quantum processors — and honoured with the 2023 Nobel Prize.

7.1From one atom to a crystal: bands

Start with what Chapters 3 and 4 built. One atom has sharp levels. Bring two atoms together and each level splits in two (the electrons can share symmetrically or antisymmetrically); bring together the ~10²² atoms of a real crystal and each atomic level fans out into a band of that many states, packed so densely they form a near-continuum. Filling the bands with electrons — two per state, Pauli insisting — decides everything about a material's electrical life:

  • In a metal the topmost occupied band is part-full: states are available at infinitesimal cost, and charge flows freely.
  • In a semiconductor the valence band is exactly full and the next — the conduction band — exactly empty, separated by a forbidden band gap Eg: 1.12 eV in silicon, 1.74 eV in cadmium selenide, a few electron-volts in insulators (the distinction is only of degree).

Promote an electron across the gap — thermally, electrically, or by absorbing a photon with hν ≥ Eg — and two mobile characters appear at once: the electron in the conduction band, and the bubble it left behind, the hole, which behaves in every respect like a positive particle (Dirac's antimatter logic, domesticated in a crystal). Both move through the periodic lattice not with the free electron's mass but with an effective mass m* set by the band's curvature — in CdSe the electron acts thirteen times lighter than a free electron. Remember Chapter 3: confinement energy scales as 1/m. Light effective masses mean quantum effects at sizes an experimentalist can actually reach.

One more character. The negative electron and positive hole attract, orbiting each other like a fragile, giant hydrogen atom called an exciton — but hydrogen rescaled by the crystal: the small effective masses and the semiconductor's strong dielectric screening (ε ≈ 10) inflate the Bohr radius of §3.5 from 0.05 nm to

aBexc = ε meμ a0  ≈  5.6 nm in CdSe, ~10 in InP, ~20 in PbS THE EXCITON'S NATURAL SIZE

That number is the whole plot. It is the size the electron–hole pair wants to be. Make a crystal smaller than it, and the pair is squeezed into a box tighter than its natural orbit: welcome to the strong confinement regime, where the box, not the Coulomb attraction, rules the energies — and rules them by Chapter 3's formula.

7.2The Brus equation: size becomes colour

Model the dot as a sphere of radius R with hard walls (the surrounding ligands and solvent are a high potential barrier). The particle-in-a-sphere ground state has energy π²ħ²/2m*R² — the 1/L² lever of §3.2 — and both carriers pay it. Add back the electron–hole attraction as a first-order correction (Brus, 1984) and the dot's optical gap is

Eg(R) = Egbulk + ħ2π22R2 (1me* + 1mh*) − 1.786 e24πε0εR BRUS, 1984

Read it as an engineer reads a datasheet:

  • The bulk gap sets the floor — the colour an infinitely large crystal would have. It chooses the neighbourhood (CdSe: red; InP: near-infrared; PbS: deep infrared).
  • The confinement term grows as 1/R² — the box term, doubled up because electron and hole are each confined. It is the tuning knob: shrink the dot and the gap opens, sliding the emission from red toward blue.
  • The Coulomb term shrinks only as 1/R, and enters with a minus sign — at small R the R² term always wins. Confinement beats attraction; the box beats the atom.

Emission runs the film backwards: an excited dot relaxes to its band edge in picoseconds (shedding heat), then the electron and hole annihilate — recombine — and the energy leaves as one photon at hν = Eg(R). One size, one colour; a distribution of sizes, a distribution of colours — which is why synthetic control (§7.4) is optical purity. The hard-wall model overestimates confinement a little (real walls are finite, and tunnelling tails leak out — §3.4 again), but it captures the law: in the lab as in the equation, CdSe marches from deep red at 5 nm radius to blue-green under 1.5, exactly the 1/R² march. Do the sum yourself

CdSe, R = 2 nm: bulk gap 1.74 eV; confinement 0.376/R² × (1/0.13 + 1/0.45) = 0.93 eV; Coulomb −2.57/(10.2 × 2) = −0.13 eV. Total ≈ 2.55 eV → λ = 1239.84/2.55 ≈ 490 nm: a two-nanometre dot of a red material glows blue-green. Every constant is in the Lexicon; the arithmetic is GCSE-level. That a child's television rests on it is the point of this almanac.

7.3Three discoveries of the same thing

Like much of quantum theory, the dot was found more than once, on both sides of the Iron Curtain, in two different states of matter.

In glass. Coloured filter glass — the deep reds and yellows of darkroom safelights and traffic signals — had been made for a century by growing tiny semiconductor crystallites (CdS, CdSe) inside molten glass, with an empirical folklore: the colour depended, mysteriously, on the heat treatment. In 1981 Alexei Ekimov at the Vavilov State Optical Institute in Leningrad, studying copper-chloride-doped glasses by X-ray scattering and absorption, proved the correlation: the smaller the crystallites (down to ~2 nm), the bluer the absorption edge — and with the theorist Alexander Efros he identified the cause as quantum size confinement. Generations of glassmakers had been tuning a quantum box by recipe, without knowing it.

In a flask. Independently, at Bell Labs in 1983, Louis Brus — studying colloidal CdS particles as photocatalysts — noticed that freshly-made small crystallites had measurably different spectra from the same material aged and grown slightly larger. He worked out the spherical-box theory above, putting the effect on a quantitative footing and, crucially, in solution — where chemistry could get at it.

Made to order. The field's third act was synthetic mastery: in 1993 Murray, Norris and Bawendi at MIT published the hot-injection method (§7.4) — monodisperse CdSe dots at any chosen size from a repeatable recipe. Size control to within a lattice constant turned a laboratory curiosity into a material platform; the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went jointly to Bawendi, Brus and Ekimov, the committee noting that they had “planted an important seed for nanotechnology” — and, unusually for a Nobel citation, that the products were already glowing in living rooms.

7.4Growing a box, three thousand atoms at a time

The hot-injection synthesis is crystal engineering with kitchen drama. Into a flask of high-boiling solvent (octadecene, say) with long-chain ligands (oleic acid, phosphines) at ~300 °C, inject a cold syringe of precursors — a cadmium salt and a selenium compound. The physics of the next ninety seconds decides everything, and it follows the classic LaMer curve:

  1. Burst nucleation. The injection supersaturates the solution instantly; thousands of billions of tiny crystal seeds nucleate at once — and the same burst crashes the supersaturation below the nucleation threshold, so no new seeds form afterwards. One birth cohort, hence one size.
  2. Growth. Remaining precursor feeds the existing seeds. In the diffusion-limited regime small dots grow faster than large ones — size focusing — sharpening the distribution as it grows.
  3. Quench. At the target size — seconds to minutes, monitored live by the colour of an aliquot under a UV torch — cool the flask. The ligands' oily tails cap the surface, halting growth and keeping the dots colloidal, a stable “solution” of crystals that pours like ink.

Best practice adds a jacket. A bare dot's surface is a forest of dangling bonds — mid-gap trap states that swallow carriers and kill the glow. Growing a thin shell of a wider-gap semiconductor around the core (CdSe core, ZnS shell — a type-I core–shell heterostructure) walls the exciton away from the surface: quantum yields jump from a few percent toward unity, and stability improves by orders of magnitude. Commercial dots add graded alloy shells and polymer overcoats; the state of the art emits >95% of absorbed photons with a spectral width under 25 nm.

Colloids are not the only dots. Strain-driven epitaxial dots (Stranski–Krastanow growth: InAs islands self-assembling on GaAs) sit inside photonic structures as today's best single-photon guns. And electrostatically defined dots — puddles of a few electrons corralled by gate voltages in silicon or GaAs, boxes made of pure voltage — carry single electron spins as qubits: several of the leading quantum-computing platforms are, literally, quantum dots wired into the machinery of Chapter 2.

7.5How a dot behaves under the lamp

  • Absorption is a staircase, emission is a needle. A dot absorbs everything above its gap (higher levels and continuum pile up), so one UV lamp excites every size of dot at once — but each dot emits only at its own band edge. One excitation source, any output colour: the property displays exploit.
  • The Stokes shift — emission sits slightly redward of the first absorption peak (band-edge fine structure and phonon shedding) — lets the glow escape without being immediately re-absorbed by neighbouring dots.
  • Blinking. Watch a single dot and it telegraphs: bright, dark, bright, on timescales from milliseconds to minutes. The dark spells strike when an extra charge strands in the dot: the next exciton then dies by Auger recombination — dumping its energy into the spare carrier instead of a photon, a picosecond process that outruns emission. Thick graded shells tame it; understanding blinking was a twenty-year subplot of the field.
  • One photon at a time. The same Auger effect means a small dot almost never emits two photons together — pump it with pulses and you get antibunched single photons on demand, the raw material of quantum optics and quantum cryptography (§5.5).

7.6Where the boxes went

  • Displays. The “Q” in a QLED television is exactly this chapter. A blue LED backlight pumps films of green and red dots whose emission is spectrally narrow (~25 nm) and tuned to the eye's primaries: purer primaries, wider gamut (approaching the Rec. 2020 standard), at high brightness and without the burn-in of organic emitters. Current sets use cadmium-free InP dots — the reason your remote's physics is also a regulatory story. Next generation: electroluminescent QD-LED pixels, dots driven directly by current, no backlight at all.
  • Biology's torches. A dot is twenty times brighter than an organic dye, does not photobleach through hours of imaging, and one synthesis yields a full palette excited by one laser. Antibody-tagged dots track single receptors on living cell membranes; near-infrared dots (PbS, InAs — wavelengths where tissue is translucent) map lymph nodes and tumour margins in surgery.
  • Light harvesting. Luminescent solar concentrators — dot-doped window glass that pipes re-emitted light to photovoltaic edges; PbS dot solar cells whose gap is chosen to harvest the infrared that silicon wastes; and multiple-exciton generation, one hot photon paying out two electron–hole pairs, probing past the Shockley–Queisser limit.
  • Sensors and cameras. PbS-dot photodiode layers laminate onto ordinary CMOS chips, giving cheap infrared vision — machine inspection, night driving assistance, spectroscopy in a phone.
  • Quantum machines. Epitaxial dots as single- and entangled-photon sources; gate-defined silicon dots holding spin qubits with coherence times in the seconds after isotopic purification — the exclusion principle, the qubit dial and the Bell bench of earlier chapters, all working in one device family.

7.7The arc, closed

Follow the thread back. A television's red pixel glows at 630 nm because an exciton recombined across a gap widened by confinement (this chapter), in a box whose levels exist because confined waves resonate (Chapter 3), governed by a state vector turning unitarily (Chapter 2), in a crystal held rigid by the exclusion principle (Chapter 4), emitting into field modes whose grains Planck was forced to hypothesise in a desperate December (Chapter 1) — and the photon that reaches your eye is detected, like everything else, one whole quantum at a time (Overview). One theory, unbroken, from a Berlin oven in 1900 to the screen in front of you. It remains strange. It has never yet been wrong.

A last number

A 3 nm CdSe dot contains roughly four thousand atoms — big enough to pour, ship and print by the tonne, small enough that removing a single monolayer of atoms visibly shifts its colour. It is the largest object in this almanac whose behaviour is set, wholesale, by a formula from Chapter 3 — matter's quantum nature, engineered rather than endured.
