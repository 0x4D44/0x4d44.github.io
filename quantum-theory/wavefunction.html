Chapter 3

Wavefunctions at Work

The abstract machinery becomes concrete the moment you choose a Hilbert space: functions on space, evolving under one equation. This chapter solves that equation in the handful of settings where it yields exactly — the box, the oscillator, the barrier, the atom — because those four solutions, between them, run most of the visible universe and everything in Chapter 7.

3.1The wavefunction: a particle smeared into a shape

Chapter 2's arrow, for a particle that can be anywhere along a line, becomes a shape — a curve spread over space called the wavefunction, written ψ(x).

Think of ψ(x) as the particle's “amplitude for being here” at every point x. It's the same arrow from Chapter 2, just with one component for every possible position — so it's a whole curve rather than a short list. Square its height and you get the odds: |ψ(x)|² tells you how likely the particle is to turn up near x, and the probabilities over all positions add to one. (Formally ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩ — the overlap of the state with the “definitely at x” state.) Feed this into Postulate V and you get the equation Erwin Schrödinger wrote down in 1926, the F = ma of the quantum world:

iħ ∂ψ∂t = − ħ22m ∂2ψ∂x2 + V(x) ψ TDSE

Since energy eigenstates carry all the dynamics as mere phase (§2.6), the working problem is the time-independent equation Ĥψ = Eψ: find the shapes ψn and the energies En the potential permits. Three general truths are worth carrying in:

  • Curvature is kinetic energy. The equation says ψ″ ∝ (V − E)ψ: where the particle is classically allowed (E > V) the wavefunction oscillates, and the more surplus energy, the faster; where it is classically forbidden it bends the other way — decaying exponentially rather than vanishing. That leak is tunnelling (§3.5).
  • Confinement quantises. A wave pinned by boundary conditions fits only certain shapes, like a violin string. Discrete energies are not an extra postulate; they are resonance.
  • Nodes count. The ground state has none; each rung up adds exactly one. More nodes, more curvature, more energy — a rule of thumb that reads off energy ordering at a glance.

These three habits — oscillate where allowed, decay where forbidden, count the nodes — let you sketch the answer to almost any one-dimensional problem before solving a thing. The rest of this chapter is four cases where you can solve it exactly.

Go deepermomentum space, and why wave packets spread

Momentum has its own wavefunction, φ(p) — the same state written in the momentum basis (the momentum operator is p̂ = −iħ ∂/∂x, the unique choice giving [x̂,p̂] = iħ). It is the Fourier transform of ψ(x), and Fourier analysis proves a function and its transform can't both be narrow — which is ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2, the uncertainty principle wearing analysis instead of algebra. A localised bump — a wave packet — is built from a band of momenta; it glides at the classical speed but also spreads, because its faster momentum components outrun the slow ones. A free electron pinned to an ångström doubles its width in about 10−16 s, which is why picturing the electron as a tiny ball fails almost instantly.

3.2The particle in a box

The simplest confining potential — and, since it is the engine of Chapter 7, the most important twenty lines of algebra in this almanac. Let V = 0 inside a well of width L and infinite outside. The wavefunction must vanish at the walls, so the interior solutions are standing waves with a whole number of half-wavelengths:

ψn(x) = √2/L  sin (nπx/L),  En = n2π2ħ22mL2,  n = 1, 2, 3… THE BOX

Read the formula like an engineer, because Chapter 7 will:

  • 1/L² — the lever. Halve the box and every level quadruples. Confinement energy is the knob that a quantum dot's radius turns.
  • No zero rung. The lowest state has E1 > 0: zero-point energy, the uncertainty principle made visible — a confined particle cannot sit still.
  • Scale test. An electron in a 1 nm box: E1 ≈ 0.38 eV, optical territory. A 1 g bead in a 1 cm groove: E1 ≈ 10−58 J, with levels so dense the ladder is a ramp — the classical world, recovered.

The box is less of a toy than it looks. π-electrons delocalised along a conjugated dye molecule are particles in a box the length of the molecule — extend the chain and the absorption marches from ultraviolet into the visible, which is why carrot orange and jeans indigo are, quite literally, box lengths. Semiconductor quantum wells (a nanometre-thin sandwich of one crystal in another) are boxes in one dimension and power laser pointers; a quantum dot is the same idea in all three.

Switch to “eigenstate” and step n up: each rung adds one more bump and one more node (a point of zero chance), and the energy climbs as n². Now narrow the box with the width slider — every level leaps higher. That 1/L² kick is the exact knob a quantum dot turns to pick its colour (Chapter 7). Flip to the superposition and watch the blob slosh — that's what “motion” looks like here.

3.3The harmonic oscillator: physics' favourite problem

Almost any potential looks like a parabola at the bottom of a valley, so the harmonic oscillator V = ½mω²x² is the universal small-vibration problem: molecular bonds, crystal lattices, and — decisively for Chapter 6 — each mode of the electromagnetic field. Its solution is the prettiest shortcut in physics. Instead of grinding through a differential equation, Paul Dirac noticed you can build a ladder: one operation nudges any state up by exactly one rung, a partner operation nudges it down, and there is a bottom rung you cannot fall below. That alone — no calculus — forces the energies to be a perfectly even ladder of steps ħω:

En = (n + ½) ħω, n = 0, 1, 2… AN EVENLY SPACED LADDER, FOREVER

The two rungs-movers are the ladder operators

with Ĥ = ħω(â†â + ½). The single commutation relation does all the work: â† raises any state by one quantum ħω, â lowers it, and the descent must stop at a ground state with â|0⟩ = 0 — from which En = (n+½)ħω follows with no differential equation ever solved. In quantum field theory (Chapter 6) these very operators create and destroy particles.

A perfectly even ladder — the only potential with that property, which is why “a quantum of vibration” is a well-defined particle-like thing: the phonon in a crystal, the photon in a field mode. The ground state solves a first-order equation and is a Gaussian, ψ0 ∝ e−mωx²/2ħ — the unique state that saturates ΔxΔp = ħ/2. Its zero-point energy ½ħω is no formality: it is why helium refuses to freeze at any pressure below 25 atmospheres, it is measurable in molecular spectra as isotope shifts, and summed over field modes it becomes the vacuum energy whose consequences (Casimir, Lamb) open Chapter 6.

3.4Through the wall: tunnelling

Point a wave at a barrier taller than its energy. Classically the particle bounces, always. Quantum mechanically the wavefunction cannot stop dead at the wall — inside the forbidden region it becomes a decaying exponential e−κx with

κ = √2m(V0 − E) / ħ EVANESCENT DECAY

and if the barrier's far side arrives before the amplitude has quite died, the wave resumes oscillating there — smaller, but alive. For a rectangular barrier of width a the exact transmission is T = [1 + V0² sinh²(κa)/4E(V0−E)]−1, which in the thick-barrier limit is the number to remember:

T ≈ e−2κa EXPONENTIALLY SENSITIVE

That exponential is ferocious, and it is the reason tunnelling built three industries and one Sun:

  • Alpha decay (Gamow, 1928 — the first triumph of tunnelling): the alpha particle rattles against the nuclear Coulomb wall ~10²¹ times a second with a tunnelling chance so tiny that half-lives range from microseconds to billions of years — a factor of 10²⁴ in lifetime from a modest range of barrier shapes, exactly as the exponential predicts (the Geiger–Nuttall law).
  • The Sun: at 15 million kelvin, protons' thermal energy is ~1 keV against a ~1 MeV Coulomb summit. Fusion happens only because nuclei tunnel through the last stretch; without it, stars are impossible.
  • The scanning tunnelling microscope (Binnig & Rohrer, 1981): the vacuum gap between a metal tip and a surface is a barrier; the tunnelling current changes by a factor of ~10 per ångström of gap. Servo the current, raster the tip, and you image individual atoms with a machine that fits on a desk.
  • Flash memory stores your photographs by tunnelling electrons through ~7 nm of oxide onto a floating gate — and the same leak, unwanted, is a hard floor under how small a transistor's insulation can shrink.

Lower the particle's energy or widen the barrier and watch the transmission T collapse — not smoothly, but off a cliff, because it falls exponentially. The wave doesn't stop dead at the wall; it leaks through as a shrinking tail and comes out the far side, smaller but alive. That thin leak lights the Sun, fires alpha decay, and reads atoms one at a time in a scanning tunnelling microscope.

3.5The hydrogen atom, solved honestly

The problem that broke classical physics yields to the Schrödinger equation exactly — one of only a handful of real systems that does. For the Coulomb potential V = −e²/4πε₀r, rotational symmetry lets the three-dimensional equation separate in spherical coordinates, ψ = Rnl(r) Ylm(θ,φ), and each factor brings its own integer:

The quantum numbers of one electron
symbolnameallowed valueswhat it counts
nprincipal1, 2, 3, … the energy rung: En = −13.606 eV/n², total nodes = n−1
lorbital0 … n−1 angular momentum |L| = √l(l+1) ħ; the letters s, p, d, f are l = 0, 1, 2, 3
mmagnetic−l … +l the component Lz = mħ along a chosen axis — quantised direction

The energies reproduce Bohr's ladder exactly — same formula, same Rydberg — but everything else is transformed. There are no orbits: |ψ|² is a standing cloud of probability amplitude, time-independent in any stationary state (the electron in hydrogen's ground state has zero orbital angular momentum — nothing circulates at all). The 1s cloud peaks in density at the nucleus but the most probable radius is exactly the Bohr radius a0 — Bohr's number survives as the crest of a distribution. And each level n hides n² degenerate states of different shape (2n² once spin doubles the count in Chapter 4) — the bookkeeping that will build the periodic table.

The equation also quietly fixes its own scale. Balancing Coulomb attraction against uncertainty-principle stiffness gives a0 = 4πε₀ħ²/mee² = 0.0529 nm: atoms are the size at which those two pressures draw. Small print that matters: the proton is not infinitely heavy, and using the reduced mass μ shifts every hydrogen line by one part in 1,836 — deuterium was discovered (1931) as a faint twin line displaced by exactly this. Relativity and spin then split the levels further (fine structure, ~10−4 eV; the Lamb shift, Chapter 6, finer still), and each refinement has matched experiment for a century. It remains the most accurately understood bound system in science.

Click through the orbitals — 1s, 2p, 3d. Each glowing cloud is |ψ|²: where the electron is likely to be. There are no orbits and nothing circles; the electron is a standing shape. Notice the dark gaps (nodes) multiply as you climb, and how s, p and d clouds have their own personalities — the shapes that, stacked by Chapter 4's rules, become the whole periodic table.

What resisted, and what comes next

Helium — two electrons — already has no closed-form solution; from there on physics lives by approximation methods (perturbation theory, variational trials, and today numerical machines of great power) built on exactly these four exact solutions as scaffolding. But the deeper novelty of many-particle quantum mechanics is not computational difficulty. It is that identical particles are indistinguishable in principle, and that fact — plus a property of the electron no classical object has — reorganises matter from the bottom up. That is Chapter 4.
