Quantum mechanics is the rulebook for the very small — the most precisely tested theory in the history of science, and easily the strangest. This almanac builds it up from scratch, in plain English, with the real maths one click away whenever you want it: the crisis that forced the theory into being, the handful of rules that run it, atoms and light and spin and entanglement — and, at the end, the tiny engineered crystals called quantum dots that put a textbook problem on the side of your television.

Fire electrons at the screen one at a time. Each lands as a single dot — a particle. But let thousands pile up and they paint stripes — a wave. Now hit “both + detector” to peek at which slit each one goes through. The stripes vanish. Watching changed the answer.

That little experiment is the whole of quantum mechanics in one picture. Everything in this almanac is, in the end, a way of explaining it.

Here is the puzzle. Each electron leaves the gun, crosses to the screen, and arrives at one spot, like a thrown pebble. Yet where it lands is steered by both slits at once — because the pattern that builds up is the striped signature of a wave passing through two openings and overlapping with itself. A single electron, it seems, explores both paths and interferes with itself. And the instant you install a detector that records which slit it really used, the stripes disappear and you get two plain heaps, as if the electron were an ordinary particle after all. The mere existence of a which-way record changes what happens. No one has ever made this weirdness go away.

What “quantum” means

Quantum mechanics is the rulebook for the very small — atoms, electrons, light. Down there, the world runs on rules that feel nothing like everyday life: a thing can be in a blur of possibilities at once, chance is built into the fabric of reality, and looking is never free. Strange as it sounds, it is the most precisely tested theory in the history of science (right to twelve decimal places), and it quietly runs about a third of the economy — every chip, laser, LED, solar cell, MRI scanner and phone camera is quantum machinery. You don't have to like what it says about reality (nobody quite does). You can still understand exactly what it says. That's the aim here.

Richard Feynman — who understood this as well as anyone — called the double slit “a phenomenon which is impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics. In reality, it contains the only mystery.” Get comfortable with the figure above and the rest is detail — beautiful, precise, world-building detail, but detail.

This really happens

The build-up above isn't a thought experiment. In 1974 Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi, and unmistakably in 1989 Tonomura's team at Hitachi, fired electrons one at a time — never more than one in the whole apparatus at once — and filmed the stripes assembling, dot by lonely dot, over twenty minutes. In a 2002 reader poll for Physics World it was voted the most beautiful experiment in all of physics.

A promise about honesty. Popular accounts of quantum theory usually fail one of two ways: they go mystical (“it's all just a fuzzy cloud of maybes”) or they go falsely cosy (“it's only waves, nothing to see here”). It is neither vague nor tame. It is ferociously exact about the odds of what you'll measure — and genuinely, famously unsettled about what is “really there” in between. This almanac keeps those two things apart: it calculates first, and clearly flags where it's stepping into interpretation (that's Chapter 5). The chapters run in the order the subject is best learned — the crisis that forced it, then the machinery, then the machinery at work, then its strangest consequences, and finally the nanocrystals that put it on your television.

Conventions & symbolsreference — for when you want it

SI units unless stated; energies usually in electron-volts (1 eV = 1.602 × 10−19 J, the energy an electron picks up falling through one volt). h is Planck's constant, ħ = h/2π the “reduced” version that shows up whenever angles do. A ket |ψ⟩ is a quantum state (Chapter 2 explains the bracket properly); operators wear hats (Ĥ, x̂, p̂) where it helps. The imaginary unit is i; a star means complex conjugate (z*). One conversion earns its keep everywhere: λ = 1239.84 eV·nm / E turns a photon's energy into its wavelength — learn it and you can do quantum optics in your head.
