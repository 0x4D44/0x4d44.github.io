The Sail Loft · forms, developments and pictorial views

Forms & pictorials

Every object on HMS Vellum, from a gun barrel to a lantern, is a handful of simple solids joined together. This deck teaches you to see those solids, find their true lengths and true shapes, blend their curves, cut them open into nets, and draw them in three dimensions: isometric, oblique, planometric and perspective. Then it shows you how to make a sketch or an illustration look solid with light, tone and texture.

⚓ Master Shipwright Fraser's briefing

Master Shipwright Euan Fraser: "Up here in the sail loft we cut flat canvas and fold it into solid shapes: covers, hoods, cartridges, lantern panels. Down on the gun deck, every barrel is a frustum of a cone and some cylinders with a sphere on the end. You cannot cut a pattern or draw a gun properly until you can see the plain geometric forms inside it. In the Plan Room, one Minecraft block is our unit cube: if you can draw a block in every view, you can draw the ship."

The skills here run through the whole course. The question paper asks you to interpret graphic items (which includes reading views, developments and pictorials) and the assignment asks you to produce them: sketched orthographic views in Task 3, production drawings and an exploded pictorial in Task 1, and a rendered scene in Task 2.

What this deck covers

Nine chapters: 1 Geometric forms · 2 True lengths, true shapes, auxiliary views · 3 Tangency · 4 Ellipses · 5 Interpenetration · 6 Surface developments · 7 Pictorial views · 8 Perspective and sketching · 9 Illustration techniques. Drawing standards, sections, assembly drawings and exploded views live on the Standards & views deck; 3D modelling and rendering live on the CAD deck.

1 · Geometric forms FORM · common forms and partial cuts

A geometric form is a regular three-dimensional solid that can be described exactly: you can say what shape its faces are and how big it is. The course expects you to know the common forms, their partial cuts (what is left when a plane slices part of a form away), and how real components are built by combining them.

FormWhat it isNamed by
PrismTwo identical, parallel end faces joined by flat side faces (rectangles in a right prism, where the sides are at 90° to the ends).The shape of its end: triangular, square, hexagonal…
CylinderTwo identical, parallel circular ends joined by one curved surface. In a right cylinder the axis is at 90° to the ends.Diameter and length.
PyramidA polygon base with triangular faces that meet at one point, the apex. In a right pyramid the apex is directly above the centre of the base.The shape of its base.
ConeA circular base joined to an apex by one curved surface.Base diameter and height.
SphereEvery point on its surface is the same distance (the radius) from its centre.Diameter.

Partial cuts

When a flat plane cuts through a form, the new flat face it leaves is called the cut face (or section). Its shape depends on the form and on the angle of the cut:

  • A cone or pyramid cut parallel to its base leaves a frustum: the base, a smaller parallel top, and sloping sides. A bucket and a lampshade are frustums of cones.
  • A cylinder cut at an angle to its axis has an elliptical cut face. Cut at 90° to the axis it is a circle; cut along the axis it is a rectangle.
  • A cone cut at an angle that slices right across it gives an ellipse; cut parallel to a sloping side, a parabola; cut more steeply, a hyperbola. Through the apex and along the axis, a triangle.
  • A sphere cut by any plane gives a circle.
  • A prism or pyramid always gives a straight-sided polygon, with one side for each face the plane crosses (counting an end or base face if the plane leaves through it).

The form rack: cut a solid and read its three views

Choose a form and a cut. The plan, elevation and end elevation are computed from the cut solid, with hidden detail dashed and centre lines in thin chain. The note tells you where the cut face is true shape, where it is only an edge, and where it is foreshortened.

Reading forms in orthographic views

Each form has a "signature" in third-angle views. Learn these and you can name the forms inside any component in an exam drawing:

  • Cylinder (axis vertical): a circle in plan; rectangles in elevation and end elevation, with a centre line.
  • Cone: a circle in plan (the apex is at its centre, where the centre lines cross); triangles in elevation and end elevation.
  • Square pyramid: a square with its diagonals in plan (the diagonals are the sloping edges seen from above); triangles in elevation.
  • Hexagonal prism: a hexagon in plan; rectangles divided by vertical edge lines in elevation.
  • Sphere: a circle in every view.

Components built from simple forms

Real objects are combinations of forms. Recognising them helps three ways: you can sketch the object by building up the forms, you can dimension it (each form needs its own sizes), and you can model it in CAD by adding and subtracting features. Fraser's favourite example is the gun barrel.

Plan: a circle with centre lines crossing at its centre. Elevation: a triangle.
Plan: a square with both diagonals drawn. Elevation: a triangle.
Plan: a circle. Elevation and end elevation: identical rectangles with a vertical centre line.
Plan, elevation and end elevation: three identical circles.
Plan: a hexagon. Elevation: a rectangle divided into three by two vertical lines.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"A cone's plan is just a circle. How is that different from a cylinder's plan?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "On its own, it isn't: a single view is never enough. That is why you read views together. Project down from the plan into the elevation: a cylinder's sides stay parallel, a cone's run in to a point. Where a cone has been cut, the plan also shows the cut face as an ellipse or a curve inside the circle."

2 · True lengths, true shapes and auxiliary views FORM · true shapes · auxiliary views

An orthographic view only shows a line at its real length, or a surface at its real shape, when that line or surface is parallel to the plane of the view. That plane is the picture plane: the imaginary flat sheet, square to your line of sight, that the view is drawn on. Then you see its true length or true shape. If it is tilted to the view it looks shorter: it is foreshortened. If it is at 90° to the view you see it end-on, as an edge view: a surface becomes a line and a line becomes a point.

This matters because you cannot measure a foreshortened line or cut a pattern from a foreshortened shape. Developments (chapter 6) need true lengths; sections and inclined faces need true shapes.

True length of a line: the rotation method

The sloping edge of a pyramid is not parallel to any principal view, so it is foreshortened in all of them. To find its true length, turn it until it is parallel to a view:

  1. In the plan, swing the edge about the apex (with compasses) until it is horizontal, parallel to the elevation.
  2. Project the swung end down to the base line in the elevation.
  3. Join that point to the apex in the elevation. That line is the true length.

You can check it with Pythagoras: the edge runs up the height of the pyramid while it runs across, in plan, half the diagonal of the base. For a 60 × 60 base and 80 high, that is √(80² + 42.4²) ≈ 90.6 mm.

True shape of an inclined face: the auxiliary view

An auxiliary view is an extra view, projected in a direction that is not one of the three principal directions, chosen so that it looks straight at a sloping face. When a face is inclined to the plan but at 90° to the elevation (like the cut top of the mast below), the elevation shows it as an edge. Look at it square-on, by projecting at 90° to that edge, and you see its true shape.

  1. Number the corners in the plan and find the same points on the cut line in the elevation.
  2. Project from each point at 90° to the cut line.
  3. Draw a datum (centre line) parallel to the cut line, a convenient distance away.
  4. Transfer each point's distance from the plan's centre line, measured with dividers, along its projector from the new datum.
  5. Join the points. Curved edges are joined with a smooth freehand or French-curve line. As it is a cut surface, the true shape is usually hatched.

The mast-head cut: an auxiliary view, stage by stage

Change the angle of the cut and turn the mast. Play the construction, or step through it. Every point is projected from the real geometry: lengths along the cut come from the elevation, widths from the plan.

Worked example 1 · true shape of a cut cylinder

A cylindrical mast, diameter 56 mm, is cut by a plane at 30° to the horizontal. The plane is at 90° to the elevation. (a) State what shape the cut face appears as in the plan. [1] (b) State the true shape of the cut face and calculate the lengths of its axes. [3]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. (a) A circle. The cylinder's surface is vertical, so from above every point of the cut edge lies on the circle.
  2. (b) An ellipse. A cylinder cut at an angle to its axis has an elliptical section.
  3. Minor axis = the diameter = 56 mm. Across the cut, the width is unchanged: it comes straight from the plan.
  4. Major axis = 56 ÷ cos 30° ≈ 64.7 mm. Along the cut, the length is the cut line in the elevation, which is its true length because the plane is at 90° to the elevation.

Marks follow the pattern of one mark per correct fact or value; the working in the last step shows where the number comes from.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why does the auxiliary view have to be projected at exactly 90° to the cut line?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Because only then are you looking straight at the face. Tilt your projectors and you are looking at it slantwise again: it comes out foreshortened, just as it was in the plan. The cut line already gives you true lengths one way; the 90° projection lets you add the true widths the other way."

3 · Tangency FORM · internal and external radii

A tangent touches a curve at one point without cutting across it. Wherever a straight edge runs smoothly into a curve, or one curve runs smoothly into another, the two are tangent. Drawings of hooks, links, brackets, spanners and cam profiles are built from tangent lines and arcs, and the arcs that join them are called blends (or blend radii). Three facts make every construction work:

An external blend touches the outside of a circle, so its centre is R + r from that circle's centre. An internal blend wraps round the circle and encloses it, so its centre is R − r from that circle's centre. That is the whole secret of "locating the centres of internal and external radii".

The method

To draw a blend of radius R, find every place its centre could be for each item it must touch (a locus), then take the point where the loci cross:

  • To touch a line: the centre lies on a line parallel to it, R away.
  • To touch a circle externally: the centre lies on an arc of radius R + r about the circle's centre.
  • To touch a circle internally: the centre lies on an arc of radius R − r.

Then find the tangent points, where the blend meets each item: drop a perpendicular from the centre to a line, or join the two centres (extended, for an internal blend) to cross the circle. Draw the arc between the tangent points only, and stop each straight line exactly there. In CAD the fillet tool and the tangent constraint do this for you, but the geometry is the same.

The blacksmith's blends: locate the centre, prove it touches

Pick a blend and play the construction. The check below the drawing measures the finished geometry: the distances from the centre and the angle between the curves at each tangent point (0° means a perfectly smooth join). Then tick the box and try to find the centre by eye.

Worked example 2 · describe a blend construction

A gun-port hinge strap has two holes, centres O₁ and O₂, with radii 20 mm and 13 mm. Describe how to construct an external blend of radius 34 mm that touches both. [4]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. With centre O₁, draw an arc of radius 34 + 20 = 54 mm. External: add the radii.
  2. With centre O₂, draw an arc of radius 34 + 13 = 47 mm. The point where the arcs cross is the blend's centre C.
  3. Join C to O₁ and C to O₂. Where these lines cross the circles are the tangent points. The touching point is always on the line of centres.
  4. With centre C and radius 34 mm, draw the arc between the two tangent points. Stopping at the tangent points avoids overlaps or gaps.

A "describe" question wants the steps in order, with the numbers. A sketch with the distances labelled would earn the same marks.

⚠ Exam lookout

Answers with the right idea but no sizes lose marks. Always say what radius the locus has (R + r or R − r, worked out) and which centre it is drawn from. The 2025 course report praised "a marked improvement in candidate's use of terminology, found in the course specification" in the CAD techniques questions. The same habit pays here: say tangent, tangent point, centre and radius, not "where it joins".

4 · Ellipses FORM · ellipses

An ellipse is a closed curve shaped like a stretched circle. Its longest diameter is the major axis and its shortest is the minor axis. They cross at 90° at the centre and divide each other in half, and they are drawn as thin chain lines. Two points on the major axis, the foci, define it exactly: for every point on the curve, the distances to the two foci add up to the length of the major axis.

You meet ellipses all the time in graphics: a circle seen at an angle, the cut face of a cylinder (chapter 2), every circle in an isometric drawing (chapter 7), and the ends of cylinders in perspective.

Two constructions

Concentric circles. Draw two circles with the same centre, one on the major axis and one on the minor axis. Divide them with radial lines (12 is usual). Where a radial line cuts the big circle, draw a line parallel to the minor axis; where it cuts the small circle, draw a line parallel to the major axis. They meet on the ellipse. Join the points with a smooth curve.

Trammel (paper strip). On the straight edge of a strip of paper mark a point P, then mark A at half the minor axis from P and B at half the major axis from P. Slide the strip so that A always stays on the major axis and B on the minor axis. P marks out the ellipse. It is quick and needs no compasses.

Plotting an ellipse: circles, strip and tilt

Play the concentric-circle construction, slide the trammel, or tilt a circle to see why an ellipse appears. The widget checks a plotted point against the definition (the two focal distances add up to the major axis).

Why circles become ellipses in isometric

In isometric, the faces of a cube are all tilted away from you, so a circle drawn on any face is seen at an angle: it becomes an ellipse. Drawn with full-size measurements along the axes (an isometric drawing), a circle of diameter d becomes an ellipse with major axis about 1.22d and minor axis about 0.71d. The major axis is always at 90° to the axis that is perpendicular to that face: horizontal on the top face.

To draw one accurately, plot it: draw the true circle in an orthographic view, mark points on it with a grid of ordinates, and transfer each pair of measurements along the isometric axes (the ordinate method). The quicker "four-centre" method draws arcs from four centres and is only an approximation.

Worked example 3 · a circle at an angle

A circular gun port, diameter 60 mm, is seen face-on in one view and tilted at 60° to the picture plane in another. (a) State the major axis of the ellipse in the tilted view. [1] (b) Calculate the minor axis. [1]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. (a) 60 mm. The diameter along the tilt axis is still parallel to the picture plane, so it stays true length.
  2. (b) 60 × cos 60° = 30 mm. The diameter across the tilt is foreshortened by the cosine of the tilt angle (see the "tilted circle" option in the widget).

5 · Interpenetration FORM · intersections of right prisms and cylinders

When one solid passes into or through another, the surfaces meet along a line of intersection (sometimes called the line of interpenetration). Pipes joining pipes, a gun barrel through a gun-port lining, a ventilator through a deck, a beam through a mast: the drawing is not finished until that line is plotted, because it is also the edge you would cut and weld or glue along.

  • Two prisms (flat faces) meet in straight lines.
  • A cylinder meeting a flat face meets it in a curve (part of an ellipse), unless you see the face edge-on, when it is a straight line.
  • Two cylinders meet in a curve, except in one special case: equal diameters with axes that meet. Then the curve is seen as straight lines in the elevation.

Plotting the line of intersection

You find points on the curve by drawing lines along the surface of the smaller solid, called generators, and seeing where each one pierces the larger solid.

  1. Draw the view in which the smaller solid is seen end-on (for a horizontal barrel, the end elevation). Divide its circle into 12 equal parts and number them: these are the generators.
  2. Find each generator in the view where the big solid is seen end-on (for a vertical mast, the plan). Transfer the positions using a 45° mitre line (a line at 45° drawn where the projectors from the plan and the end elevation meet; it turns a horizontal projector through 90°, so a depth measured in one view is carried into the other), or measure them with dividers. Each generator stops where it meets the mast's outline.
  3. Project each meeting point into the elevation. Where it crosses the same generator projected across from the end elevation is one point on the curve.
  4. Join the points with a smooth curve. Decide which parts are hidden: a point is hidden if it is on the far side of either solid, or the other solid is in front of it.

The gun through the mast: plotting the curve of intersection

Choose the mast and what passes through it, then change the sizes and offset. Play the construction to watch each generator travel from the end elevation, round the mitre to the plan and down to the elevation. Try equal diameters with no offset.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Do I really need twelve generators? Couldn't I just draw a nice curve?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Twelve gives you the important points: the top, bottom, front and back of the barrel, where the curve turns. Fewer, and you are guessing its shape. And notice the offset barrel: its curve is not symmetrical any more, and part of it goes hidden. Only plotted points show you that."

Worked example 4 · explain the method

Explain why generators are numbered in the end elevation when plotting the intersection of a horizontal cylinder with a vertical one. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. In the end elevation the horizontal cylinder is seen as a circle, so its surface lines appear as points that can be spaced out equally (cause),
  2. so each numbered point can be traced into the plan and elevation and the same generator found in every view, giving matching points on the curve (effect).

An "explain" answer needs cause and effect. The 2025 course report says most candidates "only give the effect, without the cause".

6 · Surface developments FORM · surface developments

A surface development (a net) is the true-size pattern of every surface of a solid laid out flat (often only the curved or sloping surface is asked for), so that it can be cut from flat sheet (card, sheet metal, canvas) and folded or rolled into the solid. Packaging, ducting, hoppers, lampshades, cartridges and covers all start as developments. Every line in a development must be a true length, because it will be cut and joined.

There are two methods, depending on how the solid's surface lines (generators) run:

Parallel-line development

For prisms and cylinders, whose generators are parallel. The development is a strip whose length is the perimeter of the base (π × D for a cylinder). Heights are carried straight across from the elevation, so a cylinder cut at an angle unrolls with a wavy top edge.

Radial-line development

For pyramids and cones, whose generators radiate from the apex. The development is drawn with compasses centred on the apex, radius equal to the true length of the sloping edge. A cone gives a sector of a circle with angle 360° × r ÷ L, where L is the slant height.

When a cone is cut at an angle, each cut point lies part-way up its generator, and most generators are foreshortened in the elevation. Find each point at its true distance from the apex before swinging it round: project it horizontally across to the outside generator in the elevation, which is the only one seen at true length. A pyramid's corner edges are not at true length in the elevation either (chapter 2), so find each edge's true length by rotation first and mark the cut point on that.

The sail loft: unroll it, unfold it

Pick a solid and a cut, set its sizes, then fold and unfold it. Each face turns about the fold line it shares with its neighbour, by exactly the angle between them, until the whole surface lies flat. The development beside it is drawn to scale from true lengths, with its key sizes in the note.

Fold lines and finishing a net

Appendix 2 of the course specification gives the line type for a fold line: a thin chain line, long dash and double dot. The outline that will be cut is continuous thick. A curved surface is rolled rather than folded, so the curved surface of a cylinder or cone has no fold lines: the thin generators on it are construction lines used to plot a cut. (If an end or base is attached to the net, it joins along a fold line.)

Worked example 5 · a speaking trumpet

The cone of a speaking trumpet has a base diameter of 60 mm and a vertical height of 80 mm. (a) Calculate the true length of its sloping side. [1] (b) Calculate the angle of the sector in its development. [2]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. (a) L = √(80² + 30²) = √7300 ≈ 85.4 mm. The slant height is the hypotenuse of the height and the base radius.
  2. (b) Sector angle = 360° × r ÷ L The arc of the sector must equal the base circumference: 2πL × θ/360 = 2πr.
  3. = 360 × 30 ÷ 85.4 ≈ 126.4°. Check in the widget: choose Cone, base 60, height 80.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why can't I just measure the sloping edge of the pyramid in the elevation?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Because in the elevation the corner edges are leaning towards you or away from you, so they are foreshortened. Use that length and the net will not close: your sides will be too short. Find the true length first (chapter 2), then swing it."

7 · Pictorial views PICT · oblique, isometric, planometric

A pictorial drawing shows three faces of an object in one view, so it looks three-dimensional. Each method is a set of rules for which way the three axes go and how they are measured. Each shows one face, or none, at true shape, and that decides when to use it.

Isometric

Vertical lines stay vertical; the two horizontal axes are drawn at 30° to the horizontal, so all three axes are 120° apart. Measurements along the axes are full size. Lines that are not parallel to an axis (non-isometric lines) cannot be measured: plot their ends instead. No face is true shape, so circles become ellipses.

Strictly, a true isometric projection foreshortens every axis to about 82% (0.816) of true length, because each axis is tilted to the viewer. Drawings use full size along the axes instead (an isometric drawing): the shape is identical, just about 22% bigger, and you can measure with an ordinary rule.

Oblique

The front face is drawn true shape, face-on to the picture plane. Receding lines go back at an angle, usually 45°. In cavalier oblique they are full length, which makes the object look too deep; in cabinet oblique they are half length, which looks more natural. Put the face with circles and detail at the front, where it stays true shape, and put the longest side along the front to reduce distortion.

Planometric

The plan is drawn true shape, turned so its sides are at 45°/45° or 30°/60° to the horizontal, and heights are drawn vertically from it. It is quick for interiors and room layouts (the floor plan can simply be traced and turned) and circles in plan stay circles. Because it looks stretched upwards, some illustrators shorten the verticals.

One ship, six projections

Switch the projection and watch the drawing morph from one set of axes to the next. The key reports each axis's angle to the horizontal and its scale, computed from the projection. The scale is shared, so isometric projection really does come out smaller than isometric drawing.

Worked example 6 · choose a method

The Plan Room must draw a gun-port lid: a flat plate 400 mm × 300 mm with a circular glazed porthole in it. State the most suitable pictorial method and justify your choice. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. Oblique (cabinet) with the plate face-on. The method must suit the object.
  2. The face with the circle is drawn true shape, so the porthole is drawn as a circle with compasses rather than an ellipse, and it is quicker. Half-depth receding lines make it look realistic. The justification links the method to the feature.

8 · Perspective and sketching PICT · sketching techniques

Perspective draws things as the eye sees them: objects get smaller as they get further away, and parallel lines that run away from you appear to meet. The point where they meet is a vanishing point (VP), and for horizontal lines every VP lies on the horizon line (HL), which is always at the viewer's eye level.

  • One-point perspective: one face of the object is parallel to the picture plane, so it is drawn true shape. Only the lines going away from you converge, to a single VP. Horizontals and verticals of the front face stay horizontal and vertical. Good for interiors and corridors.
  • Two-point perspective: the object is turned at an angle. Each set of horizontal edges runs to its own VP, one left and one right, both on the horizon. Verticals stay vertical. The nearest vertical edge, the leading edge, is drawn first and is the tallest.
  • If the horizon is above the object you see its top (a bird's-eye view); below, you see its underside (a worm's-eye view).
  • Vanishing points close together make the object look distorted. Spread them out, even off the edge of the paper.

The perspective builder

Drag the vanishing points, the horizon and the leading edge. The block is calculated as a real object seen by a real eye, so the grid of unit blocks shrinks exactly as it should, and the note tells you the viewing angle and distance your VPs imply.

Sketching techniques

Sketching is freehand drawing, quick and without instruments. It is how designers record, develop and communicate ideas, and it is how the assignment's Task 3 is done. The specification lists these techniques:

  • Proportion: the sizes of parts relative to each other. Compare everything with one unit (a block, the width of the base) and check by eye: "the height is about two-thirds of the length".
  • Line quality: start with light, thin construction lines and then go over the real outline with firm, darker, confident lines. Construction lines can be left in, but light. Outlines can be made heavier to make the object stand out.
  • Crating: sketch a light box that encloses the whole object, then carve the shape out of it. It keeps the proportions and the pictorial angles right.
  • Line sketching from related orthographic views: given an elevation, plan and end elevation, build a pictorial by measuring proportions from the views (or, the other way, sketch the views from a pictorial). Line up the views with light projection lines.
  • Oblique and isometric sketches: use the same axis rules as in chapter 7, by eye.

Manual or electronic sketching

Pencil and paper

  • Cheap, quick to start, nothing to charge or load.
  • Easy to annotate anywhere, in any meeting or workshop.
  • Rubbing out is slow and editing a sketch is hard.
  • Must be scanned or photographed to share.

Tablet and stylus

  • Undo, layers, copy and resize: quick to edit and develop ideas.
  • Easy to share and store digitally, and to drop into a DTP layout.
  • Costs money, needs power, and takes time to learn.
  • A small screen can make proportion harder to judge.
⚠ Exam lookout · Task 3 sketching

Sketch audit: spot the faults

A cadet has sketched an elevation and plan of a stepped block with a drilled hole. Tick every fault you can see, then check. Each new sketch has a different set of faults (occasionally none).

Worked example 7 · describe two-point perspective

Describe how to begin a two-point perspective sketch of a gun carriage. [3]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. Draw a horizontal horizon line at eye level and mark two vanishing points on it, well apart.
  2. Draw the leading (nearest) vertical edge of a crate that will hold the carriage, at its true height.
  3. Draw light construction lines from the top and bottom of the leading edge to each vanishing point, mark the width and depth by eye, and draw verticals at those points. Each point is a separate, specific step: "describe" wants a sequence of facts, one mark each.

9 · Illustration techniques ILL · light, shadow, tone, texture

Illustration makes a drawing look solid and made of something. You decide where the light comes from and then show what that light does. The specification names these techniques: light, shadow, reflection, tone, layout, material and texture, plus visual enhancement. They apply whether you use pencils and markers or software.

  • Light source: choose one direction and keep to it. Faces turned towards it are light, faces turned away are dark.
  • Tone: the range from light to dark. A block shows three tones (light, medium and dark faces); a curved surface shows a smooth gradation.
  • Shadow: the core (form) shadow is the dark side of the object itself; the cast shadow falls on the surface it stands on, away from the light, darkest close to the object.
  • Reflected light: light bouncing up from the ground lightens the lower edge of a curved object, inside its shadow. It stops the shadow side looking flat.
  • Reflection and highlights: a shiny surface shows a small, sharp highlight where the light reflects straight to your eye, and mirrors its surroundings. Polished metal shows hard-edged bands of light and dark; glass shows white streaks. A matt surface has soft tone and no sharp highlight.
  • Material and texture: the look and feel of the surface: wood grain, brushed or polished metal, fabric weave, brick. Texture tells the viewer what the object is made of.

The figurehead studio: light, tone and shadow

Move the light round and up. The tone of each face, the cast shadows and the sphere's highlight are computed from the light direction. Change the material, then render the same scene as three tones or pencil hatching, the way you would by hand.

Layout and visual enhancement

Layout is how the illustration sits on the page: its size, position and the space round it, so it catches the eye and leads to the information. Visual enhancement techniques make an illustration stand out: a heavier outline round the silhouette, a coloured background panel or shape behind it, a drop shadow, highlights on edges, and contrasting colour. The design elements and principles behind layout are on the Design & DTP deck.

Manual media or computer-aided?

Manual media

Graphite pencils, coloured pencils, markers, pastels and fineliners. Quick to pick up, expressive, cheap, good for preliminary ideas and sketches. Mistakes are hard to undo, and a large or repeated illustration takes a long time.

Computer-aided

Gradient fills, transparency and drop shadows in DTP and illustration software, and rendering in 3D CAD. Easy to edit, repeat and resize; materials, lighting and reflections can look photorealistic. Needs equipment, software and skill, and can be slow to set up for a quick idea.

Rendering a 3D CAD model, with its light sources, materials, reflections and a scene that places the model in context (as in the assignment's Task 2(a)), is covered on the CAD deck.

Worked example 8 · explain an illustration technique

An illustration of a brass ship's bell shows a thin white line along one edge and a strong dark band beside it. Explain why the illustrator has used these. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. Polished brass is highly reflective and mirrors its surroundings, giving sharp highlights and hard-edged dark reflections (cause),
  2. so the strong contrast between the white highlight and the dark band makes the bell look shiny and metallic to the viewer (effect).
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Is the shadow side of a ball just black?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Never. Look at the widget: the darkest part, the core shadow, is a band just past the edge of the light. Below it the ball picks up light bouncing off the deck, the reflected light. Draw it all one black and the ball turns into a flat disc."
