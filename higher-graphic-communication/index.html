Briefing room · read before your first watch

Welcome aboard, Cadet.

This is a complete, interactive guide to Higher Graphic Communication, the Scottish course about reading, planning and making graphics: the drawings that let a product be manufactured, and the posters, leaflets and rendered images that sell it. It is for anyone in S5 or S6 who has passed National 5 Graphic Communication. It covers every item in the course specification, one deck at a time, with diagrams, models you can turn and unfold, worked examples set out the way markers like, and a short check at the end of each chapter.

You will learn it with 1805 (Blockhaven) Squadron, the Air Cadets who already wrote up Higher Maths, Higher Physics, Higher Computing Science and Higher Engineering Science. This time they are posted to the Plan Room of HMS Vellum, a 74-gun ship of the line built block by block in Minecraft. A plan room is a drawing office. Every plan, chart, signal flag, poster and model the fleet uses is drawn there, so the squadron has a lot to learn and a lot to draw.

The animated banner at the top of the page is decorative. It is described in words in the section “Decoding the theme”.

The course at a glance

QualificationHigher Graphic Communication, course code C835 76, SCQF level 6, 24 SCQF credit points
Study timeAbout 160 hours of learning, including time to prepare for assessment
EntryUsually taken in S5 or S6. The course specification says candidates should have achieved National 5 Graphic Communication, or equivalent qualifications or experience, before starting.
Awarding bodyQualifications Scotland, which replaced SQA on 1 February 2026. This site follows the course specification version 2.0 (September 2018), valid from session 2018–19, including its Appendix 2 on standards and conventions.
AssessmentTwo parts, 140 marks in all: a question paper (90 marks, exam hall) and an assignment (50 marks, done in class). Your grade, A to D, comes from the total mark. There are no other components.
What it is aboutTwo areas of skill: 2D graphic communication, and 3D and pictorial graphic communication. In both you plan, draw and evaluate graphics, read graphics made by others, and use drawing standards correctly.

How you are assessed

The question paper

The paper has one section with a mixture of short (limited) and extended answers and scenario-based questions. You may answer in words, sketches, annotations or a mix. The specification says sketching is not a requirement and you are not required to draw with instruments: marks are for the information you convey, not for how pretty the sketch is. Write in blue or black ink, because scripts are scanned and pencil or highlighter may not show up. Some marks go to more challenging questions, which need you to interpret or combine more complex techniques, for example describing a CAD modelling process in detail.

The specification samples five areas, each with a range of marks:

The ranges overlap and do not add up to one number: the smallest ends sum to 63 and the largest to 116, while the paper is always exactly 90 marks. In any one year the split falls somewhere inside the ranges. CAD and desktop publishing are each worth at least 20 marks every year, so between them they are always at least 40 of the 90.

The assignment

The assignment is set each year by the awarding body and marked externally. You do it on your own, over 8 hours, in a supervised classroom, once all the course content has been taught. It is closed-book: no notes, no internet, no exemplars and no help from your teacher. Everything you make, by hand or on screen, is handed in on paper, on a maximum of 10 single-sided A3 pages (there is no penalty for going over), and there is no word count. It has three areas:

In the assignment you produce 2D and 3D production drawings to the standards (third-angle projection, dimensioning, line types and scale), plan and make a promotional publication, and review, evaluate and justify your decisions. In 2025, for example, Task 1 (production drawings) was worth 20 marks, Task 2 (promotional work: a rendered scene, preliminary layouts and a final poster) 26 marks, and Task 3 (freehand sketched orthographic views) 4 marks. The marking is positive: marks are added for skills you show, never taken away. The assignment deck goes through it in detail.

The report's advice: “Candidates must use the correct terminology, set out in the Higher Graphic Communication course specification.” This site uses that terminology throughout, and spends extra time on design elements and principles, ‘explain’ answers, standard symbols and digital technology.

In the report, “most” means 70% of candidates or more, “many” 50 to 69%, “some” 25 to 49% and “a few” less than 25%. Look for the ⚠ Exam lookout boxes in each deck; the ones headed “From the course report” use only the report's own wording.

How to use this site

  1. Start with Standards & views. Line types, third-angle projection and sections are the grammar of every other drawing. Then do Forms & pictorials, which builds the 3D thinking that CAD needs. After that the order matters less: CAD, Design & DTP and Technology & society can be taken in any order. Finish with The assignment and the Exam drill.
  2. Use the specification's words. The 2025 report says the improvement in CAD answers came from better terminology. When you describe a model, say extrude, revolve, shell, work-plane and profile, not “stretch it out” or “hollow it”. Every deck defines the terms the first time they appear, in bold.
  3. Play with every widget. Unfold the glass box, slide the cutting plane, turn the block model. Before you change anything, predict what the view will look like, then check. That habit is what handles an unfamiliar drawing in the exam.
  4. Sketch as you read. Keep a pencil and paper beside you. When a page shows a plan and an elevation, sketch the end elevation yourself before you look. Freehand sketching is assessed in the assignment, and a quick sketch can support any written answer in the paper.
  5. Try the worked examples yourself first. Cover the solution, write your own answer, then open it. The marks are shown as •1, •2 … in the order a marker would look for them. They are typical mark allocations written for this site, not reproductions of official marking instructions.
  6. Pass the ✈ pre-flight check at the end of each chapter. Multiple-choice questions mark themselves. For open questions you write your answer, reveal the marking guidance and mark yourself honestly.
✎ Describe, explain, compare

The command word tells you what earns the marks. Describe: give the features or steps, one point per mark, not just a list of words. Explain: give the cause and its effect, in the context of the question. “The heading is reversed out (white on a dark panel), so it contrasts strongly with the page and draws the eye first” earns more than “it stands out”. Compare: say what is the same or different about both things. The Exam drill deck practises all three.

Every pre-flight check you pass counts towards promotion through the Air Cadet ranks, from Cadet to Cadet Warrant Officer. Progress is saved in this browser only. Nothing is sent anywhere.

Then she is redrawn, again and again, in each kind of drawing this course teaches: a wireframe sketch (faint construction lines first, then firm lines); a third-angle orthographic drawing (the elevation first, then the plan projected up and placed above it, and the end elevation seen from the bow and placed to its right, with projection lines, a 45° mitre line, chain centre lines and dashed hidden detail); an isometric view built block by block on isometric grid paper; an exploded view that lifts the hull, decks, masts and sails apart along one vertical axis; a render that sites the model on the sea with a light source, a cast shadow and a reflection; and a print proof with bleed, a trim line, crop marks, registration marks and a CMYK colour bar.
