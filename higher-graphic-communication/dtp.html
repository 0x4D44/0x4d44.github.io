The Flag Locker · colour, layout and desktop publishing

Design & DTP

A poster has about three seconds to stop someone walking past it. This deck teaches the decisions that win those three seconds: how to plan a promotional document, how colour works, the design elements and principles, page layout and grids, typography, images and effects, file types, and how a design gets from screen to printing press. It is worth 20 to 30 of the 90 question-paper marks, and it is most of the promotional half of the assignment as well.

What this deck covers. Every item listed under Techniques used for producing effective promotional documents and publications and Desktop publishing (DTP) in the course specification: planning strategies (thumbnails, visuals and annotation); colour theory (warm, cool, contrast, harmony, accent, advancing and receding); the design elements and principles; the generic DTP terms and techniques; proofs, registration marks, crop marks and bleed; raster and vector file types. CAD lives on the CAD deck, and DTP's place in marketing and society on Technology & society.

⚠ Exam lookout · why this deck needs extra care

1 · Planning a promotional document DTP · planning strategies

Promotional graphics are graphics that sell, advertise or inform: posters, leaflets, flyers, magazine pages, web banners, packaging and exhibition displays. They are the third kind of graphic in the course, after preliminary graphics (sketches and ideas made while designing) and production graphics (the accurate drawings used to manufacture something). A promotional graphic is judged by one test: does it make the right people notice it, understand it and act on it?

That test cannot be passed by accident. Professional designers plan before they open the software, and the course expects you to do the same.

Five questions before you draw anything

Every promotional job starts with a brief: the client's description of what they want. Read it and answer five questions. Your answers become the reasons you give later when you justify your design.

The five planning questions, applied to Captain Grant's open-evening poster.
QuestionWhat it meansFor the open-evening poster
PurposeWhat must the document do? Inform, persuade, advertise an event, sell a product, recruit, warn.Persuade young people to come to an open evening and join the squadron.
AudienceWho must notice and read it? Age, interests, reading level, what attracts them. Also called the target audience.12 to 17 year olds, and their parents, who will want the date, time and place.
MarketWhere and how will it be seen, and what else competes for attention there? Local or national, print or screen, a busy noticeboard or a quiet waiting room.A school corridor noticeboard covered in other posters, and the squadron's social media page.
ContentWhat must appear? Title, copy text (the written words supplied by the client), images, logo, contact details.Title, date and time, three photographs, the squadron badge, a short paragraph of copy text.
StyleWhat mood or theme should the design have? Formal, playful, vintage, technical, adventurous. Style decides fonts, colours and image treatment.Adventurous and energetic, but trustworthy for parents: bold sans serif title, strong contrasting colours.

Research and investigation

Research is finding out what you need to know before designing. For a promotional document that usually means:

  • Looking at existing products: collect posters, leaflets or web pages that do a similar job, and note what works (a striking image, a clear date) and what fails (too much text, poor contrast).
  • Asking the audience: a short questionnaire or conversation with people in the target audience about what catches their eye.
  • Studying the client: the logo, the company colours, the fonts they already use, the message they want to give.
  • A mood board: a collage of colours, textures, fonts and images that capture the style you are aiming for.

Research is only useful if it leads somewhere. Finish each piece with a conclusion ("bold reverse-text titles were readable from across the corridor, so I will use one") and gather the conclusions into a short specification: a list of what the design must achieve. Each later decision can then be checked against that list.

Generating ideas: thumbnails, visuals and annotation

The course names three planning strategies. Learn the exact difference between them.

Thumbnail
A small, quick sketch of a possible layout, drawn to the right proportions of the page. Text is shown as lines or scribbles, images as boxes (often with a cross through them). Thumbnails are cheap, so you draw several to explore different arrangements before choosing one.
Visual
A larger, more developed drawing of the chosen layout, close to the final product, with colours, fonts, real headings and image positions shown. A visual lets the client see what the finished document will look like before it is produced.
Annotation
Short notes added to thumbnails and visuals to explain the design decisions: which design elements, principles and DTP techniques are used, where, and why they suit the brief.

What good annotation looks like

Annotation is where planning earns marks, and the difference between weak and strong annotation is the difference you will meet again in 'explain' questions. A strong annotation does three things: it names the term from the course specification, says where it is used, and says why (the effect on the reader).

Weak and strong annotations on the same thumbnail.
WeakStrong
"Nice big title.""Title in white reverse text on a navy band across the top, so it contrasts strongly and is the first thing read from across the corridor."
"Pictures here.""Aircraft photo square cropped to remove the hangar wall, so the reader's eye goes straight to the aircraft."
"Flags for decoration.""Three pennants repeated at equal spacing create rhythm, leading the eye across the page to the copy text."
"Text in columns.""Copy text in two columns on a two-column grid, so the lines are short and easy to read, and both columns align with the image edges."

The strong versions are longer, but every extra word is doing a job. In the assignment, this is how you show the marker you know which elements, principles and DTP terms you have used.

Justifying a proposal

To justify a design is to give reasons, linked to the brief, for the decisions you made. A justification answers the client's question "why should I pick this one?" Use the five planning questions as your structure: this layout suits the purpose because…, it will attract this audience because…, it will stand out in this market because…, it presents all the content clearly because…, and its style matches the theme because…. Each reason should name a specific feature of your layout. "It looks good" is an opinion, not a justification.

Thumbnail planner: choose, annotate, develop

Flight Lieutenant Murray has three thumbnails for the open-evening poster. Pick one, then tap each numbered pin and choose the strongest annotation for it. When every pin is annotated, develop the thumbnail into a visual and watch the sketch turn into a coloured layout.

2 · Colour theory PROMO · colour theory

Colour is the first thing a viewer notices, before they read a single word. It sets the mood, pulls the eye to what matters and ties a document together. The course specification lists the colour terms you must know: warm, cool, contrast, harmony, accent, advancing and receding. Each one is a tool with a predictable effect on the viewer, and an exam answer about colour should name that effect.

The colour wheel

A colour wheel arranges colours in a circle so that their relationships can be seen at a glance. The traditional artist's wheel starts from three primary colours, red, yellow and blue, which cannot be mixed from other colours. Mixing two primaries gives a secondary colour (orange, green, violet), and mixing a primary with the secondary next to it gives a tertiary colour (such as yellow-orange or blue-green). Twelve segments in all.

Warm and cool colours

Warm colours are the reds, oranges and yellows: the colours of fire and sunlight. They feel energetic, exciting and friendly, and they grab attention. Cool colours are the blues, greens and violets: the colours of sea, sky and shade. They feel calm, clean, professional and trustworthy. A sale poster leans warm; a bank's leaflet leans cool. Many good designs use one temperature for most of the page and the other for the few items that must stand out.

Advancing and receding colours

Warm colours appear to come towards the viewer: they are advancing colours. Cool colours appear to sink back away from the viewer: they are receding colours. The effect is strongest with bright, saturated warm colours against duller, cooler ones, and it is the same effect that makes distant hills look blue and grey in a landscape painting. A designer uses it to create depth on a flat page: put the title in warm red-orange on a cool blue background and it seems to float in front of the background; reverse the colours and the same title seems to sit behind a frame.

Harmony, contrast and accent

Harmony
Colours that sit next to each other on the wheel (analogous colours), such as blue, blue-green and green. They share a common colour, so they look as if they belong together. Effect: a calm, unified, pleasing scheme. Tints and shades of a single colour also give a harmonious scheme.
Contrast
Colours that are opposite each other on the wheel (complementary colours), such as red and green, blue and orange, yellow and violet. Placed side by side, each makes the other look stronger. Effect: vibrant, eye-catching, makes an item stand out. Contrast also comes from value: a light colour against a dark one (yellow on navy) is high contrast even though both are not complementary.
Accent
A small amount of a contrasting colour used in an otherwise harmonious or single-colour scheme. Because it is the only item that differs, the eye goes straight to it. Effect: draws attention to one key item, such as the date, a button or a logo. An accent only works if it is used sparingly: accent everything and nothing stands out.

Value: tints and shades

Value is how light or dark a colour is. Adding white makes a tint (higher value, lighter); adding black makes a shade (lower value, darker). Value is a design element in its own right (see chapter 3), and it is one of the most reliable ways of creating contrast and hierarchy: dark, low-value items carry more visual weight than pale, high-value ones.

Colour models: RGB for screens, CMYK for print

Screens and printers make colour in opposite ways, and a designer has to know which one the document is for.

RGB (red, green, blue)
An additive colour model. Screens make every colour by adding together red, green and blue light; with none you get black, with all three at full strength you get white. Use RGB for anything seen on a screen: websites, social media, presentations, on-screen adverts.
CMYK (cyan, magenta, yellow, key)
A subtractive colour model. Printing inks absorb (subtract) parts of the white light reflected from the paper. Cyan, magenta and yellow are the three process colours; K stands for key, the black plate. Black is printed separately because mixed CMY makes a muddy dark brown rather than a true black, and because black text printed from one ink is sharper and uses less ink. Use CMYK for anything printed.

Why designs for print are set up in CMYK. A printing press can only lay down cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink, so every colour in the document must eventually be described as CMYK percentages. The range of colours a system can produce is called its gamut, and the CMYK gamut is smaller than the RGB gamut of a screen: some very bright screen colours, especially vivid greens, blues and oranges, simply cannot be printed. If a designer works in RGB, the software converts those colours when the file is printed and they come out duller than they looked. Working in CMYK from the start means the colours on screen are a closer preview of the colours on paper, and the client is not disappointed. This is also why the assignment asks for a minimum of three CMYK colours.

Colour wheel: harmony, contrast, accent and depth

Choose a base colour by tapping a segment (or use the arrow buttons), then choose a scheme. The swatches, the sample signal-poster and the CMYK readout all update. The value slider mixes the base colour with white (tint) or black (shade). The second panel tests advancing and receding colours.

✎ Writing about colour

Name the colour term, point to where it is used, then give its effect on the viewer. "The yellow date panel is an accent colour in a mainly blue layout, so it is the first thing the viewer notices." That is one cause (yellow accent on the date panel) and one effect (noticed first): a complete 'explain' point.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"My poster looked brilliant on the screen and dull when it came off the printer. Is the printer broken?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "No, lad. Your screen makes colour from light and my press makes it from ink, and ink cannot reach every colour light can. 3 · Design elements and principles PROMO · elements & principles

Design elements and principles are the vocabulary of layout. The design elements are the basic ingredients a designer works with. The design principles are the ways of arranging those ingredients so that a layout communicates. The course lists five elements and eleven principles, and every one of them can be asked about by name.

📘 The five elements

line · shape · texture · value · mass/weight

📘 The eleven principles

alignment · balance · contrast · depth · dominance · emphasis · proportion · rhythm · unity/proximity · white space · grid structure

Describe or explain? Read the command word

Questions on this topic nearly always start with describe or explain, and they want different things. The general marking principles in published marking instructions say a 'describe' answer must give "a statement or structure of characteristics and/or features, not just an outline or a list", and an 'explain' answer must "relate cause and effect".

Describe

Say what is there and where. One relevant, specific feature per mark.

Describe rhythm: "The three pennants are repeated at equal spacing across the top of the page. The sub-headings all use the same red bold font."

Explain

Give a cause (what the designer did, and where) and an effect (what it does to the viewer). One cause linked to one effect per mark.

Explain emphasis: "The date is in yellow on a navy panel, which makes it stand out so it is one of the first things the viewer reads."

⚠ Exam lookout · effect without cause scores nothing

From the 2025 course report: on 'explain' questions "Most candidates continue to struggle with this command word and only give the effect, without the cause." An answer such as "it draws the viewer's attention" is an effect floating in mid-air: what draws attention? Always point to the feature in the layout first. And do not use the principle as its own effect: "the title is red to create emphasis" explains nothing. Say what the emphasis does: the reader sees it first, it stands out from the body text, it shows what is most important.

The five design elements

Each card gives a precise definition, a sample in poster colours, and model sentences. In the 'explain' sentences, the cause and the effect are marked so you can see the two halves every mark needs.

Line

What it is: a mark joining two points. Lines can be thick or thin, straight or curved, solid or dashed. They separate areas (rules), frame items, underline headings and lead the eye.

Describe: "A thick red rule underlines the title. A curved line runs from the image up to the heading."

Explain: The curved line runs from the image to the heading, which leads the viewer's eye from the picture to the event name.

Shape

What it is: an enclosed, two-dimensional area. Geometric shapes (rectangles, circles, triangles) look ordered and precise; organic shapes (waves, blobs, silhouettes) look natural and lively. Shapes hold text and images, group items and add interest.

Describe: "Triangular pennant shapes run along the top. A circular target shape sits behind the logo."

Explain: The wave shape along the bottom edge links the poster to the sea, so the viewer recognises the naval theme straight away.

Texture

What it is: the surface quality of an item: how it would feel if you could touch it. On a printed page texture is visual: a pattern or image (wood grain, blocks, fabric, paper) that suggests a surface. A textured fill in DTP software applies one.

Describe: "A wooden-plank texture fills the panel behind the title. The rest of the background is a smooth flat blue."

Explain: The plank texture behind the title suggests the blocks of the Minecraft world, so the audience recognises the theme, and it contrasts with the smooth areas around it.

Value

What it is: the lightness or darkness of a colour or tone. A colour with white added (a tint) has a high value; with black added (a shade), a low value. Low-value (dark) items look heavier and come forward; high-value (pale) items look lighter and sit back.

Describe: "The title is in a dark, low-value red. The panel behind it is a pale, high-value tint of the same red."

Explain: The dark, low-value red title on a pale, high-value panel contrasts strongly, so the title is easy to read and is seen before the paler items.

Mass / weight

What it is: how heavy an item looks: its visual weight, or how strongly it pulls the eye. Large, dark, saturated, dense or textured items have more mass; small, pale, thin or open items have less. Mass is what a designer balances.

Describe: "The large, dark navy image on the left has the most visual weight. The pale text panel on the right has much less."

Explain: The large dark image block has the greatest visual weight, so the viewer's eye lands on it first and it anchors the layout.

The eleven design principles

Alignment

What it is: placing items so that their edges or centres line up along common lines (left, right, centre, top). Aligned items look ordered and deliberate; items placed "by eye" look careless.

Describe: "The title, the image and the body text all line up on the same left-hand edge. The text column ends on the same right edge as the title bar."

Explain: Aligning the title, image and text on one left edge gives the page a neat, organised look, so it appears professional and is easy to follow from top to bottom.

Balance

What it is: the even distribution of visual weight across a layout. Symmetrical (formal) balance mirrors items either side of a centre line: calm, formal, traditional. Asymmetrical (informal) balance uses different items whose weights even out, such as one large item against several small ones: lively and modern.

Describe: "The layout is asymmetrical: one large dark image fills the left side, and two small coloured panels sit on the right."

Explain: The large image on the left is balanced by the two smaller panels on the right, so the page feels stable and not lopsided, while looking more dynamic than a centred layout.

Contrast

What it is: a clear difference between items: in colour (complementary colours), value (light against dark), size (large against small), shape, or font (bold sans serif against light script). Contrast makes items distinct and creates interest; too little and everything blurs together.

Describe: "Large yellow capitals sit on a navy block. Small white body text sits beneath them."

Explain: The yellow title on the dark navy background is very easy to read from a distance, so the event name is noticed even on a busy noticeboard.

Depth

What it is: the illusion of a third dimension on a flat page: some items seem nearer, others further away. It is created by overlapping items, drop shadows, advancing (warm) and receding (cool) colours, size (near things look bigger), and perspective.

Describe: "Three panels overlap. Each has a drop shadow. The front panel is warm orange-red and the back panel is cool blue."

Explain: The drop shadow and warm colour on the front panel make it seem to float above the others, so it looks nearest and most important.

Dominance

What it is: one item clearly more important than all the others, usually because it is the largest, the strongest colour or the most central. The dominant item is the focal point: where the eye lands first. A layout without a dominant item has no clear starting point.

Describe: "The photograph of the aircraft fills the top two-thirds of the page. The three small images below it are much smaller."

Explain: Making the aircraft photograph far larger than anything else gives the eye an obvious place to start, so the viewer immediately understands the poster is about flying.

Emphasis

What it is: making a particular item stand out from those around it so that it gets attention. It is created through contrast of colour, size, weight or font, an accent colour, a shape or line around the item, white space around it, or a drop shadow. Dominance applies to the whole layout's main item; emphasis can be given to several smaller items (a date, a price, a keyword).

Describe: "The word 'FLY' is larger, bold and red, and is circled. The other words are small and grey."

Explain: Setting 'FLY' in large bold red among small grey words makes it stand out, so it is the word the viewer remembers.

Proportion

What it is: the size of items compared with each other and with the page. Good proportion gives each item a size that matches its importance: the title large, key images medium, body text small. It also covers keeping an image's shape when it is resized (not stretching it) and keeping related items, such as a set of icons, the same size as each other.

Describe: "The title is very large and spans the page width. The image is about half the width. The body text is small."

Explain: The title is much larger in proportion than the body text, which creates a hierarchy, so the viewer reads the title first and the details last.

Rhythm

What it is: a repeated pattern of items (shapes, colours, images, headings) with regular spacing, which creates a sense of movement and leads the eye through the layout in order. Numbered steps and repeated heading styles are rhythm too.

Describe: "Five pennants are repeated at equal spacing along the top. The three steps are numbered in matching navy circles with identical red headings."

Explain: Repeating the numbered circles and red headings at equal intervals leads the viewer's eye from step 1 to step 3 in the right order.

Unity / proximity

What it is: unity is the sense that every part of a layout belongs together as one planned design; proximity is one way to achieve it: placing related items close together so the viewer reads them as a group. Unity also comes from a consistent colour scheme, repeated fonts, shared alignment and a common theme.

Describe: "Each caption sits directly under its image. The same three colours are used throughout."

Explain: Placing each caption close beneath its own image makes the viewer read the two as a pair, so it is clear which caption describes which picture.

White space

What it is: the empty areas around and between items. It does not have to be white: it is any area left clear of text and images. White space stops a layout looking cluttered, separates groups, and makes the items it surrounds stand out.

Describe: "There is a wide empty margin around the logo and title, which sit alone in the centre of the page."

Explain: Leaving a large area of white space around the logo isolates it from everything else, so the viewer's eye is drawn to it and the page looks uncluttered and professional.

Grid structure

What it is: the invisible framework of margins, columns, rows and gutters on which a layout is built. Items are placed so that their edges sit on the grid lines, either within one column or spanning several. The grid is what makes alignment, consistent spacing and a multi-page document's unity possible. Chapter 4 builds one.

Describe: "The page uses a three-column grid. The title spans all three columns. The image spans the first two columns and the body text fills the third."

Explain: Placing every item on the three-column grid keeps the edges aligned and the spacing equal, so the layout looks organised and the reader can easily follow the content.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Contrast and emphasis sound the same. And what's the difference between dominance and emphasis?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "They are linked, but not the same. Contrast is the difference itself: yellow against navy, big against small. Emphasis is what you use that difference for: making a particular item stand out. You create emphasis by contrast. And dominance is about the single biggest pull on the page, the one item that wins. A poster has one dominant item, but it can emphasise the date, the price and the web address as well."

4 · Page layout: size, grids, guides and master pages DTP · page set-up

Before a single word is placed, a DTP document is set up: the page size, the orientation, the number of pages, the margins and columns, and the master pages. Getting this right first saves hours later, because everything you add will snap into a structure that already works.

Page size, orientation and document sizing

Page size is the width and height of the finished page. In the UK most print uses the ISO A series: each size is exactly half of the one above, cut across its long side, so every A size has the same proportions (1 : √2, about 1 : 1.414). That is why a design can be scaled from A4 to A3 without distortion.

Orientation
Portrait is taller than it is wide; landscape is wider than it is tall. Posters, leaflets and magazine pages are usually portrait; banners, screen slides and certificates are often landscape. Choose the orientation that suits the content and where the document will be displayed.
Document sizing
Setting the size of the document in the software when it is created (a standard size such as A4, or a custom size such as a 2 m × 0.8 m display banner), and changing it later if the job changes. The document size should match the finished, trimmed size; any bleed is added around it (chapter 8).
Single-page format
A document of one page (or one side): a poster, a flyer, an advert. Everything must work in one view.
Multi-page format
A document of several pages: a leaflet, a programme, a magazine, a booklet. Pages are often designed as spreads (two facing pages seen together). Multi-page documents need consistency from page to page, which is what master pages are for.

Margins, columns, gutters and the grid

Margin
The space between the edge of the page and the content area. Margins keep text away from the trimmed edge (where it could be cut off) and frame the content with white space. The 2025 assignment poster, for example, had to keep a 21 mm border free of text.
Column
A vertical division of the content area into which text and images are placed. Short lines in narrow columns are easier to read than very long lines across a whole page.
Gutter
The gap between two columns, keeping their text apart. (On a spread, the inner margins either side of the fold are also called the gutter.)
Grid
A non-printing network of lines (columns, rows, margins and often a baseline grid for text) that the designer uses to position items consistently. A grid can be shown or hidden.
Guides
Non-printing lines the designer drags onto the page (or sets by number) to mark a position, such as the line every image top must touch. Guides help to align items precisely and consistently.
Snap
A setting that makes an item jump to a guide, grid line or another item's edge when it is moved close to it. Snap makes accurate alignment quick and stops items being placed a fraction of a millimetre out.

Grid structure, the design principle from chapter 3, is the result of using a grid well: every item's edges sit on the grid, so the layout has consistent alignment and spacing. A three-column grid does not mean three identical columns of content. A title can span all three; an image can span two; a pull quote can sit in one. The grid is the rule underneath; the variety comes from how many columns each item spans.

Grid builder: margins, columns, gutters, baseline and snap

Set the page and the grid. The column width is calculated from the page width, margins and gutters, and the placeholder content re-flows into the new columns. Drag the image box (or use the arrow buttons) with snap on and off to feel what snap does.

⚠ Exam lookout · grid structure

From the 2025 course report: "Question 1(c), grid structure, was particularly demanding for many candidates." To describe grid structure, do not just say "it uses a grid". Say how many columns, which items span which columns, and what lines up: "The page uses a two-column grid. The title and main image span both columns, and the body text is split between the two columns. The caption and the pull quote align with the left edge of the second column."

Worked example · Describe · grid structure

Refer to the three-column page in the grid builder (A4 portrait, three columns). Describe how grid structure has been used. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. The page is divided into three equal columns separated by gutters, inside a margin on all four sides. States the structure: number of columns, gutters, margins.
  2. The title spans all three columns, the image spans two columns, and the body text runs in single columns, with every edge lining up on a column edge. A second, different point: how items use the grid and what aligns.

Master pages and layers

A master page is a template page. Anything placed on it (guides, margins, columns, a logo, a running headline, a footer, an automatic page number) appears on every document page that uses that master. Change the master once and every page updates. A long document can have several masters: one for chapter openers, one for ordinary pages.

Layers are like a stack of transparent sheets laid over the page. Each item lives on one layer: background images on the bottom layer, text on the top, guides on their own layer. A layer can be hidden, locked so it cannot be moved by accident, or reordered to bring items to the front.

✍ Model answers · purposes

State a purpose of a master page. It holds the items that repeat on every page, such as the header, footer, folio and guides, so they appear in the same place on each page; this keeps the document consistent and saves time because they are set up only once.

State a purpose of guides. They let the designer align text boxes and images precisely and consistently, for example keeping every image top on the same line across a spread.

State a purpose of layers. They separate items so that, for example, the background can be locked while text is edited, and items can be brought in front of or sent behind others.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If the guides don't print, why bother? I can line things up by eye."

Master Shipwright Fraser: "By eye you'll be a millimetre out here and two there, and on a 24-page regatta programme the reader feels it even if they can't say why. The guides are the shipwright's chalk line: nobody sees them in the finished hull, but every plank is straight because of them."

5 · Typography and text DTP · fonts & text

Typography is the choice and arrangement of type. Most of a promotional document's message is carried by words, so the fonts you choose and the way you set them decide whether the message is read at all. This chapter covers the font families, the names of every text feature on a page, and the techniques for shaping text.

Serif, sans serif and script fonts

The three families, their character and typical use.
FamilyWhat it looks likeCharacterGood for
SerifSmall strokes (serifs) finish the ends of letters; strokes often vary from thick to thinTraditional, formal, trustworthy, classic or vintageBody text in printed books and newspapers; titles that need a traditional feel
Sans serifNo serifs; strokes usually of even thicknessModern, clean, simple, boldHeadings and titles seen from a distance; text on screens; signs
ScriptLetters joined or flowing, like handwriting or calligraphyElegant, personal, decorative, celebratoryShort titles, names, invitations. Hard to read in long passages or in capitals, so use sparingly

Font styles are the variations within one font: bold, italic, underline, and sizes. Changing style rather than font is a neat way to create contrast while keeping unity. In the assignment you must use two contrasting fonts: typically a bold sans serif for the title paired with a serif or script for other text, so the difference is obvious.

Text on the page: the vocabulary

The course expects you to name each of these features on a layout. The anatomy widget below shows them all on one page of the regatta programme.

Title
The main name of the whole document or poster, the largest text.
Heading
A name for a section or article within the document; sub-headings divide the text further.
Header
The area at the top of a page, repeated on each page (often from the master page), holding items such as a running headline or logo.
Running headline
A line of text repeated at the top of every page, such as the publication's name or the chapter title, telling the reader where they are.
Footer
The area at the bottom of a page, repeated on each page, holding items such as the folio or contact details.
Folio
The page number.
Caption
A short piece of text placed beside or under an image to explain it.
Pull quote
A short, striking extract from the body text, enlarged and set apart (often between rules or in a box) to catch the eye and tempt the reader into the article.
Column rule / rule
A rule is a printed line used to separate or emphasise content (under a heading, above a footer). A column rule is a vertical line printed in the gutter between columns.
Reverse (reverse text)
Light text on a dark background, the reverse of the usual dark on light. Strong contrast, so it draws attention; best for short text, as long passages are tiring to read.
Drop cap
An enlarged first letter of a paragraph that drops down over several lines. It marks the start of the text and draws the reader's eye to where reading begins.
Placeholder text (lorem ipsum)
Dummy text, usually scrambled Latin beginning "Lorem ipsum…", used to fill a layout before the real copy is ready. It shows how much space text will take and how the layout looks, without the reader being distracted by the meaning.
Indent
The first line of a paragraph starts further in than the rest, marking a new paragraph.
Hanging indent
The first line starts at the left edge and every following line is indented: the first line "hangs" out. Used for lists, numbered points and references, so the first word is easy to spot.
Line spacing
The vertical distance between lines of text (also called leading). Too tight and lines crowd together; too loose and the paragraph falls apart. Slightly more than the font size is normal for body text.
Justification
How lines of text are aligned: left (ragged right edge, easiest to read), right, centred (for short titles and invitations) or fully justified (both edges straight, as in newspapers; the software stretches the spaces, which can leave uneven gaps).

Anatomy of a page: the regatta programme

Tap a term to light up that feature on the page, or tap a part of the page to find its name. Every label is a term from the course specification.

Text boxes and shaping text

Text box
A frame that holds text in a DTP document. Text can only be placed inside a text box (or along a path), and the box can be moved, resized, rotated and linked to other boxes.
Handles
The small squares on the corners and edges of a selected text box, image or shape. Dragging a handle resizes the item; a corner handle (with Shift held, in most software) scales it in proportion; a rotation handle turns it.
Text wrap
Making text flow around an image or shape instead of running behind or over it. Text can wrap around the image's rectangular frame or, after full cropping, around the outline of the object itself (chapter 6).
Flow text along a path
Making a line of text follow a drawn path, such as a curve, a circle or a wave, rather than a straight baseline. It adds movement and can link text to an image (a title following an aircraft's climb, a name around a badge).
Extended text
Body text that is longer than one text box can hold, continued (linked, or "threaded") from one text box into another, in the next column or on a later page. The software shows a marker when a box has overflowing text, and the overflow is carried into the linked box. When the text is edited, it re-flows through all the linked boxes automatically.

Type-setting playground

This is real text set by your browser, so what you see is what the settings do. Try each justification, change the line spacing, add a drop cap, an indent or a hanging indent, split the text into columns with a column rule, or reverse it. The readout names the settings in course terms.

Worked example · Explain · fonts

The regatta programme uses a bold sans serif title and a serif body text. Explain why these fonts are suitable. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. The heavy, bold sans serif title can be read clearly from a distance and gives the programme a modern, energetic feel suited to a sporting event.
  2. The two fonts contrast strongly with each other, so the reader instantly tells the titles from the body text, which makes the programme easy to navigate. Other creditable points: serif body text is traditional and suits the naval theme; repeating the same two fonts on every page creates unity.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why fill the layout with Latin nonsense? Why not just wait for the real words?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "Because the layout can't wait. Placeholder text shows how much space the copy will take and how the page looks, so the design can be approved while the words are still being written. And because nobody can read it, the client judges the design, not the spelling."

6 · Images and effects DTP · images, fills & effects

Images do most of the persuading on a promotional document, and DTP software gives you a set of tools for bringing them in, trimming them, and dressing up shapes and text. Know what each tool does and, just as important, why a designer would use it.

Getting content in and out

Copy / paste
Copying an item (text, image, shape) to the computer's clipboard and pasting a duplicate, in the same document or another program. Quick for repeating items such as a logo on several pages.
Import
Bringing a file made elsewhere into the document: placing a photograph, a logo, a CAD rendering or a text file from a word processor. Importing keeps the original file and quality, and the item can be updated if the original changes.
Export
Saving the document, or part of it, in a different file format for another use: a PDF for the printer, a JPEG or PNG for a website or social media.

Cropping: square and full

Cropping removes unwanted parts of an image. The course names two kinds, and the 2024 marking instructions credited the difference.

Square cropping
Trimming an image with straight, rectangular edges (dragging the image frame's handles inwards). Used to remove distracting edges, to focus on the important part (zooming in on the cockpit), and to make the image fit the space available in the layout.
Full cropping
Cutting precisely round the outline of the object so that all of the background is removed. The object then sits directly on the page, it can be placed over your own background or colour, and text can wrap closely around its actual shape.
Rotate
Turning an item about a point, by a typed angle or with the rotation handle. A slight rotation of an image or text box adds movement and energy; a rotated title can follow a diagonal in the layout.

Crop bench: square crop, full crop and text wrap

Start with the original photo. Square crop it (the sliders set the crop rectangle), then full crop it and watch the crop outline shrink-wrap the aircraft and the text re-wrap around its shape. This is real text, wrapped by the browser around the cropped image.

Fills and effects

Colour fill
Filling a shape, text box or text with a single flat colour.
Colour picking
Using an eyedropper (colour picker) tool to sample an exact colour from an image or logo, then applying it elsewhere. Matching colours from the photographs or the client's logo ties the layout together (unity) and keeps company colours exact.
Textured fill
Filling a shape with a pattern or image of a surface: wood, stone, fabric, blocks. It adds visual texture and can suggest a theme.
Gradient fill
A fill that blends gradually from one colour (or value) to another. A linear gradient blends along a straight line; a radial gradient blends outwards from a centre. Gradients suggest light, curvature or depth and can make a background more interesting than a flat colour.
Transparency
Making an item partly see-through, so items behind it show through. Used to lay text panels over photographs while keeping the photograph visible, to fade a background image so text on it stays readable, and to create layered effects.
Drop shadow
A soft shadow placed behind and offset from an item, as if it were raised above the page with light falling on it. It creates depth and separates the item from its background, making it stand out.

Effects playground: fills, transparency, shadow, rotate

Style the pennant badge. Try each fill, use the colour picker on the photograph to sample a colour, and adjust transparency, drop shadow and rotation. Watch how each one changes what the viewer notices.

Worked example · Describe · cropping in a layout

Describe how cropping has been used on the images in the recruitment poster in the crop bench. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. The aircraft photo has been square cropped to remove the hangar wall and fuel bowser, focusing on the aircraft and fitting it into the space in the layout.
  2. The aircraft has then been full cropped, removing all the background so it sits on the page and the text wraps around its outline. Say which kind of cropping, which image, and what it achieves. "Cropped to shape" is not the course's term: say full cropping.

7 · Raster and vector file types DTP · file types

Every image in a document is stored in one of two fundamentally different ways. Choosing the wrong one gives you blurry logos, giant files or a white box round an image that should have no background.

Raster (bitmap) image
A grid of tiny squares called pixels, each storing one colour. Rasters can show photographs and subtle changes of colour, because every pixel can be different. But they have a fixed number of pixels: enlarge one too far and the pixels show as jagged steps (pixelation). The file size depends on the number of pixels and how many bits store each pixel's colour.
Resolution
The number of pixels per unit length when printed, in pixels per inch (ppi, often called dpi). About 300 ppi is the usual standard for print, so an A4 photo printed at full size needs about 2480 × 3508 pixels. Screen images need far fewer.
Vector image
Made of mathematically described points, lines, curves and fills. It can be scaled to any size, from a badge to a ship's sail, without losing quality, and files are usually small for logos, icons, text and diagrams. It cannot easily represent a photograph.
Compression
Reducing file size. Lossless compression packs the data more efficiently but keeps every pixel exactly (PNG, TIFF with LZW). Lossy compression throws away detail the eye is less likely to notice, which shrinks photographs dramatically but causes blurring and blocky "artefacts", worse each time the file is re-saved (JPEG).
The six file types named in the course specification.
TypeRaster / vectorFeaturesTypical use
JPEG (.jpg)RasterLossy compression gives small files; millions of colours; no transparency; quality drops each time it is re-saved; opens on almost any device or softwarePhotographs on the web, email, social media; digital camera images
PNG (.png)RasterLossless compression; supports transparency (a transparent background); larger than JPEG for photosLogos, icons and graphics with flat colour or text for screens; images that need a transparent background
TIFF (.tif)RasterVery high quality, uncompressed or lossless; supports CMYK colour, layers and transparency; large filesHigh-quality images for professional printing and archiving

Zoom bench: raster against vector, and the size of a pixel

Zoom in on the squadron badge stored as a small raster and as a vector. Then use the calculator: the uncompressed size of a raster is width × height × bits per pixel ÷ 8 bytes. The JPEG quality slider really compresses the badge in your browser and measures the result.

Worked example · Explain · choosing a file type

The squadron logo is to appear on the website, on letterheads and on a 3 m display banner. Explain why it should be supplied as a vector file. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. A vector logo is stored as points, lines and curves rather than pixels, so it can be scaled up to the 3 m banner without becoming pixelated or blurred.
  2. The vector file is small because it stores drawing instructions, not millions of pixels, so it loads quickly on the website and is easy to email to the printer. Also creditable: SVG supports a transparent background, so the logo sits cleanly on any colour.

8 · Pre-press and print: proofs, marks and bleed DTP · pre-press

Pre-press is everything that happens between the finished layout and the printing press: checking, preparing the file, and making the printing plates. Mistakes caught here cost a few minutes; mistakes caught after a print run of 5,000 programmes cost the whole job.

Four-colour printing

Commercial printing builds full-colour images from the four process inks, cyan, magenta, yellow and key (black). The software splits the CMYK document into four separations: one greyscale image per ink, showing how much of that ink goes where. Each separation becomes a printing plate, and the sheet passes through the press picking up one ink after another. For a picture to come out sharp, all four impressions must land in exactly the same place. That is what registration marks are for.

Separation press: four plates, one picture

Run the press to watch the poster separated into its cyan, magenta, yellow and key plates, then printed one ink at a time. Each plate is calculated from the colours of the poster. Then drag the misregistration slider: when the plates do not line up, colour fringes appear on every edge, and the registration marks show it at once.

Proofs, crop marks, registration marks and bleed

Proof (pre-press proof)
A trial version of the document, printed or as a PDF on screen, made before the full print run so that the client and designer can check it: spelling, missing items, image quality, colours, positions and folding. Errors are marked up and corrected, and a new proof made if needed. Signing off a proof means agreeing it is ready to print.
Crop marks
Thin lines printed in the corners of the sheet, outside the page, that show where the paper is to be trimmed to its finished size. (Also called trim marks.) They are themselves cut away.
Registration marks
Small targets (a circle with a cross-hair) printed outside the trim area on every colour separation. When the four colours are printed exactly on top of each other, each target looks like a single black mark; if they are misaligned, coloured fringes show. Their purpose is to ensure the multi-colour printing has aligned correctly; they also help to line up machines that cut or fold the sheet.
Bleed
Extending any image or colour that touches the edge of the page a few millimetres (commonly 3 mm) beyond the trim line. Trimming is never perfectly accurate; with bleed, a slightly off-line cut still lands on printed colour, so no thin white edge appears.

Proof builder: bleed, crop marks and the guillotine

Build a print-ready proof by adding bleed, crop marks and registration marks. Then set how far the guillotine drifts and trim the stack. Without bleed, even a small drift leaves a white sliver along one edge.

✍ Model answers · purposes

Name the mark and state its purpose (a circle with a cross-hair outside the page): registration mark; to make sure the four colour separations are printed exactly in line with each other.

State the purpose of bleed. So that when the printed sheet is trimmed, any slight inaccuracy in the cut does not leave an unprinted white edge, because the colour continues past the trim line.

State a purpose of a proof. To check for and correct errors (spelling, layout, colour) before the full print run, so that mistakes are not printed many times over.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why not just make the poster exactly A3 and cut exactly on the edge?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Because the guillotine cuts through a stack of five hundred sheets, and the stack shifts by a hair. Cut 'exactly on the edge' and half your posters have a white whisker down one side. Give me three millimetres of bleed and I can be a hair out and nobody will ever know."

9 · Promotional documents and displays: brief to final PROMO · techniques

Everything on this deck comes together when you produce a promotional document or a graphic display for real. This chapter puts the steps in order, shows how to evaluate a layout, and finishes with exam-style answers to practise against.

Graphic displays

A graphic display is a promotional graphic presented in a space rather than in the hand: an exhibition stand, a pop-up banner, a shop window, a noticeboard display, a digital screen in a foyer. The same principles apply, with three extra pressures: it is seen from a distance (titles must be large, high-contrast and short), while people are moving (one dominant message, read in seconds), and often at a large size (so logos should be vector and photographs high resolution). A display for the squadron at a careers fair might pair a 2 m pull-up banner with an A4 leaflet: the banner attracts, the leaflet informs.

From brief to final

  1. Read the brief and answer the five planning questions: purpose, audience, market, content, style.
  2. Research existing products, the audience and the client; draw conclusions into a specification.
  3. Generate ideas as several thumbnails, annotated with design elements, principles and DTP terms.
  4. Develop the strongest thumbnail into a visual; justify the choice against the brief.
  5. Set up the document: size, orientation, bleed, CMYK colour, margins, columns, master pages, layers.
  6. Produce the layout: import and crop images, set the type, apply colour, fills and effects.
  7. Proof, correct and export a print-ready file with crop and registration marks and bleed.
  8. Evaluate the result against the brief and specification.

Evaluating a layout

To evaluate is to judge how well something meets its aims, with reasons. Work through these questions, and for every judgement point to the feature that causes it:

  • Purpose and audience: is it obvious within seconds what the document is for, and is the style right for the audience?
  • Hierarchy: is there a dominant item? Is the most important information (what, when, where) seen before the details?
  • Structure: are items aligned on a grid? Is the spacing consistent? Is there enough white space?
  • Colour and type: do the colours support the mood, with enough contrast for legibility? Do the fonts contrast and suit the theme?
  • Unity: does it look like one planned design, with consistent colours, fonts and style?
  • Production: are images sharp at the printed size, colours set in CMYK, and is bleed included?
⚠ Exam lookout · skill and visual impact

In the 2025 assignment the final poster, task 2(c), was worth 11 marks, including up to 3 for skill and visual impact. The 2025 course report notes "some improvement in terms of skill and visual impact, but most candidates were unable to demonstrate a higher level", and advises candidates to "consider the overall skill they are trying to demonstrate and the visual impact that they are trying to create." In practice: one clear dominant item, a visible grid, deliberate contrast and a restrained, consistent colour scheme do more for impact than adding more effects.
