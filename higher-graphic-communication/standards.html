The Plan Room · drawing standards and views

Standards & views

Every gun, block and hatch on HMS Vellum starts as a drawing in the Plan Room. A drawing is only useful if a stranger can read it exactly as you meant it, so the Plan Room works to a shared rulebook: the British Standard conventions in Appendix 2 of the course specification. This deck teaches that rulebook (graphic types, line types, dimensions, tolerances, third-angle projection, sections, assemblies, scales and building symbols) and the reading skill that the question paper tests hardest: interpreting drawings you have never seen before.

Master Shipwright Euan Fraser: "Cadets, I have built this ship twice: once on paper and once in blocks. The paper one came first, and it had to be right, because the carpenters built exactly what I drew, mistakes and all. A thick line means an edge you can see. A dashed line means one you can't. 1 · Graphic types, manual media and the CAD choice STD · graphic types

Three jobs, three kinds of graphic

The course sorts graphics by the job they do in the life of a product or publication. Learn the three names exactly: they are course-specification terms, and the 2025 course report says candidates "must use the correct terminology".

  • Preliminary graphics are the graphics a designer makes while designing: thumbnail sketches, idea sketches, annotated sketches, quick orthographic and pictorial sketches, layout thumbnails and visuals. They are fast and freehand. Their job is to generate, explore, develop and communicate ideas, to the designer and to the client, before anything is fixed.
  • Production graphics are the graphics used to make the product: accurate, dimensioned drawings to the standards in this deck, such as orthographic views, sectional views, assembly drawings, exploded views, and building plans. Their job is to give a manufacturer or builder every piece of information, with only one possible reading.
  • Promotional graphics are the graphics used to market the product: posters, adverts, leaflets, web pages, rendered illustrations placed in a scene. Their job is to attract and persuade a customer. They are covered on the Design & DTP deck.

The three types are linked. Preliminary graphics lead to the production drawings; the production model (often a CAD model) is then rendered for the promotional graphics. In your assignment the three areas are marked separately: preliminary graphics 5–15 marks, production graphics 10–25 marks and promotional graphics 10–25 marks.

Manual techniques are graphics made by hand: freehand sketching, drawing with instruments, and rendering with pencils, markers and pastels. Know the common media and what each is good at.

Manual or electronic? The relative merits

The course asks for the relative merits of manual techniques compared with electronic (computer-aided) ones. The fair answer is that each wins at different stages. A "merit" in an answer needs a reason: say what is better and why it matters in the situation given.

Manual is better when…

  • you need ideas fast: a sketch takes seconds and needs no software, so it suits brainstorming and client meetings;
  • you are away from a computer: paper and pencil are cheap, portable and need no power;
  • you want to show your preliminary skills: the 2025 course report reminds centres that digital sketching may only use a straight-edge tool, because a circle tool is "like using a set of compasses".

Electronic is better when…

  • you need accuracy and repeatable line types, so drawings meet the standard every time;
  • the design changes: edit the model once and every linked view, section and dimension updates;
  • you need to share or reuse: files can be emailed, stored in the cloud, copied, arrayed, and sent to manufacturing machines;
  • you want realism: a 3D model can be rendered with materials and lighting for promotional work.

Against electronic methods: software and hardware cost money, staff need training, files can be lost or corrupted without backups, and quick idea generation is slower than on paper. Against manual methods: changes mean redrawing, copies have to be scanned or photocopied, and accuracy depends on the person's skill.

Computer-aided techniques: the map

The course groups the electronic side into four strands. Each has its own deck here; you only need the map now.

  • CAD (computer-aided design): 2D drawing and 3D modelling of components and assemblies, from which production drawings are generated. See CAD.
  • DTP (desktop publishing): page-layout software for promotional publications. See Design & DTP.
  • Digital capture and input: devices that bring images and data into the computer, such as scanners, digital cameras, graphics tablets and 3D scanners.
  • Output: devices that turn files into physical things, such as printers, plotters, and 3D printers. Input, output and networks are covered on Technology & society.

Worked example 1 · Explain · graphic types

The Plan Room designs a new signal-lamp bracket. Explain why the designer produces preliminary graphics before any production drawings. [2]

Show solution [2 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Preliminary sketches are quick to produce, so many different ideas can be explored and compared before time is spent on accurate drawings. Cause (quick) linked to effect (many ideas compared).

2 · Drawing standards and line types STD · line types

A drawing standard is an agreed set of rules for how drawings are made, so that anyone trained in the standard reads a drawing the same way. The course uses British Standard conventions and lists the ones you must use in Appendix 2 of the course specification. In the Plan Room, think of them as Admiralty orders: not suggestions.

The first rule is line type. Every line on a technical drawing has a weight (thick or thin) and a pattern (continuous, dashed, or chain), and the combination tells the reader what the line means. Only one kind of line is thick all the way along: the outline. Everything else is thin, so that the shape of the object always stands out.

Worked example 2 · Describe · line types

An elevation of a deck cleat shows a hole drilled right through it from front to back. Describe how the hole is shown in the elevation and in the end elevation. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. In the end elevation the hole is hidden inside the cleat, so its two edges are drawn as thin dashed (hidden detail) lines. Looking from the side you cannot see the hole: hidden detail.
  2. In the elevation the hole is seen end-on, so it is drawn as a circle in a thick (outline) line. You look straight into it: a visible edge.
  3. A thin chain centre line (long dash, dot) runs along the hole's axis in the end elevation, and two crossing centre lines mark its centre in the elevation. Every hole gets its centre lines: they also show where it is dimensioned from.

3 · Dimensioning and tolerances STD · dimensioning

The anatomy of a dimension

A dimension states one size of the object. It is built from thin lines only:

  • Extension lines (also called projection lines) carry the feature out of the view. Leave a small gap between the object and the extension line, and run the extension line a little past the dimension line.
  • The dimension line runs between the extension lines, with a filled arrowhead touching each one.
  • The size is written above the dimension line, near its middle, in millimetres. Do not write "mm" after every number: the title block says once that sizes are in millimetres. Vertical dimensions are written to be read from the right-hand side of the sheet.
  • A leader line points from a note or size to a feature. It ends in an arrowhead touching an outline (for example, pointing at a hole), or in a dot when it ends inside a surface.

Chain, parallel and running dimensions

When several features lie along one direction you have three choices.

  • Chain dimensioning: each size runs from one feature to the next, all on one line. It clearly shows the distance from any feature to the next one.
  • Parallel dimensioning: every size is measured from the same datum (a reference edge), each on its own line, the smallest nearest the view. Because every size is relative to the datum, it avoids the risk of errors adding up.
  • Running dimensioning: also measured from one datum, but on a single line: the arrows point away from the origin, and each size is written at its extension line.

Why does the choice matter? Every real part is made slightly off-size. With chain dimensions, each feature is positioned from the one before, so the errors accumulate. Try it.

Chain, parallel or running? Make some parts and see the errors pile up

Choose a dimensioning style and a tolerance, then make parts. The magenta bands show (magnified) where each step edge could end up; the dots record where the last edge actually ended on each part you made.

Rules for placing dimensions

  1. Dimension each feature once: no repeated or redundant sizes.
  2. Place dimensions outside the view where you can, smallest nearest the view so that lines do not cross.
  3. Do not dimension to hidden detail: dimension the feature in the view where it is seen as an outline.
  4. Never use an outline or a centre line as a dimension line (a centre line may be extended to act as an extension line).
  5. Keep the gap between the object and the extension line.

Tolerances

No part can be made to an exact size. A tolerance is the amount a size is allowed to vary. The largest allowed size is the upper limit (maximum), the smallest is the lower limit (minimum), and the tolerance is the difference between them. The size the designer aims for is the nominal size. Appendix 2 gives three ways to write a tolerance, and two labels for whether it matters.

  • Common tolerance: the upper limit is written above the lower limit. Here: maximum 30.95, minimum 30.55, tolerance 0.40.
  • Asymmetrical tolerance: the nominal size with the upper and lower deviations. Here: maximum 30 + 0.35 = 30.35, minimum 30 − 0.55 = 29.45, tolerance 0.90.
  • Symmetrical tolerance: the nominal size ± the same amount. Here: maximum 30.15, minimum 29.85, tolerance 0.30.
  • Functional (marked F): a dimension essential to the function of a component or space, such as the diameter of a shaft that must fit a bearing. Non-functional (NF): a dimension not essential to the function, such as the overall length of a handle.

Functional dimensions usually get tight tolerances, because they decide whether parts fit and work. Non-functional ones can be looser, which makes the part cheaper to make. Tight tolerances cost money: more careful machining, more inspection, more rejects.

The armourer's bench: will this trunnion pin fit its hole?

A Ø20 pin (the shaft) goes into a Ø20 hole. Set the limits of each with the sliders or a preset. The zones are magnified about a thousand times; the table works out the limits and the three ways of writing them.

The names clearance, transition and interference fit are engineering terms that help you think, but they are not in the course specification list. What the exam does ask is that you can read a tolerance and calculate maximum and minimum sizes.

Worked example 3 · Calculate · limits of size

(a) A gun-port hinge pin is dimensioned 45 ± 0.2. State its maximum and minimum lengths. [2] (b) Three plates are chain dimensioned along a bar as 25 ± 0.1, 40 ± 0.1 and 35 ± 0.1. Calculate the largest and smallest possible distance from the first edge to the last. [2]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Maximum = 45 + 0.2 = 45.2 Symmetrical tolerance: add the tolerance to the nominal size.
  2. Minimum = 45 − 0.2 = 44.8 Subtract it for the lower limit.
  3. Largest: 25.1 + 40.1 + 35.1 = 100.3 Chain: every dimension could be at its upper limit at once.
  4. Smallest: 24.9 + 39.9 + 34.9 = 99.7 So the overall length can vary by ±0.3, three times the tolerance of any one dimension. Parallel dimensioning from one datum would keep each edge within ±0.1.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If the drawing is at 1:5, do I write the dimensions five times smaller?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "Never. A dimension always states the real size of the object. The scale changes how big the drawing is, not what the numbers say. That's how the carpenter can build the gun carriage without a calculator."

4 · Third-angle orthographic projection STD · third angle

An orthographic projection describes a 3D object with a set of flat 2D views, each looking straight at one face, with all the views lined up on the sheet. The views are named:

  • Elevation: the view from the front.
  • Plan: the view from above.
  • End elevation: the view from the side (from the left or from the right).

There are two systems for arranging the views, first angle and third angle. The course uses third-angle projection only.

The glass box

Imagine the object inside a glass box. In third angle the glass is between you and the object: you stand outside, look through a pane, and trace what you see onto that pane. Then the box is opened out flat around the front pane. Watch it happen with the Plan Room's block-built cannon.

The glass box: projecting and unfolding the cannon in third angle

Press Play, or use the step buttons or the slider. The magenta dashes in step ② are projectors from the cannon to each pane. Notice which edge of each pane stays next to the elevation.

When the box is flat, the rules of third angle can be read straight off the sheet:

  1. The plan is drawn above the elevation, directly in line with it.
  2. An end elevation is drawn on the side it is viewed from: the view from the left goes on the left of the elevation; the view from the right goes on the right.
  3. In both the plan and the end elevation, the front of the object faces the elevation.
  4. The sheet carries the third-angle projection symbol, so that nobody reads it as first angle.

The symbol is itself a tiny third-angle drawing of a truncated cone: the elevation of the cone, with the view from its small end drawn on the left (two circles: the small end and the large end behind it). Learn to draw it from memory; it is a template item in the assignment.

Projection and alignment

The views share sizes. Width is the same in the plan and the elevation, so they line up vertically. Height is the same in the elevation and the end elevation, so they line up horizontally. Depth appears in both the plan and the end elevation; in the plan it runs up the page, in the end elevation it runs across. Carry depth between them with a 45° line (a mitre line), or by measuring. Thin projection lines carry every point from one view to the next. If a feature in one view has nowhere to come from in another, something is missing.

Hidden detail and missing lines

In each view, draw every edge you can see as an outline, and every edge hidden behind material as hidden detail. Finding a missing line is a common question. Use this check, view by view:

  1. Every change of surface (a step, a corner, the edge of a hole) produces a line in every view where the change is seen edge-on.
  2. Take each line in one view and project it across: it must meet a corresponding line, point or circle in the next view.
  3. Where two surfaces at different depths meet in a view, there must be a line between them.
  4. If the edge is behind material from this direction, it is still drawn, but dashed.

The pictorial shows a block model with arrows E (elevation), P (plan) and L (end elevation from the left). Pick the correct view from the four drawings. Every drawing here is generated from the model by the edge rule above, and the wrong ones are typical mistakes.

5 · Sectional views and hatching STD · sections

Hidden detail works for a simple hole, but a part full of bores, steps and grooves becomes a tangle of dashes. A sectional view solves this. Imagine cutting the object with a flat cutting plane, removing the part between you and the plane, and drawing what is left. The inside becomes visible, so it can be drawn in outline and dimensioned clearly.

Cutting-plane notation

  • The cutting plane is drawn in the view where it is seen edge-on, as a thin long dash, dot chain line, thick at the ends.
  • Arrows at the ends point in the direction of viewing: towards the part that is kept.
  • Letters name the plane (A–A), and the sectional view is labelled to match: SECTION A–A.
  • Surfaces the plane cuts are hatched. Edges beyond the plane that you can now see are drawn as outlines. Hidden detail is normally left out of a section, because the section exists to make it unnecessary.

Slide the cutting plane through the bush on its deck plank

A flanged bush is bolted to a deck plank. Move cutting plane A–A in the plan and watch section A–A below. The magenta dashes project the cut edges from the plan down to the section.

Hatching rules

  1. Hatching is thin lines at 45°, evenly spaced.
  2. All areas of the same part are hatched the same way (same direction and spacing), even when the part appears as separate areas in the section.
  3. Adjacent different parts are hatched differently: in the opposite direction, or with a different spacing, so that the boundary between them is obvious.
  4. Some features are not hatched when the cutting plane passes along them lengthways: shafts, spindles, bolts, nuts, screws, pins, washers and keys, and thin ribs and webs. Cutting them would show nothing useful and would make a rib look like a solid block.
  5. Threads are not hatched as threads: at an internal thread, the hatching runs up to the minor diameter (the thick line), as in Appendix 2.

Types of section

  • Full section: the plane passes right through the object, and the whole view is drawn in section.
  • Part section: only a local area is cut away to show one internal feature. The edge of the cut area is an irregular, freehand, thin break line; the rest of the view is drawn normally.
  • Revolved section: the cross-section of a bar, arm or rib is turned through 90° and drawn on top of the view itself, showing its shape where it is cut.
  • Stepped section: the cutting plane changes direction in steps, so that one section passes through features that are not in line. In the section view the steps are not drawn: the view is drawn as if the cut were one flat plane.
  • Cut-away: a pictorial (3D) view with part of the outside removed to show the inside. Common in technical illustration and promotional graphics.
⚠ Exam lookout · hatching an assembly

Past marking instructions for hatching a sectioned assembly have given marks area by area: each part hatched consistently, adjacent parts clearly distinguished, and a mark each for correctly leaving out threads and a web. Marks are lost by hatching a bolt, a shaft or a web, and by changing direction within one part.

Worked example 5 · Describe · hatching a sectioned assembly

A sectioned assembly of a block pulley shows: the cheek plates (two areas, one part), the sheave between them, and the pin through all three. Describe how the section should be hatched. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Hatch with thin lines at 45°, evenly spaced. The basic convention.
  2. Both areas of the cheek plate are hatched in the same direction and spacing. One part, one hatching style.
  3. The sheave is hatched in the opposite direction (or with a different spacing) from the cheek plates. Adjacent parts must be clearly distinguished.
  4. The pin is not hatched: it is drawn in outline. Pins, shafts and bolts cut lengthways are never sectioned.

6 · Assembly drawings and exploded views STD · assemblies

An assembly drawing shows how several parts (at Higher, at least three) fit together to make a product. It is a production graphic: it tells the people building it which part goes where and in what order. It usually includes:

  • the parts drawn in their assembled positions, often as a sectioned assembly so that the internal fits can be seen (with the hatching rules of chapter 5);
  • item references: numbers in circles (balloons) with leader lines to each part;
  • a parts list: a table of item number, part name, quantity and often material;
  • centre lines showing the shared axes on which the parts are aligned.

An exploded view is a pictorial (usually isometric) drawing with the parts pulled apart along the lines on which they assemble, kept in their assembly order and aligned on common centre lines. It shows the reader every part and how it goes together, without needing to read orthographic views. A sectioned exploded view also cuts the parts to show their insides; a full exploded view shows them whole.

7 · Scales STD · scale

A scale is the ratio of the size on the drawing to the real size: drawing : real. Choose a scale so that the object fits the sheet and its details are big enough to read.

  • Full size, 1:1: the drawing is the same size as the object.
  • Enlargement, such as 2:1 or 5:1: the drawing is bigger than the object. Used for small parts, like a pin or a watch gear.
  • Reduction, such as 1:2, 1:5, 1:10: the drawing is smaller. Building drawings use large reductions, typically 1:50 or 1:100 for floor plans and 1:200 or 1:500 for site plans.

The calculation is always the same: drawn size = real size × first number ÷ second number, and the reverse, real size = drawn size × second number ÷ first number. Convert units first (1 m = 1000 mm), and give the unit in your answer.

The Plan Room scale rule: what size on the sheet?

Choose an object (or type a length) and a scale. The bar shows the drawn length on an A3 sheet; the scale bar is graduated in real sizes. Type a measured drawing length to convert it back.

Worked example 7 · Calculate · scale

(a) The Plan Room's floor is 9.6 m long. Calculate its length on a 1:50 floor plan. [1] (b) On a 1:5 drawing a gun carriage measures 320 mm. Calculate its real length in metres. [1]

Show solution [2 marks, typical allocation]
  1. 9.6 m = 9600 mm; 9600 ÷ 50 = 192 mm. Convert to millimetres first, then divide by the second number.
  2. 320 × 5 = 1600 mm = 1.6 m. Reverse: multiply the drawn size by the second number, then convert as asked.

8 · Building construction drawings and BSI symbols STD · building

Building drawings are production graphics too, but a house is far too big and too full of fittings to draw every tap and brick. So building drawings use a set of BSI symbols, from the British Standards Institution: small standard drawings that stand for a fitting, a material, an opening or a feature of the site. Appendix 2 lists the symbols you must know, with their exact names. Typical building drawings are:

  • Location plan: the site in its surroundings (streets, neighbouring buildings), at a large reduction.
  • Site plan: the building on its plot, with boundaries, access paths, drainage, trees, contours and a north point.
  • Floor plan: a horizontal section through the building looking down, showing walls, doors, windows and fittings.
  • Elevations: the outside faces of the building, where window symbols show how each window opens.

  • Windows are drawn in elevation. For a hinged window, the two lines meet at the hinged side: at the middle of the side, the top or the bottom. A pivoted window has a diamond, with its horizontal axis drawn through. A sliding window's arrows point towards the centre division. A fixed window is marked F.
  • Materials (concrete, brickwork, block work, sawn wood, insulation board) are shown by the pattern inside a cut wall or member, as on a section.
  • Trees: existing tree, a circle with a dot; existing tree to be removed, a dotted circle; proposed tree, a circle with a plus sign.
  • The north point shows which way is north, so the reader knows which way the building faces (for sunlight, for example). Contours are lines joining points of equal height, labelled with the height: lines close together mean a steep slope.

Symbol drill: name it, or draw it from memory

Name the symbol: three of the four choices usually come from the same family (all windows, all sanitary fittings…), as the exam's distractors would. Draw it: sketch the named symbol on paper first, then reveal.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2025 course report

"Questions 2(c) and 5(d), in which candidates were asked to identify and apply standard symbols, were very poorly attempted." This is the single most specific warning about standards in the report. Symbols are pure recall, so they are cheap marks once learned: drill this widget until you can name every one, then draw each from memory.

Worked example 8 · Identify and state · site plans

A site plan for a new boathouse shows a circle with a plus sign in it, a dotted circle with a dot inside it, and a circle containing an upward arrow. (a) Name each symbol. [3] (b) State why the site plan includes contours. [1]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Circle with a plus sign: proposed tree. The plus means "to be added".
  2. Dotted circle with a dot: existing tree, to be removed. The broken outline shows it is going.
  3. Circle with an upward arrow: north point.
  4. Contours show how the height of the ground changes across the site, so the builder can see the slope of the land. Lines join points of equal height; close lines mean a steep slope.

9 · Interpreting graphic items STD · interpretation

The question paper's largest swing area is interpretation of graphic items: 12 to 30 of the 90 marks. These questions give you a drawing you have never seen (a pictorial, some orthographic views, a section, an assembly, a building plan) and ask you to read it: name views, identify features and line types, find a missing line, work out a size, describe how it would be modelled. They test spatial awareness: building a 3D object in your head from 2D information, and the reverse.

A routine for an unfamiliar drawing

  1. Orientate. Find the projection symbol and the view labels. Which view is the elevation? Where is the front? In third angle the plan is above, and each end elevation is on the side it was viewed from.
  2. Overall shape first. Read the overall width (plan and elevation), height (elevation and end elevation) and depth (plan and end elevation). Picture the bounding block.
  3. Feature by feature. Take one feature (a hole, a step, a slot) and find it in every view by projecting across. A circle in one view is a rectangle (often in hidden detail) in another.
  4. Read the line types. Thick: you can see it. Dashed: it is hidden. Chain: an axis or a centre. Hatched: material cut by a plane.
  5. Read the numbers. Ø, R, PCD, AF, □ tell you the shape without measuring. Dimensions give real sizes whatever the scale.
  6. Check. Does every line in one view have a partner in the next? A missing partner is either a missing line or your mistake.

Combined views help: a pictorial gives the overall form at a glance, the orthographic views give exact sizes and hidden features, and a section reveals what the hidden lines only hint at. When a question shows several kinds together, use each for what it does best.

⚠ Exam lookout · answering in words and sketches

You may answer with sketches, but you do not have to: marks are for the information you convey, not the quality of the sketch, and you are not required to draw with instruments. Use blue or black ink: the 2025 report reminds candidates that scripts are scanned, and pencil or highlighter may not be visible to the marker. Where a question says "explain", give the cause as well as the effect.

Worked example 9 · Interpret · reading a drawing

A third-angle drawing of a block shows, in the end elevation, a horizontal hidden line a third of the way down from the top, running all the way across. The elevation shows a rectangular notch in the middle of the top edge. Describe the feature and explain why the end elevation shows a hidden line. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. The feature is a slot cut across the top of the block, running from front to back. The notch in the elevation goes all the way through, because the end elevation's line runs right across.
  2. The slot's floor is one third of the block's height down from the top. Read the height from the hidden line's position.
  3. From the side, the floor of the slot is behind the solid end of the block, so the edge cannot be seen and is drawn as hidden detail. Cause (behind material) and effect (dashed thin line): what "explain" needs.
✈ Flight Lieutenant Murray's logbook: this deck in eight lines
  • Preliminary graphics design, production graphics make, promotional graphics market.
  • Thick continuous is the outline; everything else is thin. Hidden: dashed. Centre: long dash, dot. Fold: long dash, double dot. Cutting plane: chain, thick at the ends.
  • Dimensions: thin lines, a gap at the object, sizes in mm above the line, real sizes at any scale.
  • Chain dimensions accumulate error; parallel and running work from one datum.
  • Common, asymmetrical, symmetrical: maximum and minimum are a sum and a difference.
  • Third angle: plan above, end elevation on the side it is viewed from, symbol on the sheet.
  • Sections: 45° thin even hatching, one style per part, opposite for neighbours, never hatch shafts, bolts, nuts, washers, pins, ribs or webs.
