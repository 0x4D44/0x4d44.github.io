The Admiralty Commission · the 50-mark assignment

The assignment: eight hours in the Plan Room

Before a cadet's plans go to sea, the Admiralty asks for one complete commission: drawings a shipwright can build from, a scene and a poster that sell the idea, and quick sketches that show you can think with a pencil. The Higher Graphic Communication assignment is that commission. This deck explains the rules exactly, decodes the 2025 task mark by mark, and trains the skills that carry over to whatever task you are given.

⚓ Ship's log: Captain Isobel Grant, HMS Vellum

"Every year the Admiralty sends a sealed brief to the Plan Room. The ship changes, the parts change, the poster changes. What never changes is what the Board is looking for: drawings that follow the standards, a scene that sits properly on its deck, a layout planned before it is built, and a sketch done by hand and eye. Learn those, and the sealed brief holds no terrors."

The assignment is one of the two parts of your course assessment. It is worth 50 of the 140 marks and you do it in school, on a computer and on paper, over 8 hours of class time. The other part, the question paper, is covered on the Exam drill deck. The assignment asks you to do graphics: to model, draw, render, lay out and sketch. The question paper asks you to explain them. They reward the same knowledge from two sides, which is why chapter 7 links them.

Where these facts come from

The rules come from the Higher Graphic Communication course specification (version 2.0) and the published assignment documents. The worked marking comes from the 2025 assignment and its marking instructions, and the quotations come from the 2025 course report. The assessment was set by SQA; Qualifications Scotland replaced SQA on 1 February 2026 and runs the same course. The task itself changes every year. Where this page uses the 2025 task, it says so, and it tells you which details were only that year's examples.

The three areas and how marks are spread

The course specification divides the 50 marks into three areas of graphics, each with a range of marks.

  • Preliminary graphics (5–15 marks): the quick, early graphics a designer makes to explore and communicate ideas: thumbnails, layouts, annotated sketches, freehand views.
  • Production graphics (10–25 marks): the accurate drawings a manufacturer works from: related orthographic views, sections, assemblies, exploded views, all dimensioned and drawn to British Standard conventions.
  • Promotional graphics (10–25 marks): the graphics that sell a product or idea: rendered scenes and desktop-published layouts such as posters or leaflets.

The specification adds that the three areas "may, or may not, be thematically related". In practice the recent tasks have followed one story: in 2025, a theme park needed production drawings for a turnstile, a rendered scene of its entrance, a promotional poster, and sketches of a queue-jump wristband. The 2018 specimen task, which does print it, also came to preliminary 10, production 20 and promotional 20, though its tasks were shaped quite differently: 20, 21 and 9 marks, with a perspective sketch and a folded table talker instead of a three-view sketch and a poster. The lesson is that the mix inside each task changes from year to year, but production and promotional graphics carry most of the marks.

Two numbers are worth remembering. Over the whole assignment you earn on average 50 ÷ 8 = 6.25 marks per hour, which is 9.6 minutes per mark. And a task's share of the time should be roughly its share of the marks. If you spent four hours polishing Task 1 in 2025, you would have taken an hour from Task 2, where those 60 minutes were worth about 6.5 marks.

Read the brief like a contract

On day one, read the whole task before you open any software. Underline every must and every do not; in 2025 these included "do not include dimensions or hidden detail" in the assembly, "you must not render the exploded view" and "you must not include centre lines, colour, highlights or shadows" in the sketch. Doing what the brief forbids wastes time; leaving out what it requires loses marks.

Exam lookout · positive marking

The assignment is marked positively: you collect marks for the relevant skills you show, and marks are never taken away for errors or omissions. A mistake in one view does not cost you the marks you earned in another, so an incomplete drawing still earns what it shows. Never leave a task blank because you are short of time; a quick, correct partial answer beats nothing.

2 · Production graphics: drawings a shipwright can build from ASG · production · STD

Production graphics are the drawings that let somebody make a product: every view, size and note a manufacturer needs, drawn to agreed standards so that nobody has to guess. In the assignment you make them electronically, and in 2025 the brief said the drawings must be generated from a 3D CAD model you have produced yourself. So the production task is really two jobs: model the parts accurately, then present them as standard drawings.

⚓ Ship's log: Master Shipwright Euan Fraser

"A plan that a carpenter can misread will be misread, usually at sea. Every line type, every label and every dimension on my drawings is there so the next person does not have to ask me what I meant."

The workflow, in order

  1. Read the data sheets for each part: the draft drawings, sizes and orientation. The 2025 brief told you to use the same orientation as the views on each data sheet; marks for views depended on it.
  2. Model each part in 3D CAD (extrude, revolve, arrays, fillets and so on: see the CAD deck). Top-down or bottom-up modelling were both allowed in 2025.
  3. Assemble the parts with 3D constraints, adding any supplied STEP files (stock or library parts).
  4. Generate drawing views on an A3 template: base view, projected views, sections, detail views, at the scales the brief asks for.
  5. Dress the drawing: dimensions, centre lines, hidden detail where asked, view labels, the title block and the third-angle symbol.
  6. Print and check that lines, hatching and text are legible at the printed size.

Related orthographic views in third angle

Related orthographic views are two or more flat views of an object, each looking straight at one face, arranged so that they line up with each other. In the third-angle projection system used in this course, each view is placed on the side it was seen from: the plan (view from above) goes above the elevation, and the view from the right goes to the right of the elevation. Thin projection lines show how a feature carries from one view to the next. The Standards & views deck unfolds the glass box in detail.

Three habits earn the "related views" marks:

  • Orientation. Choose the same front as the data sheet. A perfect plan of the part turned through 90° does not match what was asked.
  • Alignment. Let the CAD software project the views from the base view so they stay aligned; do not drag a projected view out of line.
  • Hidden detail where asked. In 2025 the swivel's plan and end elevation had to show hidden detail, but the assembly drawing and the exploded view had to leave it out. Check the brief for each drawing.

Sections: full, stepped and detail views

A sectional view shows the object as if it had been cut along a cutting plane and the near part removed, so you can see inside. Where solid material has been cut, it is hatched: thin lines at 45°, evenly spaced. The cutting plane is drawn on the adjacent view as a thin chain line, thick at its ends and wherever it changes direction, with arrows showing the direction of viewing and letters naming it (A–A); the section view is labelled to match (SECTION A–A).

  • A full section cuts straight through the whole object on one plane.
  • A stepped section uses a cutting plane that steps (turns through right angles) so that it can pass through features that are not in line, such as three holes in a staggered pattern. The section view is drawn as if it were all one plane: no line is drawn where the plane steps.
  • A detail view (or enlarged partial view) is an enlarged copy of a small region, marked on the main view with a circle and a letter, and labelled with that letter and its scale. In 2025 the detail views were drawn at stated scales (for example 1:4 beside main views at 1:10).
Hatching rules you will be expected to apply
  • Thin lines at 45°, equally spaced, on cut material only. Holes and empty space stay white.
  • One part keeps the same hatching in every view. In an assembly, adjacent parts are hatched differently: a different direction or a different spacing.
  • Some items are not hatched even when the plane passes along them: webs and ribs, shafts, bolts, nuts, washers, screws and threads. In the 2018 specimen marking, the nut, washer and machine screw "must not be sectioned".

Assembly drawings and exploded views

An assembly drawing shows the finished product with all its parts (the course asks for a minimum of three) in their working positions. The 2025 marking gave a whole mark for "accurate assembly of all components… (no overlaps or gaps)", so your 3D constraints matter: mate the faces that touch, align the axes that share a centre line (centre axis), and check nothing floats. If parts overlap in the model, they will overlap in every view generated from it.

An exploded view shows the parts pulled apart along the directions in which they assemble, in order, so a reader can see how the product goes together. An exploded isometric does this in an isometric pictorial view. The two 2025 marks were for producing the exploded isometric at all, and for correct alignment, spacing and order with no overlaps. The 2025 brief also said: no hidden detail, a line drawing, and not rendered.

Dimensioning to British Standard conventions

Dimensions tell the maker the sizes. The course uses British Standard conventions (Appendix 2 of the course specification), and the 2025 brief asked for linear (chain and parallel), radial, diameter and angular dimensions, each at least once across your drawings.

  • Chain dimensioning: dimensions follow on from each other, end to end, in a line.
  • Parallel dimensioning: several dimensions all measured from one common datum (a reference edge or face), stacked in parallel. This avoids the build-up of errors that a long chain can cause.
  • Radial: a radius, marked R (for example R15), with a leader to the arc.
  • Diameter: marked with the symbol Ø (for example Ø15).
  • Angular: an angle in degrees, with a dimension line drawn as an arc.

Good dimensioning is about choosing, not about quantity. The 2025 marks asked for "appropriate dimensions for manufacture": the sizes someone would need to make the part, each given once. A few conventions help the marker read your work:

  • Leave a small gap between the outline and each extension (projection) line, and let the extension line run a little past the dimension line.
  • Put dimensions outside the view where you can, and give each size once, on the view that shows that feature most clearly.
  • Put "ALL SIZES IN mm" in the title block rather than writing "mm" on every number.
  • Where the brief mentions tolerances, know the forms in Appendix 2: common tolerance (upper limit written above lower limit), asymmetrical (a nominal size with different upper and lower limits), symmetrical (nominal ± a value), and the idea of functional and non-functional dimensions.

The template: the cheapest marks on the paper

The 2025 1(d) marks were the easiest in the whole assignment to plan for, because you could prepare them before the 8 hours began: you may use a pre-made A3 CAD template. Build one that carries the third-angle symbol, a title block with spaces for your name, the drawing title, the project title, scale and date, and the notes "ALL SIZES IN mm" and a general tolerance. Then, on every sheet, label each view: ELEVATION, END ELEVATION, PLAN, SECTION A–A, DETAIL B.

The Admiralty sheet inspector

Tick what your drawing sheets include. The A3 preview fills in as you tick, and the inspector scores the three standards-and-conventions marks the way the 2025 marking instructions describe them: every dimension type, any three template items, and each view type labelled at least once. The extra items at the end are marked in other parts of the task, so they light up the sheet but do not change this score.

3 · Promotional graphics: from thumbnails to the final poster ASG · promotional · DTP

Promotional graphics exist to catch a reader's eye and sell something: a product, an event, a theme park, a place in the squadron. In the assignment the promotional task has two halves that are marked together as a story: you plan the layout in preliminary thumbnails, then you build the final version in desktop publishing (DTP) software. In 2025 these were task 2(b), two preliminary layouts worth 6 marks, and task 2(c), the final poster worth 11. (The rendered scene, 2(a), is chapter 4.)

The brief gives you the raw materials, and some of them are fixed. In 2025 you received a set of company logos and colours, the copy text, and a bank of approved images, and you were told you could not alter the company name, logos or colours. You could use the images as supplied or edit them, and add your own graphic items made within the 8 hours. The poster had a set size (built at a scale of 1:3), a divide between two sections, and a border in which no text was allowed. Those particular sizes were 2025's; expect a different format each year (the 2018 specimen used a folded table talker), but expect the same kinds of constraint.

2(b): two preliminary layouts, mark by mark

A thumbnail is a small, quick layout sketch that shows where the main items go. In 2025 you produced two, by hand or on the computer, and every listed item had to appear on both.

Identify here means label it on the thumbnail, with a leader line to where it is used, and a few words saying how. "Emphasis" written in the margin earns less confidence than "Emphasis: the red flag is the largest, warmest shape, so the eye lands on it first". You only need to identify each choice once, but you must then use the same choices in the final poster.

If you name "drop caps" on a thumbnail and then forget to use a drop cap in the final poster, you lose twice. (The marking instructions do not say how the 2 marks for each pair are split; one mark for each effective use is the natural reading, and it is the one our self-marker below assumes.)

Exam lookout · the 2(c) ceiling, from the 2025 course report

"Task 2(c) showed some improvement in terms of skill and visual impact, but most candidates were unable to demonstrate a higher level." The report's advice: in task 2(c), candidates "must consider the overall skill they are trying to demonstrate and the visual impact that they are trying to create", and "at Higher, candidates should be able to identify a range of design elements, principles, DTP terms and techniques in task 2(b). This supports a coherent, high-quality layout."

Climbing the visual-impact ladder

The last 3 marks are where most candidates stall, so it is worth knowing what moves a layout up a rung. The marking uses the words skill (how well things are made and placed) and visual impact (how strongly the poster grabs and holds attention).

What usually separates the top rung from the middle ones (these are general design habits, not marking rules):

  • One clear focal point. Decide what the reader sees first and give it dominance: size, contrast or colour that nothing else competes with. A poster where five items are the same size has no entry point.
  • A grid, kept. Set margins, columns and gutters before you place anything, and snap items to them. Consistent alignment is what "skill" looks like from across a room.
  • White space on purpose. Empty space around the headline is what makes it read. Filling every gap is the commonest sign of a basic layout.
  • Restraint with type. Two contrasting fonts (for example a bold display face for the headline, a plain sans serif for copy), each used consistently, at a few clear sizes.
  • Colour from the brief. Use the supplied CMYK values exactly, and use them to tie the images to the layout (a colour from a photograph repeated in a band or a rule creates unity).
  • Techniques that serve the design. Transparency to blend an image into a background, text wrap round a cut-out shape, a drop cap to start the copy: each should do a visible job, not just appear.
  • Finish. No text touching edges, no stretched images, no pixelated logos (use the vector version where one is supplied), consistent spacing.

Colour theory with colours you cannot change. The brief fixes the company colours, but you still choose how to use them. Decide which is the accent: a small amount of a strong, warm colour (a signal red, say) used only where you want the eye to go. Warm colours such as red and orange are advancing: they seem to come towards the reader, so they suit the focal point. Cool colours such as blue are receding: they sit back, so they suit large backgrounds and bands. Repeating the same few colours across the poster gives harmony, and placing a light colour against a dark one gives the contrast that makes reversed text readable.

Generating ideas. The specification asks you to generate ideas to support a proposal, and the first 2(b) mark is for two effective ideas. Before drawing either thumbnail, jot three or four quick alternatives the size of a stamp (different focal points, different grids, image-led or type-led) and develop the two most different. The 2025 course report also points teachers to the Understanding Standards materials, which show what a higher level of skill and visual impact, and the 3 marks for the last 2(c) criterion, actually look like. If your teacher shares them, study them before the assignment.

Why CMYK? Printed work is made from cyan, magenta, yellow and key (black) inks, so a brief that specifies CMYK values is telling you the colours as the printer will produce them. Enter the numbers given; do not pick a colour "by eye" from the screen.

The poster review board: mark your own 2(c)

Work down the 2025-style criteria for your final poster, honestly. The preview builds a poster that matches your answers, and the board totals a mark out of 11 and tells you which change is worth the most. It applies the 2025 marking instructions as we read them, with one mark per effective use in each pair: a way to practise judging your own work, not a prediction of a real mark.

"If I use every DTP technique I know, surely the poster shows more skill?"

A poster with a drop cap, a text path, three drop shadows and a gradient all fighting each other has low visual impact, however clever each trick. 4 · Rendered scenes: siting, materials, light and render quality ASG · ILL · CAD render

A rendered scene (or rendered pictorial illustration) is a realistic image the CAD software calculates from your 3D models: it works out how light from your light sources falls on each surface, with its material, and produces a picture. A scene places the product in a relevant setting (a floor, walls, other objects) so the viewer can imagine it in use. The specification calls this "creating scenes that place 3D models in relevant contexts", and it lists the 3D-rendering techniques you need: light source, materials, reflections, shade and sited environment. The CAD deck covers the commands; this chapter is about what earns marks.

The 2025 task 2(a), mark by mark

In 2025 you had to place four copies of your assembled turnstile among supplied STEP files (the walls and floor, a waterbase sign and a bin) to match a viewpoint shown on a data sheet. The brief insisted on a render, not a screen grab, free from pixelation and grain, printed on a landscape sheet scaled to fill the page. Its 9 marks:

The turnstile, the theme park and the sign are 2025 details. The checklist they add up to is general: every model present, at the right scale, resting on something, in the right place, with the right appearance, seen from the right place, well lit, and rendered cleanly.

Siting: on the surface, not in it

The siting mark fails in two opposite ways. A model that floats a few millimetres above the floor gives itself away with a gap of light under it and a shadow that does not touch its base. A model that interferes sinks into the floor or overlaps another model. In both cases the cure is the same as in an assembly: use constraints (mate the base face to the floor face) rather than dragging by eye, then check from a low viewpoint before rendering.

Relative scale matters too. If a supplied STEP file was modelled in different units, it may arrive ten or a thousand times too big or too small. Compare it with a known size (a door, a person-height wall) before you place it.

Materials and decals

A material (or appearance) tells the renderer how a surface looks: its colour, how shiny it is, whether it is transparent, whether it has a texture such as wood grain or brushed metal. Apply the materials the brief specifies; where it gives a choice, pick what the real object would be made of. A decal is an image applied to a face of a model, like a sticker: in 2025, a poster on a sign. It must be on the correct face, the right way up, and scaled to fit without stretching.

Lighting: what "skilled" looks like

Light is what turns a model into a believable object. The ideas come from the illustration part of the course (light, shadow, reflection, tone):

  • Tone reveals form. Each face of an object at a different angle to the light should be a different tone. If the light is straight behind the camera, or the ambient (fill) light is so strong that every face is equally bright, the object looks flat.
  • Shadows ground objects. A cast shadow that touches the base of an object says "this rests here". Shadows switched off, or so faint they vanish, lose that.
  • Highlights show the material. A small bright highlight on a shiny surface says "metal" or "gloss"; its absence says "matt".
  • Light from above and to one side (like the sun in the morning) usually shows form best. Avoid lighting so harsh that shadows are pure black and detail disappears, and so dim or so bright that the image is under- or over-exposed.

Render quality: no pixels, no grain

Pixelation means you can see the square pixels: the image was output at too low a resolution for the size it is printed. An A3 sheet is 420 × 297 mm, which at a print resolution of 300 pixels per inch (ppi) needs about 4961 × 3508 pixels: a screen-sized 1920 × 1080 image stretched to A3 is far too coarse. A ray-traced render works out the light by following many rays through each pixel; each round of rays is a sample (some software calls them passes), and the more samples it averages, the smoother the result. Grain (or noise) is the speckle left when too few samples have been calculated: let the render run longer, or raise its quality setting. And a screen grab of the modelling window is not a render at all: the brief ruled it out.

The lighting rig: what earns the 2 + 2

A small deck scene with a crate and a gun-carriage block. Move the light round and up, change the fill light, switch shadows, lift the models off the deck, and change the render's samples and output resolution. The panel applies simple rules taken from the marking descriptors (faces must differ in tone, shadows must be on and attached, exposure must be sensible, no visible pixels or grain) to suggest which rung each ladder would reach. It is a teaching model of the descriptors, not how a marker judges a real render. Press play to orbit the light.

Render checklist before you print
  • Every model in the scene? Every one resting on a surface, none overlapping?
  • Sizes believable relative to each other?
  • Positions and camera viewpoint match the data sheet?
  • Materials as specified; decal on the right face, right way up, not stretched?
  • Shadows on and attached; faces in different tones; at least one highlight on a shiny surface?
  • Rendered (not a screen grab), at a resolution big enough for the printed size, with no visible grain?
  • Printed landscape and scaled to fill the page, as the 2025 brief required?

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Stretching adds no detail: it makes every pixel bigger, and that is exactly what 'pixelation' means. 5 · Preliminary graphics and sketching ASG · PICT sketching

Preliminary graphics are the quick graphics made early in a design: thumbnails, annotated sketches, rough layouts and freehand views. Their job is to communicate an idea quickly, not to be beautiful. The course specification says so plainly: at this stage the purpose is to communicate relevant information rather than to demonstrate presentation skills. That is good news in an 8-hour assignment: neat, correct and quick beats slow and decorated.

The assignment has two kinds of preliminary graphic. The layout thumbnails for the poster (chapter 3) and a sketching task. In 2025 the sketch was a freehand orthographic drawing of a wristband, from a draft CAD drawing on a data sheet. In the 2018 specimen it was a two-point perspective sketch of a simple scene. The specification's sketching skills are: proportion, line quality, vanishing points, line sketching using related orthographic views, single- and two-point perspective, and oblique and isometric forms. Any of these could appear, so practise all of them.

A three-view sketch, step by step

  1. Study the object first. Find its overall width, depth and height, and how they compare: "about one and a half times as wide as it is high". You are estimating proportion by eye, which is allowed; measuring the data sheet is not.
  2. Box in the elevation with light construction lines at its overall width and height.
  3. Project upwards with light vertical lines from the elevation's edges, and box in the plan above it at the right depth. Every vertical edge in the elevation lines up with the same edge in the plan.
  4. Project sideways for an end elevation if one is asked: its height comes straight across from the elevation, and its width equals the plan's depth.
  5. Add the features inside each box (steps, holes, slots), still lightly, checking each lines up across the views.
  6. Line in the visible outlines with firm, dark, confident strokes. Leave the construction lines faint; they show your method and need not be rubbed out.
  7. Add hidden detail as short, evenly spaced dashes, wherever a feature is behind a surface in that view.

Line quality and proportion

Line quality is the clarity and consistency of your lines: construction lines faint, outlines bold and continuous, hidden detail evenly dashed. Proportion is the relationship between sizes: a sketch is "to fair proportions" when its width, height and depth compare the way the real object's do, even though no size is measured. Two tips for proportion: compare everything with one unit (the height of the base, say, and how many of those make the width), and draw the overall box before any detail, so detail cannot push the object out of shape.

Line up the projectors

The elevation is fixed. Drag the plan and the end elevation into position (or focus one and use the arrow keys), and use the sliders to correct the size you sketched each view at. Thin projectors run from every edge of the elevation: they turn green where they meet the matching edge of another view. The checks are computed from the views' positions and sizes. Press "Re-sketch" for a new set of mistakes to correct, or "Show me" to watch the views move into third-angle positions.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I can't measure, how can the plan be the right width?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "You don't measure it: you project it. Draw the elevation first, then run light lines straight up from its ends. The plan's width is whatever lies between those lines, so it cannot be wrong. Projection is how the views agree without a ruler. That is also why the third mark is for 'projected and aligned'."

Practise the other sketch types too

Because the sketching task changes, keep all the specification's types in your hands: isometric (verticals vertical, the two horizontal axes at 30°, circles as ellipses), oblique (front face true shape, receding lines usually at 45°), and one- and two-point perspective (horizontal edges converging on vanishing points on the horizon line). The Forms & pictorials deck covers each one.

6 · Presenting ten A3 pages, and a mock brief ASG · presentation

Everything you hand in is paper, and the marker has nothing else. So the last skill of the assignment is making your evidence easy to find and easy to credit.

The page budget

The 2025 brief suggested four or five A3 pages for Task 1, three for Task 2 and one for Task 3: eight or nine in all, inside the guide of ten. Treat the guide as a budget. Plan your sheets before you start: which drawing goes on which page, and roughly where.

Ordering and labelling

  • Label every piece of work with its task number (for example "Task 1a(ii)"). Put it in the title block or a clear heading.
  • Label the back of every page with your name, date of birth, Scottish Candidate Number (SCN), centre name and centre number. Your teacher may help with labelling, printing, collating and stapling.
  • Keep task order. You do the tasks in order and you should present them in order.
  • Label every view on production sheets, and every identified element, principle and DTP technique on your thumbnails.
  • Print at a legible size. Check that thin lines, hatching and small dimension text survive printing. The render and poster should fill their sheets.

Reviewing, evaluating and justifying

The course specification says the assignment gives you the chance to "review, evaluate and justify" your decisions on the choice of graphic items and techniques. In the 2025 marking there was no separate evaluation mark, but this thinking earned marks all the same, in three places:

  • In 2(b), identifying your design elements, principles and DTP techniques with a short reason is a justification. The 2018 specimen went further and asked candidates to "annotate and justify" their use of rhythm and emphasis, with marks for full justification.
  • Between 2(b) and 2(c), the mark for "a relationship between planning… and the final layout" rewards reviewing your thumbnails and developing the stronger one.
  • Before you print, review each drawing against the brief's list. That review is where marks are found, not lost.

A justification links a cause to an effect: "I used reverse text on the dark band so that the headline stands out against the busy photograph." This is exactly the "explain" skill the 2025 report says most candidates struggle with in the question paper ("only give the effect, without the cause"), so practising it here pays twice.

A mock brief: the Vellum commission

Here is an original practice assignment in the same shape as a real one, written for this site (it is not an SQA task, and its mark split is ours). It deliberately differs from 2025, to remind you that the shape changes: Task C has two sketches. Give yourself 8 hours across several sessions, closed book, and use the self-assessment grid afterwards.

⚓ Admiralty brief: HMS Vellum gun-deck refit and recruitment drive

The Plan Room of HMS Vellum is refitting the training guns on the gun deck and launching a recruitment campaign for 1805 (Blockhaven) Squadron. You are asked to produce production drawings for a training gun carriage, a rendered view of the gun deck, a recruitment poster, and preliminary sketches of two small fittings.

Your data is on the data sheet below: a three-part carriage. Part 1, the carriage body, is a block 300 long × 160 wide × 120 high with R20 rounded top corners at each end, a slot 40 wide and 30 deep along its full length, and a Ø30 axle hole right through its width. Part 2, the truck (wheel), is Ø140 × 40 thick with a Ø30 bore and six Ø20 lightening holes in a radial array on a 90 pitch circle. Part 3, the axle, is Ø30 × 260. Two trucks fit on the axle hard against the body's sides, and the axle projects 10 beyond each truck. All sizes are in mm.

Task A, production (18 marks, suggested 3 hours, 4 A3 pages). (a) Related orthographic views of the body, with hidden detail and dimensions for manufacture [4]. (b) A plan and a sectional elevation of the truck through its centre, a detail view of the bore with at least three dimensions, and hidden detail on the plan [4]. (c) An assembled drawing: a sectional end elevation on cutting plane X–X (a vertical plane through the axle's centre line, so the axle is cut lengthways: remember which parts are not hatched), a full sectional elevation on Y–Y (along the slot), and an enlarged view of one truck on the axle; no dimensions, no hidden detail [4]. (d) An exploded isometric line drawing of the assembly, not rendered [2]. (e) Standards on all drawings: dimension types, template, view labels and line types [4].

Task B, promotional and preliminary (24 marks, suggested 3 h 30 min, 3 A3 pages). (a) A render of two assembled carriages on a timber gun deck against a planked bulkhead, with the squadron crest as a decal on the bulkhead, lit as if by daylight through a gun port [7]. (b) Two preliminary layouts of an A3 portrait recruitment poster, "Join the Plan Room", with a divide one third of the way down; copy text of your own (40–60 words), two contrasting fonts, the squadron crest, three images (your render, a signal-flag hoist, a Grob Tutor aircraft) and three CMYK colours (navy C100 M70 Y0 K40, signal red C0 M100 Y100 K0, flag yellow C0 M15 Y100 K0). Identify two design elements or principles and two DTP techniques [6]. (c) The final poster at full size, using your choices from (b) [11].

Task B data. Use your own render as one image; draw the signal-flag hoist and the Grob Tutor yourself within the time (simple flat shapes are fine); the squadron crest is a circle with a red triangle, as in Figure 3.1.

Task C, preliminary (8 marks, suggested 1 h 30 min, 1 A3 page). (a) A freehand isometric sketch of the gun's quoin (a wedge 120 × 80 mm, sloping from 60 mm high to 10 mm, with a Ø25 turned handle on its thick end) [4]. (b) A freehand third-angle elevation and plan of a signal-flag toggle (a Ø20 × 60 mm wooden cylinder with a Ø6 hole across its middle) with hidden detail and no centre lines [4]. Straight edge only.

The marks come to 18 + 24 + 8 = 50. By area: production 18 (Task A), promotional 7 + 11 = 18 (B(a) and B(c)), preliminary 6 + 8 = 14 (B(b) and Task C): all inside the specification's ranges. You can load these marks into the planner in chapter 1. A mark-share time plan would be A 2 h 53 min, B 3 h 50 min, C 1 h 17 min. The suggested times above (3 h, 3 h 30 min, 1 h 30 min) give the sketches a little extra, as the 2025 brief did for Task 3, because freehand work cannot be hurried; the extra comes out of Task B.
