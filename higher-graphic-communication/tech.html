The Signal Room · digital technology in graphic communication

Technology & society

Every drawing, poster and model the Plan Room makes has to be captured, stored, shared, protected and finally output to paper, screen or plastic. This deck covers the hardware, software and networks behind that work, the law and the locks that protect it, the way it has changed industry and everyday life, and how to stay safe in a graphics studio.

What this deck covers. In the course specification this is Using technology in graphic communication (file management, cloud computing, cloud storage, digital rights management, digital input and output devices, advantages and limitations of CAD) and Graphic communication technology and society (the paperless office, use of recycled materials, CAD as it supports manufacturing and other industries, DTP in marketing and promotional activities, remote working, communication crossing international boundaries), plus the safe working practices that support studio work. In the question paper, digital technology in graphic communication is worth 4 to 12 marks. A table at the end maps each chapter to the specification.

⚑ Exam lookout

Do not treat this as the "easy" topic. From the 2025 course report: "Some candidates found Digital Technology in Graphic Communication questions to be very demanding." Five separate question parts touched it. The marks are short (often 1 or 2 each), but they need a precise point, linked to the scenario in the question.

1 · Hardware, software and networks TECH · input & output devices

A graphic communication system is the set of equipment and programs a designer uses to create, store, share and output graphics. It has three parts.

  • Hardware is the physical equipment: the computer itself, and the devices plugged into it.
  • Software is the programs that run on the hardware: CAD (computer-aided design) for 2D drawings and 3D models, DTP (desktop publishing) for page layouts, image-editing software for photographs, and rendering software for realistic illustrations.
  • A network is two or more computers connected so they can share files, printers and an internet connection. A LAN (local area network) links machines in one building, such as a studio. A WAN (wide area network) links sites that are far apart; the internet is the largest WAN.

An input device puts information into the computer. An output device takes information out of it, in a form people can see or hold. The processor in between does the work, and storage keeps the results.

Digital input devices

The question is always "what does the designer need to get into the computer?" A flat drawing, a photograph, a hand movement, or a solid object each needs a different device.

Resolution: pixels per inch and dots per inch

A raster image (a photograph or a scan) is a grid of tiny coloured squares called pixels. Its pixel dimensions are how many pixels wide and tall it is, for example 3000 × 2000. Multiply them and divide by a million to get megapixels: 3000 × 2000 = 6 000 000 pixels, or 6 megapixels.

Resolution is how tightly those pixels are packed when the image is shown or printed:

  • ppi (pixels per inch) describes an image or a screen: how many pixels sit along one inch.
  • dpi (dots per inch) describes a printer or a scanner: how many dots of ink, or samples, it makes along one inch.

The same pixels spread over a bigger print make each pixel bigger. Once each pixel is large enough to see, edges turn into a staircase (pixelation). A common rule for prints looked at close up, such as a leaflet, is about 300 ppi at the final printed size. Large posters seen from across a room can use less, because the eye cannot pick out the pixels from a distance. Vector graphics (shapes stored as maths, such as SVG and DXF) have no pixels, so they stay sharp at any size. That is why logos are kept as vectors (see Design & DTP for raster and vector file types).

Worked example 1 · how many pixels does an A4 print need?

The Plan Room wants to print a photograph of HMS Vellum to fill an A4 sheet (210 mm × 297 mm) at 300 ppi. Calculate the pixel dimensions needed. There are 25.4 mm in one inch. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. Width: 210 ÷ 25.4 = 8.27 inches, and 8.27 × 300 = 2480 pixels. Convert the printed size to inches, then multiply by the pixels per inch.
  2. Height: 297 ÷ 25.4 = 11.69 inches, and 11.69 × 300 = 3508 pixels. So the photograph needs at least 2480 × 3508 pixels (about 8.7 megapixels). A smaller image would have to be stretched, and each pixel would print bigger than 1/300 inch.

Resolution lab: will this photograph print sharply?

Set the pixel width of the image and the width it will print at. The magnifier shows a 10 mm square of the print exactly as the pixels would fall, and the readouts show the effective resolution and the uncompressed file size.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"So is dpi the same as ppi? People seem to use them for the same thing."

Master Shipwright Fraser: "People do mix them up, and you won't lose a mark for it in ordinary use. But strictly, ppi is about the image or the screen: pixels. Dpi is about the printer or scanner: dots of ink, or samples. An inkjet printer makes several tiny dots of cyan, magenta, yellow and black to build the colour of one pixel, so a 1200 dpi printer is not printing 1200 pixels per inch. If you scan at 600 dpi, though, you get a 600 ppi image. That's the one place they line up."

Output devices turn the file into something people can see or hold. Two big families: 2D output (screens and printers) and 3D output (machines that make or cut real objects). The machines that cut and engrave follow vector paths, so they need vector or CAD files, not photographs.

Inkjet or laser? This comparison comes up often. Think about the job: one high-quality colour photograph proof suits an inkjet; three hundred copies of a text-heavy brief suit a laser. When you compare, say what differs and what that means for this job: "The laser printer has a lower cost per page, so printing 300 briefing sheets would be cheaper."

Digital photography

Digital cameras replaced film in graphic work because each photograph is a file the moment it is taken. Typical benefits, each worth a mark only when linked to its result:

  • The photographer can review the image on the camera's screen and re-shoot at once, so a poor shot does not have to wait for film to be developed.
  • Images can be shared instantly by email or cloud, so a client abroad can approve them the same day.
  • One memory card or drive can store thousands of images, with no physical storage space needed.
  • On screen, the image can be enlarged to inspect detail, and viewed without any light in the room.
  • Images can be edited (cropped, colour-corrected, retouched) and placed straight into DTP layouts, websites and CAD renders.

The catches: high-resolution files are large and fill storage quickly; images are easy to copy, which raises the rights problem in chapter 4; and an image with too few pixels cannot be enlarged without pixelation.

Worked example 2 · describe (2 marks)

A designer is producing freehand concept sketches for a new range of ship's lanterns. Describe two advantages of using a graphics tablet and stylus rather than a mouse. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. The stylus is pressure-sensitive, so pressing harder gives a thicker or darker line, letting the designer vary line weight and tone as they would with a pencil. A feature (pressure sensitivity) and what it does for sketching.
  2. Holding a pen is a natural drawing action, so freehand curves on the lanterns are smoother and more controlled than lines dragged with a mouse. A second, different feature. Two versions of "it's easier" would only earn one mark, if any.

Worked example 3 · explain (2 marks)

Explain why a 3D scanner would be used to help design a replacement for the damaged figurehead on HMS Vellum. [2]

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. The scanner measures millions of points on the carved, curved surface of the figurehead (cause), so its complex shape is captured accurately without measuring each curve by hand (effect). Cause and effect, in the context of the figurehead.
  2. The scan can be converted into a mesh and imported into CAD (cause), so the designer can repair the damaged area digitally and 3D print or machine a replacement that matches the original (effect). A second linked point. "It's quicker" on its own, with no cause, earns nothing.

2 · File management TECH · file management

File management is how you name, organise, store, protect and share digital files so that you, and everyone you work with, can always find the right version. A design project can produce hundreds of files: research photographs, sketches, CAD parts, assemblies, drawings, renders, DTP layouts and proofs. Poor file management wastes time and causes expensive mistakes, such as sending a manufacturer last week's drawing.

The Plan Room's rule is Captain Grant's: "If a signal cannot be found when it is needed, it was never sent."

Naming conventions

A naming convention is an agreed pattern that every file name follows. A good convention means anyone can tell what a file is without opening it, and files sort into a sensible order.

Good habits

  • Describe the content: vellum_lantern_body_v03.ipt
  • Use the same order every time: project, item, stage, version
  • Write dates year first, 2026-03-14, so they sort in date order
  • Pad version numbers: v01, v02 … v10, so v10 does not sort before v2
  • Use underscores or hyphens, not spaces or symbols, so files transfer cleanly between systems and to the web

Bad habits

  • IMG_4032.jpg: says nothing about the content
  • poster final FINAL (2).pdf: which is really final?
  • new drawing.dwg: there will soon be ten of them
  • 14/3/26 hull.ipt: slashes are not allowed in many file systems
  • Different people using different patterns on the same project

Folder structure

A folder structure (or directory hierarchy) is the tree of folders the files live in. A good one mirrors how the project works, so files are grouped by stage and type, and it is the same for every project, so people know where to look. It should not be so deep that files are buried six clicks down.

Version control

Version control means keeping track of each saved state of a file, so you can see what changed, who changed it, and go back if a change was wrong. There are three common ways to do it.

  • Numbered versions by hand: save as _v01, _v02 and so on, never overwriting. Simple, but relies on everyone being careful.
  • Version history in cloud storage: the service keeps earlier versions automatically, with the date and the person who saved each one (chapter 3).
  • Product data management (PDM) software, used with CAD in industry: files are checked out to one person to edit and checked in again, so two people cannot overwrite each other's changes to the same part, and every revision is logged.

Back-ups

A back-up is a separate copy of your files kept so they can be restored if the original is lost, damaged, deleted or encrypted by malicious software. A full back-up copies everything; an incremental back-up copies only what has changed since the last back-up, which is quicker and uses less space. Back-ups should be automatic and regular, and you should test that you can actually restore from them.

A widely used guide is the 3-2-1 rule: keep 3 copies of important data, on 2 different types of storage, with 1 copy off-site (away from the building, for example in cloud storage). One fire, flood, theft or failed drive then cannot destroy every copy.

File size and compression

For a raster image, the file size before compression depends on the number of pixels and how much colour information each pixel stores. A full-colour RGB image uses 3 bytes per pixel (one each for red, green and blue), so a 3000 × 2000 photograph is 3000 × 2000 × 3 = 18 000 000 bytes, about 18 MB. Double the width and height and the size goes up four times. A vector file's size depends instead on how many shapes it holds, and a CAD assembly's on how many parts and features it has.

Compression makes files smaller so they store and transfer faster.

  • Lossless compression (PNG, TIFF with LZW, ZIP archives) shrinks the file but restores every pixel exactly. Use it for logos, drawings, and files still being edited.
  • Lossy compression (JPEG) throws away detail the eye is unlikely to notice, giving much smaller files. Too much compression causes blocky artefacts, and each re-save as JPEG loses a little more. Use it for finished photographs on websites and in emails.

File formats and file transfer

The format decides which software can open a file and what it keeps. A native format belongs to one package (such as a particular CAD program's part file) and keeps everything, including the modelling tree. A neutral format such as STEP or IGES can be opened by almost any CAD package, so it is used to send 3D models to a client or manufacturer who uses different software, although the feature history usually does not come across. DXF does the same job for 2D drawings, and 3DS carries 3D models into rendering and animation software. For proofs, a PDF shows a layout the same on any computer, with fonts embedded. The CAD deck and the Design & DTP deck cover these formats in detail.

File transfer means moving files between people or places. Email suits small files but has attachment limits. A cloud share link suits large files and can be limited to named people, set to view-only, or made to expire. A USB drive works without internet but can be lost or carry malware. Whatever the route, send the version the receiver can open (often a neutral format or PDF), name it clearly, and check large files arrived complete.

  1. (a) STEP (or IGES). A neutral 3D format. DXF is 2D, so it would not carry the solid model.
  2. (b) It is a neutral format that can be opened by almost any CAD package, so the shipyard can open the capstan model even though it uses different software. The feature (neutral, widely readable) and its effect in this context (the shipyard can open it).

3 · Cloud computing and cloud storage TECH · cloud

"The cloud" is not in the sky. It is rows of computers called servers, in large buildings called data centres, owned by a provider and reached over the internet. The specification separates two ideas.

Definitions

Cloud storage: keeping files on a provider's remote servers instead of (or as well as) on your own device, so they can be reached from any device with an internet connection and a login.

Cloud computing: using a provider's remote servers to run software and do processing over the internet. Examples in graphics: a CAD package that runs in a web browser, design software rented by subscription and licensed online, and cloud rendering, where a photo-realistic render is calculated on powerful remote servers instead of tying up the designer's own computer for hours.

Most cloud storage services also synchronise (sync) files: when you save a file in a synced folder, the new version is uploaded, and every other device signed in to the same account downloads it automatically.

Benefits of cloud storage and cloud computing

These are the points markers look for. Each one is written as a cause and an effect, which is how you should write it in the exam, set in the scenario of the question.

Benefits, with the cause (what the cloud does) and the effect (what that means for a design team).
BenefitCauseEffect for the design team
Remote accessFiles sit on internet servers, not one machineDesigners can open them from anywhere in the world, so they can work from home, a client's office or another country
Collaboration and sharingSeveral people can be given access to the same folder or fileTeam members and clients work on, or comment on, the current version without emailing copies back and forth
Back-up and restorationThe provider keeps copies and earlier versionsWork survives a lost or broken laptop, and an older version can be restored after a mistake
Version historyEach save is recorded with a date and the person who made itThe team can see who changed what, and roll back

Limitations

  • Internet dependence. No connection, no files (unless copies are kept offline). A slow connection makes uploading large CAD assemblies or high-resolution images tediously slow.
  • Security. If an account's password is guessed or stolen (for example through a fake "phishing" email), an intruder can read, copy or delete everything. Strong passwords and two-step sign-in reduce the risk.
  • Privacy and control. The files are on someone else's computers, possibly in another country with different laws. A client may forbid confidential designs being stored this way.
  • Ongoing cost. Storage and software are usually rented by monthly or yearly subscription, which over time can cost more than buying a drive.
  • Reliance on the provider. If the service has an outage, changes its prices or closes, the business is affected.
  • Sync spreads mistakes. A file deleted or overwritten on one device is deleted or overwritten on all of them. Sync is not the same as a back-up, though version history and a deleted-files bin can rescue you.

Security is a shared responsibility: the provider protects the data centre and its servers, but the user must protect the account (passwords, two-step sign-in, care with share links).

"If everything syncs to the cloud, why would I need a back-up as well?"

4 · Digital rights management and intellectual property TECH · digital rights management

A graphic is easy to copy: one click saves it, and the copy is perfect. That is a problem for the people who made it, because their income depends on others paying to use their work. This chapter is about the law that says who owns a design, and the technical locks that make copying harder.

Intellectual property

Intellectual property (IP) is something created by someone's mind (a drawing, photograph, logo, design, invention or piece of software) that the law treats as property belonging to its owner. The owner can sell it, license it, or take action against people who use it without permission. The main kinds you need to recognise:

Kinds of intellectual property relevant to graphic communication (UK).
ProtectionCoversHow you get it
Copyright ©Original creative work: drawings, photographs, illustrations, layouts, written text, softwareAutomatic in the UK as soon as the work is created and recorded; no registration. For artistic works it lasts for the creator's life plus 70 years
Trade mark ™ / ®Signs that identify a business: logos, brand names, slogansCan be registered (®) to give strong legal protection for the brand
Registered designThe appearance of a product: its shape, pattern, colour or decorationRegistered with the Intellectual Property Office
PatentA new invention: how something worksApplied for and granted; the invention must be new and not obvious

A licence is permission from the owner to use their work, on stated conditions. Buying a stock photograph usually buys a licence to use it in certain ways, not ownership of the photograph. Fonts and CAD software are also licensed: a licence may limit how many computers a font is installed on, and an education licence for CAD software usually forbids commercial work. Passing off someone else's image or design as your own, or using it without the agreed payment, takes income and credit from its owner.

Digital rights management

Digital rights management (DRM) is the use of technology to control how digital content is used once it has been distributed: whether it can be copied, printed, edited, shared or opened at all. The law says what you may do; DRM tries to make it physically hard to do anything else. Methods a graphics business might use:

  • Watermarking: placing a visible logo, name or "PROOF" mark over an image, usually semi-transparent, or hiding an invisible digital watermark in the pixel data that identifies the owner even after copying.
  • Low-resolution previews: putting only small, low-ppi versions online, so a copy cannot be printed well. The full-resolution file is sent only after payment.
  • Encryption: scrambling a file with a key, so only someone with the key can read it.
  • Password protection and permissions: a PDF proof can require a password to open, or block printing, copying or editing.
  • Licence keys and online activation: CAD and DTP software checks that each copy has been paid for before it runs.
  • Metadata: copyright notices and the creator's name stored inside the file's information fields. Easy to strip, but useful as evidence.

Why it matters to designers and companies

  • Income: photographers, illustrators and studios are paid when their work is licensed. Free copies mean lost sales.
  • Reputation and brand: a company logo copied onto poor-quality or offensive products damages trust in the real company.
  • Evidence: a watermark, metadata or a dated version history helps prove who made a design first, if there is ever a dispute.
  • Advertising: a visible watermark carries the company's name with every shared copy.
  • Your own risk: using an image you have no licence for can lead to a demand for payment or legal action, so designers must check the rights for every image, font and model they use.

Watermark workshop: protect the ship photograph

Choose where the watermark goes, how strong it is and how big. Then try the thief's tools. The readouts are measured from the image itself: how much of the watermark survives each crop, and how much of the picture it hides.

What would stop theft? A sorting drill

Classify each measure. Legal protection gives the owner the right to act; a technical barrier makes copying harder; some popular ideas give little real protection.

⚑ Exam lookout

When asked why a company watermarks its images, "to stop theft" is not enough. The 2024 marking instructions said not to accept "theft" or "stealing" without a description. Say what the watermark prevents and why that matters: it identifies the image as the company's intellectual property; it stops others selling or passing off the image as their own; it stops the image being used without payment; it advertises the company wherever the image is shared.

Worked example 7 · explain (2 marks)

The Plan Room sells prints of its ship illustrations through a website. Explain why the illustrations shown on the website are watermarked. [2]

5 · Advantages and limitations of CAD TECH · advantages and limitations of CAD

The course asks you to know the relative merits of manual and computer-aided methods. Manual graphics are produced by hand with pencils, pens, markers, a drawing board and instruments. CAD (computer-aided design) produces drawings and 3D models with software. Neither is "better" in every case: sketching on paper is still the fastest way to get an idea down, and the assignment's preliminary sketches are done by hand. But for production drawings and models that will change, be shared and be manufactured, CAD wins in most ways. You must also know its costs.

Advantages of CAD

  • Accuracy: exact sizes are typed in, and snaps and constraints lock geometry, so parts fit when made.
  • Editing: parametric models and associative drawings update automatically, so changes are quick and consistent.
  • Re-use: parts, features and whole drawings can be copied, mirrored, arrayed or reused in a new project.
  • Libraries: standard parts (bolts, bearings, symbols) are inserted from a library instead of being drawn.
  • Visualisation: photo-realistic renders show a client the product before it exists, and can be used in promotional material.
  • Simulation and testing: stress, motion and clash (interference) checks find problems before anything is made.
  • Collaboration: files can be shared instantly worldwide, and several designers can work on parts of one assembly.
  • Manufacture: the model feeds CAM, CNC machines and 3D printers directly.
  • Standards: templates hold the title block, layers, line types and dimension styles, so every drawing matches the conventions.
  • Storage: thousands of drawings fit on a drive or in the cloud; no plan chests.

Limitations of CAD

  • Cost: powerful computers, software licences or subscriptions, and output devices are expensive.
  • Training: staff need time and money to learn the software, and keep learning as it updates.
  • Dependence: no power, hardware or software means no work; crashes and corrupt files can lose hours.
  • Compatibility: native files may not open in another package; neutral formats like STEP lose the feature history.
  • Security: digital files are easy to copy, steal or hack, and malware can destroy them, so back-ups and protection are needed.
  • Health: long hours at a screen can cause eye strain and posture problems (chapter 7).
  • Early ideas: CAD can be slower than a quick freehand sketch for getting first ideas down.

Sort them: advantage or limitation?

Each statement describes a situation. Decide whether it shows an advantage or a limitation of CAD.

6 · Impact on industry and society TECH · technology and society

The specification lists six ways CAD and graphic communication technologies have changed industry and society. For each, know what changed, who it affected, and the benefits and drawbacks. In the exam, every impact must be explained as a cause leading to an effect.

The paperless office

The paperless office is a workplace that creates, shares, approves and stores documents digitally instead of on paper. Drawings are viewed on screen and marked up digitally; proofs go out as PDFs; filing cabinets become folders on a server or in the cloud. True "zero paper" is rare, so think of it as an aim to reduce paper.

Benefits

  • Lower costs: less spent on paper, ink and toner, printers and their servicing.
  • Smaller workspaces: no plan chests or filing rooms, so less office space is needed.
  • Faster sharing and easier collaboration: a document reaches every team member at once.
  • Easier remote working: files are reachable from home or abroad.
  • Faster searching: a file is found by name or content in seconds.
  • Improved corporate image: the company is seen as modern and environmentally responsible.

Drawbacks

  • Cost of the hardware, software and training to set it up.
  • Dependence on power, devices and networks: no system, no documents.
  • Security risks: hacking, malware and accidental deletion, so back-ups are essential.
  • Screen fatigue: some checking (such as proofreading) is easier on paper.
  • Devices and data centres still use energy, so the environmental gain is not "free".

Use of recycled materials

Graphic products have physical outputs: leaflets, posters, packaging, models, signs. Technology affects how much material they use and where it comes from.

  • Recycled paper and card: printing on recycled stock reduces the trees felled and the energy and water used compared with making paper from new pulp. Look for certification (such as FSC, the Forest Stewardship Council) showing responsibly sourced fibre.
  • Digital proofing: checking layouts on screen and as PDFs means fewer test prints are wasted.
  • Virtual prototyping: testing a CAD model on screen means fewer physical prototypes are made and thrown away.
  • Nesting: CAM software arranges parts tightly on a sheet before laser cutting or CNC routing, so less offcut is wasted.
  • Recycled filament and sheet: 3D printing filament and acrylic sheet can be made from recycled plastic.
  • Design for recycling: designers can choose single materials, avoid laminating card with plastic film, and print recycling symbols on packaging so it can be sorted.

Trade-offs exist: recycled paper can be less bright and may cost more for some grades, and recycling itself uses energy and transport.

CAD as it supports manufacturing and other industries

CAM (computer-aided manufacture) uses the CAD model to generate the instructions (toolpaths) that drive CNC machines, laser cutters and 3D printers. The route from design to product has become largely digital.

Effects on industry:

  • Rapid prototyping: a 3D-printed model can be in a designer's hand within hours, so ideas are tested and improved sooner and products reach the market faster.
  • Accuracy and consistency: CNC machines follow the CAD geometry exactly, so parts are identical and fit first time, with less waste.
  • Global supply chains: a design can be emailed or shared to a factory anywhere, so companies can manufacture where costs or skills suit them best.
  • Customisation: because changes are easy in CAD and 3D printing needs no special tooling, one-off or personalised products (such as medical implants or prosthetics shaped to a patient) are practical.
  • Jobs and skills: fewer manual drafting jobs, more jobs needing CAD, CAM and IT skills, so workers need retraining.

Other industries rely on CAD too: architecture and construction (building models, site plans and walk-through visualisations, with building information modelling sharing one model between architects, engineers and builders); engineering; product design; film, television and games (3D models and animation); fashion and textiles; and medicine (models made from scans to plan surgery).

DTP in marketing and promotional activities

Desktop publishing (DTP) software lets one person design and lay out print and screen documents (posters, leaflets, adverts, social media graphics, menus, packaging) on an ordinary computer. Before DTP, this needed specialist typesetters, paste-up artists and printers.

  • In-house production: even a small business can create its own promotional material, so marketing costs fall and campaigns can start quickly.
  • Quick changes: prices, dates or images can be updated and reprinted, or reposted online, the same day, so promotions respond to events.
  • Consistent branding: master pages, templates and colour swatches keep the logo, colours and fonts the same across every product, which builds a recognisable brand.
  • Many channels from one design: one layout can be exported for print (CMYK, with bleed) and for screens (RGB) and social media.
  • Targeted and personalised material: versions can be made for different audiences, regions or languages.
  • Drawbacks: easy tools mean more low-quality design from untrained users; the market is crowded, so material must work harder to stand out; and traditional print trades have lost jobs.

Remote working

Remote working means doing your job away from the company's office, often at home, connected by the internet. Cloud storage, cloud-based software, video calls and shared screens make it possible for designers.

For the designer

  • No commute: time and travel costs saved, and less pollution.
  • Flexible hours fit family and life.
  • Can live far from the company, including in rural areas.
  • But: isolation; harder to separate work and home; must set up a safe workstation at home; relies on a good internet connection.

For the company and society

  • Smaller offices, so lower rent and energy costs.
  • Can recruit talent from anywhere, not just within commuting distance.
  • Fewer cars on the road at rush hour.
  • But: harder to supervise and support staff; security of company files on home networks; spontaneous teamwork is harder.

Communication crossing international boundaries

Graphics travel across countries and languages more than ever. Technology helps in several ways.

  • Instant sharing: email, cloud storage and video calls let teams and clients in different countries review the same design at the same time.
  • Working across time zones: a team in one country can pass work to a team in another at the end of its day, so a project can progress around the clock.
  • Standards and conventions: drawings follow agreed standards (such as BS and ISO conventions for line types, projection and dimensions), so an engineer anywhere can read them without translation.
  • Graphics instead of words: pictograms, icons, assembly diagrams and exploded views explain a product without any language, which is why flat-pack instructions are often wordless.
  • Neutral file formats: STEP, IGES, DXF and PDF let companies using different software exchange work.
  • Translation and localisation: DTP layouts can be quickly produced in several languages from one master design.

Designers must also be culturally aware. Colours, symbols, gestures and images can carry different meanings in different countries, so promotional graphics are checked for each market.

7 · Safe working practices in graphic studios TECH · safe working

A graphics studio looks safe: no lathes, no furnaces. But people spend long hours at screens, use very sharp blades, spray solvent adhesives, and run laser cutters and 3D printers. The specification asks you to know the safe working practices and systems that support graphic work in studios and other working environments.

The systems behind safe work

  • The law. In the UK the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 requires employers to protect the health and safety of employees and others, and requires employees to take reasonable care and co-operate. The Display Screen Equipment (DSE) Regulations cover people who use screens regularly: employers must assess workstations, plan breaks or changes of activity, and provide eye tests on request. COSHH (Control of Substances Hazardous to Health) covers chemicals such as solvent adhesives and sprays.
  • Risk assessment. A hazard is anything that could cause harm; a risk is how likely that harm is, and how bad. A risk assessment identifies the hazards, decides who might be harmed and how, evaluates the risks and sets control measures, records the findings, and is reviewed regularly.
  • Training and induction. Nobody uses a laser cutter, guillotine or spray booth until they have been shown how.
  • Safety information. Safety data sheets for chemicals, signs on machines, fire exits marked and kept clear, first aid kits, and a clear procedure for reporting accidents.

Workstation ergonomics

Ergonomics is designing the workplace to fit the human body. A badly set-up workstation causes neck, back and wrist pain, and eye strain, over months of work.

  • Screen: top at or just below eye level, about an arm's length away, tilted to avoid reflections. A laptop used for long periods needs a stand with a separate keyboard and mouse, otherwise the user looks down all day.
  • Chair: adjustable height and back, supporting the lower back; feet flat on the floor or on a footrest.
  • Keyboard, mouse, tablet: close enough that the elbows stay by the sides and wrists stay straight.
  • Lighting: enough light to see paper work without glare on the screen; no bright window directly behind or in front of the screen.
  • Breaks: short, regular breaks or changes of task, and looking into the distance from time to time, reduce eye strain and muscle fatigue.

Electrical safety and the room

  • Trailing cables across walkways are a trip hazard: route them along walls or under covers.
  • Do not overload sockets or plug extension leads into other extension leads; overheating can start a fire.
  • Keep drinks away from computers, printers and power strips.
  • Electrical equipment should be checked regularly (portable appliance testing) and damaged cables taken out of use.
  • Keep fire exits and walkways clear; store heavy items such as paper boxes low down, not on high shelves.

Manual tools: knives, rules and mats

Craft knives and scalpels cause more studio injuries than any machine. Safe practice:

  • Cut on a self-healing cutting mat, never on a desk or your lap.
  • Guide the blade along a metal safety rule (ideally with a finger guard). A plastic or wooden rule can be sliced, and the blade can ride over it.
  • Keep fingers behind the rule, on the far side from the blade, and cut away from your body in several light strokes, not one heavy one.
  • Use a sharp blade: a blunt one needs more force, and slips. Snap or change blades with the proper tool and put old blades in a sharps container.
  • Retract or cover the blade when you put the knife down, and carry it pointing down.
  • Rotary trimmers and guillotines: keep the blade guard in place and fingers clear of the cutting line.

Adhesives, sprays and machines

  • Spray adhesives and fixatives: the solvents give off fumes and are flammable. Use them in a spray booth or a well-ventilated area, away from flames and heat, and follow the safety data sheet (a mask may be needed). Spray inside a box or onto scrap paper to control overspray.
  • Laser cutters: never leave one running unattended, because the material can catch fire. Keep the lid closed, use the fume extraction, and cut only approved materials: some plastics, such as PVC, release toxic gases.
  • 3D printers: the nozzle and bed are hot enough to burn; let them cool before removing prints. Use in a ventilated space, as some filaments give off fumes.
  • Vinyl cutters and plotters: keep fingers clear of the moving head and blade.
  • Storage: flammable sprays and solvents in a metal cabinet; heavy materials low down; sharp tools in a rack or drawer, not loose on a bench.

Studio inspection: find the hazards

Worked example 11 · describe (3 marks)

A graphic designer spends most of the day working at a computer. Describe three ways the workstation can be set up to reduce the risk to the designer's health. [3]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. The monitor is positioned with its top at or just below eye level, about an arm's length away, so the designer does not bend the neck to see it. Screen position, with its benefit.
  2. An adjustable chair is set so that the lower back is supported and the feet rest flat on the floor or a footrest, which reduces back strain. Chair and posture.

8 · Exam practice: state, describe, explain TECH · exam technique

Technology questions are short, but they are precise. The command word tells you what kind of answer earns the mark. These are the meanings used in the marking instructions.

Command words. The marking principles are those published in the 2024 question paper marking instructions.
Command wordWhat the marker wantsExample of a full answer
State / Name / IdentifyA short, correct fact. No explanation needed."STEP."
DescribeA statement of characteristics or features, not just a one-word list. One mark per relevant point, in the context of the question."Files can be opened from any device with an internet connection, so a designer can work from home."
ExplainRelate cause and effect, in the context of the question. An effect with no cause rarely earns the mark."Because less paper and toner are bought (cause), the studio's running costs fall (effect)."
CompareSimilarities and/or differences between two things, in context."A laser printer has a lower cost per page than an inkjet, so it is cheaper for 300 copies."

The common errors in this topic

  1. Effect without cause. "It saves time." Why? What does the technology do that saves time?
  2. Vague comparatives. "It is faster", "it is easier", "it is better quality", with no context. Faster than what, at what task?
  3. Repeating one point twice. "You can share files" and "you can collaborate" are close together; for two marks, give two clearly different benefits.
  4. Ignoring the scenario. If the question is about a printing company in Belfast, mention the printers in Belfast.
  5. Bare buzzwords. "Theft", "security", "the environment" need a description of what is protected or what changes.
  6. Wrong device or format. DXF for a 3D solid model; a laser cutter for a photograph; a plotter for 300 A4 leaflets.

Model answers · a mixed set

Six short questions in the style of the paper. Try each before opening the answer.

(a) State one input device suitable for capturing an existing hand-drawn A3 hull plan. [1 mark]
  1. An A3 flatbed scanner. The original is flat, so a scanner gives even lighting with no perspective distortion. A camera could also work but is weaker.
(b) Describe one benefit of the paperless office to a design company. [1 mark]
  1. Drawings and documents are stored digitally, so no space is needed for plan chests and filing cabinets, allowing a smaller, cheaper office. A feature and its consequence.
(c) Explain why a designer would export a logo as an SVG rather than a JPEG for use on a vinyl cutter. [2 marks]
  1. SVG is a vector format, which stores the logo as mathematically defined paths… Cause.
  2. …so the vinyl cutter's blade can follow those paths, and the logo stays sharp at any size; a JPEG is made of pixels, which give no cutting outline and blur when enlarged. Effect, in the context of cutting.
(d) Explain one disadvantage of remote working for a graphic design company. [2 marks]
  1. Company files are opened on home computers and home networks, which may not be as well protected as the office network… Cause.
  2. …so there is a greater risk of confidential client designs being hacked, copied or lost. Effect for the company.
(e) Describe two safe working practices when using spray adhesive to mount a presentation board. [2 marks]
  1. Use the spray in a spray booth or well-ventilated area, so the solvent fumes are not breathed in. Ventilation.
  2. Keep it away from naked flames and heat sources, because the propellant and solvent are flammable. A second, different practice. Spraying into a box to control overspray is another.
(f) Explain how cloud storage allows a design team in different countries to collaborate. [2 marks]
  1. All team members are given access to the same shared folder on the provider's servers, and changes sync to everyone… Cause.
  2. …so each designer always opens the latest version, and team members can comment on or continue the work as soon as a colleague in another time zone saves it. Effect for the team.
