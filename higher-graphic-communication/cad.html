The Model Shop · computer-aided design

CAD

Below the Plan Room, in the Model Shop of HMS Vellum, Master Shipwright Fraser turns voxel blocks into parametric parts: cannon trunnions, capstan drums, speaking tubes, a lifebuoy for every deck. This deck teaches every CAD item in the course specification: 2D tools and constraints, modelling features and edits, terminology, assemblies, file types, rendering, and how to write a "describe how to model it" answer that collects every mark.

⚓ Master Shipwright Fraser's briefing

Master Shipwright Euan Fraser: "In Minecraft you build a ship one block at a time, and if the captain wants the hull a metre longer you rebuild half of it. In CAD you build with instructions: draw this shape, push it out this far, cut this hole here. Change one number and the whole part rebuilds itself. Learn the names of the instructions and the order they go in, and the exam's biggest question is yours."

What this deck covers, and why it matters

Computer-aided design techniques carry 20 to 30 of the 90 marks in the question paper: the joint largest maximum and the joint largest minimum of any area (both shared with desktop publishing). The 2025 course report says CAD questions "performed very well this year", with "a marked improvement in candidate's use of terminology, found in the course specification". The course is package-independent: you will never be asked about a particular program's menus. You will be asked to use the course's generic terms correctly. Every term on this deck is one of them.

1 · What CAD is CAD · generic techniques

Computer-aided design (CAD) is using software to create, edit and present designs. In Graphic Communication it has two halves. 2D CAD draws flat geometry: lines, arcs and circles, like an electronic drawing board. 3D CAD builds a virtual model of the object that you can turn, section, assemble, render and turn into drawings.

After a plain 2D drawing, there are three kinds of 3D model, and it helps to know the difference:

The CAD in this course is solid modelling. A solid model "knows" what is inside it, so the software can cut sections through it, calculate its volume and mass, check that parts do not overlap, and generate orthographic views with the hidden detail worked out for you.

Modern solid modellers are parametric (driven by dimensions you can change) and feature-based (the part is built from a list of operations, called features, that the software remembers in order). Change a dimension and the part rebuilds. The drawings made from the model are associative: linked to the model, so they update too.

Advantages and limitations of CAD

Advantages

  • Accurate to fractions of a millimetre; sizes can be measured from the model.
  • Edits are quick: change a dimension and the model and its drawings update.
  • One model gives orthographic, sectional, exploded and pictorial views and renders.
  • Parts can be checked for fit and interference before anything is made.
  • Files can be shared instantly, stored in the cloud and sent to CNC machines (computer numerically controlled cutters and mills, driven straight from the CAD data) and 3D printers.
  • Library parts (nuts, bolts, bearings) save modelling time.

Limitations

  • Software, hardware and training cost money.
  • Skilled users are needed; complex models take time to build.
  • Files can be lost, corrupted or opened in the wrong version.
  • Different packages' native files may not open in each other (see chapter 7).
  • Early "rough idea" sketching is often faster by hand.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

Your program might call extrude 'pad' or call centre axis 'insert'. Learn the specification's word for each tool and use that word in the exam. That is exactly the improvement the 2025 report praised."

  • say what CAD is and tell 2D drawing from 3D modelling;

2 · 2D drawing tools and constraints CAD · 2D tools · 2D constraints

Every 3D feature starts as a sketch: 2D geometry drawn on a flat plane. The closed shape inside a sketch that a feature uses is the profile. So the first CAD skill is drawing an accurate profile with the 2D tools, then locking it down with constraints.

The drawing tools

ToolWhat it doesTypical use
LineStraight line between two pointsMost profiles; paths
CircleCircle from a centre and a radius or diameterHoles, bosses, round profiles
RectangleFour lines at right angles from two cornersPlates, blocks

ExtendLengthens a line or arc to meet a boundaryClosing gaps

ChamferCuts a corner with a straight lineBevelled corners

The sketch pad: 2D tools on an L-shaped bracket

Pick a tool and change its setting. The new geometry (magenta) is computed from the original profile (grey): offsets keep every line parallel, fillets stay tangent, radial arrays divide the angle correctly.

Arrays: linear, box and radial

Holes in a radial array sit on a pitch circle diameter (PCD): the diameter of the circle through all their centres. (This "pitch" has nothing to do with a helix's pitch in chapter 3, which is a distance along an axis.) The radial array has one trap. Round the full 360°, the copies are 360 ÷ n apart, because the last would land on the first. Through a part angle such as 270°, the first and last copies sit at the ends of the arc, so there are n − 1 gaps: 8 holes through 270° are 270 ÷ 7 ≈ 38.57° apart. If a question says "do not use radial array", you must give that angle. (The 2024 marking instructions printed 38.58; correct to 2 decimal places it is 38.57, and markers accept either.)

2D constraints

A constraint is a rule the sketch must obey. Geometric constraints fix relationships (parallel, tangent); dimensional constraints fix sizes (a length, a diameter). A sketch with no constraints is a rough drawing that will distort the moment you edit it. A fully constrained sketch cannot change except when you change one of its dimensions.

Lock it down: constraints and degrees of freedom

Four points and a circle start with 11 degrees of freedom. Switch constraints on and watch the count fall to zero. Drag the corners, or press "Tug", to see what can still move.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I draw the rectangle carefully to the right size, why bother constraining it?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Because a drawing that is right by accident is wrong by design. The moment someone edits one line, an unconstrained sketch twists out of shape, holes drift off centre and a 'square' corner becomes 88°. Constraints carry the design intent: this edge stays parallel to that one, this hole stays on the centre line, whatever the sizes become."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • name and describe each 2D tool, and choose the right one for a job;
  • work out the angle between instances of a radial array, full or part circle;
  • name each 2D constraint and say what it controls;
  • explain why a sketch should be fully constrained.

3 · Modelling features CAD · extrude · revolve · loft · helix · sweep

A feature is a single modelling operation that adds or removes material. The specification lists five features that turn profiles into solids. Each needs a profile, and each needs something more: a distance, an axis, a second profile or a path. That "something more" is what an exam answer must state.

The worked examples name the plane each sketch starts on. XY, XZ and YZ are the three origin planes every part starts with (chapter 5): XY is flat, like the floor; XZ and YZ stand upright.

Extrude

Extrude pushes a closed profile out of its sketch plane by a set distance, at right angles to the plane. Every cross-section is identical to the profile. You state the profile (shape and sizes), the distance, and if it matters the direction (one way, the other, or symmetrically both ways). An extrusion can add material or, as extrude subtract (a cut), remove it: the usual way to make a hole or slot. "Through all" cuts right through the part whatever its thickness.

Revolve

Revolve spins a profile round an axis. You state the profile, the axis and the angle (360° for a complete part). Anything symmetrical about an axis, round in cross-section (a bottle, a wheel, a cannonball, a bollard), is a revolve: draw half its cross-section against a centre line and spin it. The profile must lie on one side of the axis; if it crosses the axis the solid would pass through itself and the software refuses.

Loft

Loft blends smoothly between two or more closed profiles drawn on separate planes, usually a base plane and one or more work planes offset from it (a work plane is a flat reference surface you can sketch on, placed wherever you need it: chapter 5 has more). Use it when the shape changes along its length: a funnel, a hopper, a boat's bow, a square duct becoming round. State each profile with its sizes, and the offset distance between the planes.

Extrude along a path (sweep)

Extrude along a path, also called a sweep, drives a profile along a path: a line, arc or chain of them in its own sketch. Pipes, handrails, handles and wire forms such as hooks and clips are sweeps. The method has a fixed order:

  1. Sketch the path, and dimension every part of it: each straight length and each bend radius.
  2. Create a work plane perpendicular to the path at one end.
  3. Sketch the profile on that work plane, centred on the end of the path, with its size.
  4. Use extrude along a path, selecting the profile and the path.

Helix

Helix (a coil) drives a profile round and along an axis at the same time: springs, screw threads, spiral staircases. You state the profile (usually a circle) and its size, its distance from the axis, the axis, and the coil's settings. Those settings are linked by one rule:

overall length = pitch × number of revolutions

  • Pitch is a distance: how far the coil advances along the axis in one complete turn, measured from the centre of one coil to the centre of the next.
  • Number of revolutions is a count: how many complete turns.
  • Overall length (height) is the distance along the axis from start to end.

Because the three are linked, you give any two and the software works out the third. A coil of pitch 20 with 11 revolutions is 220 long; a coil 220 long with pitch 20 has 11 revolutions. The pitch must be bigger than the profile's diameter or neighbouring coils would touch and pass through each other.

The feature builder: watch each feature grow

Choose a feature and press Build: the profile (magenta) is driven out, round, between or along. Change the sizes with the sliders. In helix mode, choose which two quantities you specify; the third is calculated from length = pitch × revolutions.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2025 course report

On the 2025 paper's helix and extrude-along-a-path question, "Many candidates did not include all relevant dimensions for the path in the extrude along a path section, and the terminology for the helix of pitch and number of revolutions was not used correctly by many candidates."

So: dimension every line and every bend of a path. Never write "12 rotations of pitch" or "pitch 9 turns". Write pitch 12 (a distance) and 9 revolutions (a count), or one of those and the overall length.

Worked example 1 · helix · the bell-rope spring

The ship's bell rope is fitted with a coil spring made from Ø4 wire. The centre of the wire is 18 mm from the spring's axis. The spring has 9 complete coils and is 108 mm long. Describe how the spring could be modelled using 3D CAD. [3 marks]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. On a plane through the axis (for example the XZ plane), sketch a Ø4 circle with its centre 18 from a vertical axis. The profile, its size, and its position relative to the axis.
  2. Use the helix command, selecting the Ø4 profile and the axis. Name the command and say what it uses.
  3. Set 9 revolutions and an overall length of 108 (pitch 108 ÷ 9 = 12). Two of the three settings. Any two of pitch 12, 9 revolutions and length 108 earn the mark.

Worked example 2 · loft and shell · the powder hopper

A hopper for the powder room has a straight square collar at its base, 200 × 200 and 15 high. Above the collar, the body lofts from the 200 square to a Ø80 circle 150 above the top of the collar. The whole hopper is hollow with walls 3 thick and is open at the top and bottom. Describe how to model it. [5 marks]

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. On the XY plane sketch a 200 × 200 square and extrude it 15 (the collar). Profile with sizes, feature and distance.
  2. Create a work plane parallel to the top face of the collar, offset 150 from it. The second profile needs its own plane: state the offset.
  3. On that work plane sketch a Ø80 circle, centred over the square. The second profile, with its size and position.
  4. Loft between the 200 square top face of the collar and the Ø80 circle. Name the command and the two profiles it joins.
  5. Shell, wall thickness 3, removing the top face of the loft and the bottom face of the collar. Shell needs the thickness and the faces removed. Because the collar is hollow too, the result is one thin-walled hopper, open at both ends.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • name the five modelling features and say what each needs as well as a profile;
  • describe a sweep in the right order, with every path dimension;
  • use pitch, number of revolutions and overall length correctly, and calculate any one from the other two;
  • choose the right feature for a given component.

4 · Modelling edits and Boolean operations CAD · modelling edits

A modelling edit changes a solid that already exists. Edits are features too, so they appear in the modelling tree and can be changed or suppressed later.

  • Shell removes material from inside a solid, leaving walls of an even thickness. You state the thickness and the face or faces to remove (the openings). Bottles, casings, boxes and hoppers are shelled. A shell is usually done after the main shape and before holes are added through the walls.
  • Fillet rounds an edge. Regular: the same radius all along. Irregular (variable): the radius changes along the edge. Fillets remove sharp edges for safety and comfort, and inside fillets reduce stress where parts join.
  • Chamfer bevels an edge with a flat face. Regular: equal distances on both faces (45°). Irregular: two different distances, or a distance and an angle. Chamfers ease assembly (a bolt finds a chamfered hole) and remove sharp edges.
  • Mirror copies features or a whole body across a plane: model half a symmetrical part and mirror it.
  • Array (linear, box, radial) repeats a feature, as in 2D: a row of holes, a grid of studs, a ring of bolt holes round an axis.

The edit bench: fillet, chamfer and shell a block

Apply a regular or irregular fillet or chamfer to the four vertical edges, then shell it. The volume is calculated from the actual cross-sections.

Boolean operations: add, subtract, intersect

When two solids overlap, a Boolean operation decides what the result is. Add (join, union) merges them into one. Subtract (cut, difference) removes one from the other. Intersect keeps only the volume they share. Most of the time you meet them hidden inside a feature: "extrude subtract" is an extrusion followed by a subtract.

Block shop Booleans: add, subtract and intersect

Block A is fixed. Move tool body B through it and switch the operation. The volumes are counted block by block, so you can check that add = A + B − overlap.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Does it matter whether I shell before or after I cut the holes?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "Very much. Shell works on the solid you have. Cut the holes first and the shell puts a wall round every hole, turning each into a little tube on the inside. Shell first, then cut through the thin wall. The 2024 marking instructions made exactly this point: the shell had to come before the radial array of holes for the mark."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • describe shell, fillet, chamfer, mirror and array, stating the settings each needs;
  • tell regular from irregular fillets and chamfers;
  • describe add, subtract and intersect and predict their results.

5 · Terminology, the modelling tree and planning CAD · terminology · modelling concepts

The specification's terminology list is short, and every word on it has earned marks. Learn each with a plain definition.

Component
A single part, modelled on its own (one file). A bolt, a bracket, a wheel.
Assembly
Two or more components positioned relative to each other with constraints.
Sub-assembly
An assembly used as a single item inside a bigger assembly: a wheel-and-axle unit inside a gun carriage.
Work plane / plane
A flat reference surface to sketch on. The origin planes (XY, XZ, YZ) are always there; work planes can be offset from them, angled, or placed on a face.
Axis
A reference line: for revolving, for a radial array, or for lining up parts.
Feature
One modelling operation (an extrude, a fillet, a shell…), listed in the tree.
Sketch
The 2D geometry drawn on a plane.
Profile
The closed shape in a sketch that a feature uses.
Face
One surface of a solid, flat or curved.
Edge
The line where two faces meet.
Datum
A fixed reference (point, axis or plane) that positions and sizes are measured from. The origin and its planes are the first datums of every part.
Suppress
Temporarily switch a feature off, without deleting it, so the model is rebuilt without it. Unsuppress to bring it back.

Vertices, edges and faces

A solid's boundary is made of faces, meeting at edges, which meet at vertices (corners). You select these to apply features: a fillet needs edges, a shell needs faces, a sketch can go on a face, a constraint can use a vertex. For solids like these, the counts always obey V − E + F = 2.

Count the corners: vertices, edges and faces

Pick a solid and what to count, then press Count. Hidden edges are dashed so every one can be counted.

The modelling tree

The modelling tree (feature tree, history, hierarchy) lists every sketch and feature in the order it was made. It is a family tree: a sketch placed on the top face of Extrude1 is a child of Extrude1. Because the model is parametric, the tree is how you edit it:

  • Edit a size: select the sketch or feature in the tree, change the dimension, and the model rebuilds from that point down.
  • Edit a shape: select the sketch in the tree and redraw its profile (an ellipse becomes a hexagon); every feature built on it updates.
  • Suppress a feature to see the part without it, or to make a simplified version. Its children are suppressed too, because what they were built on has gone.
  • Reorder features, or roll back the tree to insert a feature earlier.

The modelling tree: edit, suppress, regenerate

Select a sketch or feature to edit its size; suppress features and see their children go with them. The model (2.5 mm blocks) and its dimensioned elevation regenerate from the tree each time.

⚠ Exam lookout · editing a model

When a question asks how to change an existing model, the answer goes through the modelling tree: "In the modelling tree, select the sketch on work plane 2, edit the profile, change the ellipse to a hexagon 24 across flats (the distance between opposite flat sides)." In the 2024 marking instructions, a candidate who described modelling the part again from scratch earned no marks for that question. Say "modelling tree", say which sketch or feature, say what you change it to.

Why does the model update? Parametric and associative

Two linked ideas, often asked as an "explain" question, so give cause and effect:

  • The model is parametric: its features are stored as dimensions and relationships, not as fixed geometry. So when a dimension changes, the software recalculates every feature that depends on it.
  • Drawings and assemblies are associative: they are linked to the model files, not copies of them. So when the model changes, every drawing view, dimension and assembly that uses it updates automatically, and there is no risk of a drawing showing the old size.

Top-down and bottom-up modelling

  • Bottom-up modelling: each component is modelled in its own file, then all are brought into an assembly and positioned with 3D constraints. Good for standard parts, library parts and work shared out between several people.
  • Top-down modelling: components are created inside the assembly, in position, using geometry from parts already there (projecting edges, sizes and hole positions). Because each part is built in its correct place it usually needs no assembly constraints, mating parts are sure to fit, and a change to one part can update the parts that reference it.

The modelling plan

A modelling plan is the sequence of sketches, features and edits you intend to use, worked out before you start: which plane first, which feature makes the main body, what is mirrored or arrayed, when to shell. A good plan gives a short, robust tree that is easy to edit. In the exam, a "describe how to model" answer is a modelling plan (chapter 9).

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • define every term in the specification's list;
  • count vertices, edges and faces;
  • describe how to edit a model through its modelling tree, and what suppress does;
  • explain why parametric models and associative drawings update;
  • compare top-down and bottom-up modelling, and describe a modelling plan.

6 · Assemblies and 3D constraints CAD · assembly

An assembly brings components together. Each free component has six degrees of freedom (DOF): it can slide along x, y and z and rotate about each. The first component is usually grounded (fixed in place). 3D constraints then take the others' freedoms away until they sit where they belong.

ConstraintWhat it doesExample
MatePlaces two faces against each other, facing in opposite directionsUnderside of a lid on the top of a box
AlignMakes two faces (or edges) lie in the same plane, facing the same way (flush)The ends of two planks level
Centre axisLines up the axes of two cylindrical featuresA pin in a hole; an axle in a wheel
OrientateFixes the angle between faces or edges of two partsA tiller arm set at 30° to the hull
OffsetLike mate or align, with a set distance between the facesA clearance gap of 2 mm
TangentKeeps a curved face touching another face without cutting into itA cannonball resting on the deck; a cam on a follower

The assembler: constraints remove degrees of freedom

The base is grounded. Apply constraints to the arm and the pin, in any order, and watch their remaining freedoms (they keep wobbling along whatever is still free). Try an offset below zero to see an interference.

Two checks belong with assemblies. Interference is where two parts occupy the same space: impossible in reality, so CAD can check for it and highlight the overlap. Clearance is a deliberate gap so that parts move freely or can be fitted. An offset constraint can set it.

Stock and library components

Stock components are standard, bought-in parts: nuts, bolts, screws, washers, bearings, hinges, motors. CAD packages and manufacturers supply them in libraries, ready to drop into an assembly. They save modelling time, they are accurate to the published standard sizes, they keep every designer using the same parts, and the manufacturer's part number can go straight into the parts list.

Worked example 3 · assembly constraints · the swivel-gun mount

A swivel gun's yoke (with a vertical hole) sits on a fixed post (with a matching hole), and a pin passes through both. The yoke must rest on the top of the post and point 25° from the ship's centre line. Describe the 3D constraints needed. [4 marks]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Centre axis the hole in the yoke with the hole in the post. Lines up the two holes: removes four freedoms.
  2. Mate the underside of the yoke with the top face of the post. The yoke now sits on the post.
  3. Orientate the yoke at 25° to the centre-line face (or work plane) of the post. Fixes its last freedom: rotation.
  4. Centre axis the pin with the holes, and mate the underside of the pin's head with the top face of the yoke. Positions the pin.
⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2025 course report

The report singled out the assembly constraints question: "Question 3(g), assembly constraints, showed the biggest improvement in use of terminology." Keep it that way: name the constraint (mate, align, centre axis, orientate, offset, tangent), then name the two faces, axes or edges it joins, and any distance or angle.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • describe each 3D constraint and pick the right ones to position a part;
  • (useful background, not itself in the specification list) explain degrees of freedom, grounding, interference and clearance;
  • describe the use and benefits of stock and library components.

7 · File types and CAD libraries CAD · file types · libraries

Every CAD package saves in its own native format, which keeps everything: the sketches, constraints, features and the modelling tree. But a native file usually opens only in the package that made it. To pass models between packages, between companies and to machines, you use exchange formats.

FormatWhat it isUsed for
DXFDrawing Exchange Format: 2D vector geometry (lines, arcs, circles, layers)Swapping 2D drawings and profiles between packages; laser cutters, vinyl cutters and CNC routers
3DS3D Studio format: models made of polygon meshes (surfaces built from many small flat facets), with materials, textures, lights and camerasVisualisation, rendering, animation and games, rather than precise engineering
STEPStandard for the Exchange of Product model data: an international neutral 3D formatSending accurate solid models to any CAD package; suppliers' parts
IGESInitial Graphics Exchange Specification: an older neutral format for 3D (and 2D) geometryAs STEP: exchange between different CAD systems

Why use a neutral file format such as STEP or IGES? Because it is a common format that can be opened in any CAD software, whichever package made it. A supplier can send you a model of their pump, you can send your casing to a manufacturer, and the 2025 assignment supplied STEP files: a ticket scanner to build into the turnstile assembly in Task 1, and the walls and floor, a waterbase sign (for the decal) and a bin to place in the Task 2(a) scene. The price is that a neutral file normally brings in only the finished shape: the modelling tree, sketches and constraints are lost, so you cannot edit it as easily as a native model.

The pennant outline needs to go to the laser cutter to be cut from acrylic.
A model of a winch must go to a supplier who uses a different CAD package from yours.
The figurehead model, with its materials, is going to an animation studio for a promotional film.
You will carry on editing the bracket next week in the same package and need its modelling tree.
A bearing manufacturer publishes its bearings for download to users of every CAD package.
A 2D deck plan is to be opened by a naval architect in a different 2D drafting program.

CAD libraries

A CAD library is a store of ready-made models: standard fixings (nuts, bolts, screws, washers, rivets), bearings, springs, pipe fittings, and often a company's own frequently used parts. Their function:

  • Save time: no need to model a part that already exists.
  • Accuracy and standards: library parts are modelled to the published standard sizes.
  • Consistency: everyone in a team uses the same part, so assemblies and parts lists agree.
  • Information: library parts carry names and part numbers that feed the parts list and ordering.
✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • say what DXF, 3DS, STEP and IGES are used for;
  • explain why neutral formats exist, and what is lost when you use one;
  • describe the use and function of CAD libraries and stock models.

8 · 3D rendering and scenes CAD · ILL · 3D rendering

Rendering turns a 3D model into a realistic image by calculating how light falls on it. The specification names five techniques:

  • Light source: where the light comes from, how strong and what colour. Its position decides where highlights, shade and shadows fall.
  • Materials: the surface's colour, texture, shininess, transparency and reflectivity (oak, brass, glass, painted steel).
  • Reflections: shiny surfaces mirror their surroundings; metals reflect strongly, matte surfaces not at all.
  • Shade: the side of an object turned away from the light is darker. A shadow is the dark area cast by an object onto another surface.
  • Sited environment: a setting (a room, a street, a sky or an environment image) that places the product in context and gives it something to reflect.

The render lab: light, material, shadow and quality

A small ray tracer renders the scene each time you change it. Move the light, change the material, lift the models off the deck, and compare draft and high quality. Switch on "Label the render" to name what you see.

Placing models in a scene

The 2025 assignment's Task 2(a) asked candidates to place their assembled turnstile, four times, into an entrance scene built from supplied STEP files (walls and floor, a sign and a bin), and to render it from a given viewpoint. Its marking instructions show what a good scene needs. In summary:

  • Everything included and to scale. Every model present, sized correctly relative to the others.
  • Sited, not floating, not interfering. Each model rests on a surface. Nothing hovers above the floor; nothing sinks into it or into another model.
  • Positioned as specified. Models placed where the brief shows them.
  • Suitable materials and colours on every model and on the walls and floor.
  • A decal applied correctly. A decal is an image (a logo, a poster, a label) applied to a surface of a model, like a sticker.
  • The right viewpoint and scale in the rendered view (the camera position you were given).
  • Lighting, highlights and shadows applied with skill: up to 2 marks.
  • Render output quality: clear, with no pixelation or grain: up to 2 marks. It must be a true render, not a screen grab.
✔ Why shadows matter in a scene

A shadow is what tells the eye that an object is resting on something. Turn shadows off in the render lab, or lift the models a few millimetres, and they appear to float. That is the quickest way to lose the "sited on a surface" mark.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"My render looks speckly and blocky. Is my computer too slow?"

Captain Grant: "Blocky is pixelation: the image resolution is too low for the page size, so raise it. Speckles are grain (noise): the renderer took too few samples per pixel, so soft shadows and reflections are rough. Raise the quality setting and be patient. A slow, clean render beats a fast, grainy one: the marking instructions reward 'no pixelation or grain'."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • describe light source, materials, reflections, shade and sited environment, in the context of a model;
  • tell shade from shadow, and explain what a sited environment adds;
  • list what a good rendered scene needs: scale, siting, materials, decals, viewpoint, lighting, quality.

9 · Writing "describe how to model it" answers CAD · describing modelling techniques

The biggest single CAD question is usually "Describe how the component could be modelled using 3D CAD software" for 5 to 7 marks. The marking instructions give one mark per correct modelling step. A step only earns its mark if a modeller could follow it: the command, where it is applied, and the sizes. Use this order every time:

  1. Plan: decide the main feature (extrude, revolve, loft, sweep, helix) and the order of the rest.
  2. Profile: name the plane or face it is drawn on, the shape, and its dimensions.
  3. Feature: name the command and give the distance, axis and angle, path, second profile, or helix settings.
  4. Further profiles and features: each on a named face or work plane (with any offset), each with sizes.
  5. Edits: shell (thickness and faces removed), fillets and chamfers (sizes), mirror (the plane).
  6. Patterns and Booleans: array type, number and spacing or angle; extrude subtract with a depth or "through all".

Loses the mark

  • "Draw the shape and extrude it."
  • "Make a hole in the top."
  • "Make the spring shape."
  • "Bend the pipe round the corner."

Gains the mark

  • "On the XY plane, sketch a 120 × 80 rectangle and extrude it 20."
  • "On the top face, sketch a Ø20 circle 30 from each edge and extrude subtract through all."
  • "Use the helix command on the Ø4 profile, 18 from the axis, with pitch 12 and 9 revolutions." (three separate marks in a typical scheme)
  • "Sketch the path: vertical 120, arc R40, horizontal 90", then "extrude along the path". Dimensioning the path and using the command are separate marks.

Worked example 4 · sweep, shell and radial array · the speaking tube

The captain's speaking tube is a Ø30 tube that runs 120 up, turns through a R40 bend and runs 90 across, ending in a flared mouth. The mouth is a loft from Ø50 to Ø80 over 40. The whole part is hollow, 2 thick, open at both ends. Six Ø4 holes, equally spaced on a PCD of 65 and parallel to the mouth's axis, pass through the flared wall of the mouth. Describe how to model it. [7 marks]

Show solution [7 marks]
  1. On the XZ plane, sketch the path: a vertical line 120, a tangent arc R40 and a horizontal line 90. The path with all its dimensions: the report's top warning.
  2. On a work plane perpendicular to the path at its lower end, sketch a Ø30 circle centred on the path. Profile, size and the perpendicular work plane.
  3. Extrude along the path (sweep) the Ø30 profile. The command.
  4. On the end face, sketch a Ø50 circle; on a work plane offset 40 from it, sketch a Ø80 circle; loft between them. Two profiles, the offset, the command.
  5. Shell, wall thickness 2, removing both end faces. Thickness and faces removed. Shell comes before the holes.
  6. On the work plane at the end of the mouth, sketch one Ø4 circle on the 65 PCD and radial array it 6 times round 360° (60° apart) about the mouth's axis. Array type, number, angle and axis.
  7. Extrude subtract the six circles by 30, back through the flared wall. The Boolean that makes the holes. 30 carries each hole fully through the sloping wall; a set depth, not "through all", stops the cut reaching the bend of the tube behind.

Worked example 5 · revolve · the lifebuoy

A lifebuoy is a ring with a circular cross-section of Ø100; the ring's outside diameter is 700. Describe how to model it. [3 marks]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. On the XZ plane, draw a vertical axis (centre line). A revolve needs an axis.
  2. Sketch a Ø100 circle with its centre 300 from the axis. 700 ÷ 2 = 350 to the outside; 350 − 50 = 300 to the centre of the cross-section.
  3. Revolve the circle 360° about the axis. Command and angle.

The answer builder: order the modelling plan

The steps are shuffled, and two of them would earn nothing. Use the arrows to put the rest in a workable order and leave out the weak ones, then check. Each step must come after what it is built on.

⚠ Exam lookout · describe and explain

A "describe" answer needs one developed point per mark: for modelling, one complete step per mark. An "explain" answer needs cause and effect. The 2025 report notes that on explain questions "Most candidates continue to struggle with this command word and only give the effect, without the cause." For CAD that means: not just "the drawing updates", but "the drawing is associative, linked to the model file, so when the model's dimension changes the drawing updates automatically."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • write a complete modelling description for a component, one marked step per point;
  • put features in a workable order (profile before feature, shell before holes);
  • include every size: path lengths and radii, pitch and revolutions, offsets, thicknesses, array counts and angles.
