The Courts-Martial Room · exam technique

Exam drill: the Courts-Martial Room

On HMS Vellum a court-martial sits at the long table in the great cabin. The court does not guess what an officer meant. It reads what is written down, checks it against the Articles, and records only what is proved. The marker of your question paper works the same way. This deck is about getting what you know onto the page in a form the court can credit: how the 90 marks are laid out, what each command word asks for, the words the marking instructions use, how to attack each kind of question, the errors the 2025 course report found, and a timed mini-paper to sit and mark yourself.

⚓ Ship's log: Captain Isobel Grant

"I have sat on courts where a good officer lost his case because he told us the effect and never the cause, and where a nervous midshipman won hers because every sentence said what she did, where and why. Knowledge gets you into the room. Technique gets it written into the record. Flight Lieutenant Murray and Master Shipwright Fraser will drill you until both come naturally."

Everything here follows the Higher Graphic Communication course specification, the marking principles published with the 2024 question paper and the 2025 course report. Qualifications Scotland, which replaced SQA on 1 February 2026, runs the course now. Every practice question on this page is original: none is copied from a past paper.

1 · The question paper: the charge sheet QP · 90 marks

The course is graded A to D on one total out of 140. The question paper gives 90 of those marks; the assignment gives the other 50. You sit the paper in an exam hall, in 2 hours 30 minutes, in one section. Expect to answer every question: the paper is built to be attempted in full.

90 marks64% of the 140-mark course total
2 h 30 min150 minutes, one section
1 min 40 sper mark: 150 ÷ 90 = 1.67 minutes
Blue or black inkscripts are scanned; pencil may vanish

What the paper looks like

  • The questions are a mixture of limited responses (a word, a name, a short statement) and extended responses (a modelling description worth six or seven marks, for example). Many are scenario-based: a product, a building or a publication is shown, and every part of the question is about that context.
  • You can answer in words, sketches, annotations or a mix. The specification says you may include sketches to support an answer, but sketching is not a requirement, and you do not have to draw with instruments. The 2024 marking principles say the marks are for the information conveyed, not for the quality of the sketch. A rough sketch that shows the plane, the profile and the dimensions earns the same as a beautiful one.
  • No data booklet or formulae sheet is listed in the specification for this paper. Anything you calculate, such as a tolerance limit, a missing dimension or a true length, you work out from the drawing with simple arithmetic.
  • Appendix 2 of the course specification (the standards and conventions you must know) may not be taken into the exam or displayed in the room. The symbols and line types have to be in your head.
  • Use blue or black ink. The papers are scanned for marking, and the 2025 course report warns that "responses written in pencil or highlighter pen may not be visible to markers". Sketch in ink too.

Where the 90 marks come from

The specification splits the paper into five areas. Each has a range of marks, not a fixed number, so the split changes from year to year. The ranges add up to between 63 and 116, but only 90 marks are ever on the paper, so read them as a guide to weight.

Area of the question paperMarksRevise it in

The specification adds that "a proportion of marks" are for more challenging questions: ones that need you to interpret or combine more complex techniques, describe or justify a more detailed process, or solve a problem, for example in CAD. In practice that means the long "describe how to model" questions and the questions that make you read an unfamiliar drawing.

Pacing: the minute is the currency

Divide the time by the marks: 150 minutes ÷ 90 marks = 1.67 minutes a mark, which is 1 minute 40 seconds. Most candidates want a few minutes at the end to check, so a working pace is a little faster. If you also spend 5 minutes reading through at the start and keep 10 minutes back to check, you have 135 ÷ 90 = 1.5 minutes a mark: 1 minute 30 seconds.

Anatomy of a question

Every question gives you three clues before you write a word: the command word (what kind of answer), the marks (how many separate points) and the context (what the answer must be about). Read all three, every time.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Do I have to answer the questions in order? The long CAD one scares me."

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "No rule says you must, but the parts of a question usually lean on the stem and on each other, so answer each question's parts in order once you start it. Across questions, do what keeps you moving. If a long CAD description is stalling, write the plan you are sure of (plane, profile, first feature and its sizes), leave space, and come back. Positive marking means a half-finished description still banks every point it makes. A blank space banks nothing."

⚠ Exam lookout: positive marking

The 2024 marking principles begin with positive marking: you build up marks for what you show; marks are not taken off for errors or omissions. A wrong extra sentence does not cost you a mark you have already earned. So never leave a question blank, and never cross out a reasonable attempt unless you have written a better one.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • state the paper's length (2 h 30 min), its marks (90) and its share of the course (64% of 140);
  • name the five areas and roughly how heavily each is weighted;
  • convert any question's marks into minutes at about 1 minute 40 seconds a mark;
  • read the command word, the marks and the context before writing.

2 · Command words: what the court asks for QP · command words

The command word is the verb that starts the question. It tells you what kind of answer earns the marks. The 2024 marking instructions spell out the principles for three of them (describe, explain and compare), and the 2025 course report singles out one of those as the paper's biggest weakness. Learn what each wants and you stop leaking marks on questions you actually know.

State, name, identify

These want a term or a fact, and nothing more: "Name the view labelled X" → "Plan". "Identify the DTP feature labelled A" → "Drop cap". One point earns one mark. Use the exact term from the specification; a description in place of the name ("a big letter at the start") may not be accepted when the question asks you to name it. In DTP questions, identify is often paired with where: "identify where alignment has been used". Then you must point to the place on the layout ("the left edge of the heading lines up with the left edge of the body text in column 1").

Describe

The 2024 marking principles say a description must be "a statement or structure of characteristics and/or features, not just an outline or a list", and that the number of marks tells you the number of points needed. So a two-mark describe question wants two developed points, each a full statement about the thing in the question.

A list (weak)

"Describe how rhythm has been used in the layout. [2]"

"Repetition. Headings."

A description (strong)

"Each sub-heading repeats the same magenta, bold sans serif style, which sets up a regular beat down the page. The numbered steps 1 to 4 are repeated at equal spacing along the bottom, so the eye moves along them in sequence."

A "describe how to model" question in CAD is still a describe question: each step is a characteristic of the process, and each step needs its detail (plane, profile, sizes, feature, values) to count. Chapter 4 drills it.

Explain: the command word the report flagged

The marking principles say an explanation must "relate cause and effect and/or define relationships", in the context of the question. The cause is what was done, and where: the feature, the choice, the setting. The effect is what that does: for the reader, the user, the manufacturer or the designer. One linked cause and effect is one mark. A two-mark explain question wants two different linked pairs.

⚠ Exam lookout: from the 2025 course report

The report names three 2025 questions that began with "explain" (1(d), 1(e) and 4(g)) and says: "Most candidates continue to struggle with this command word and only give the effect, without the cause." It advises centres and candidates to "use the Understanding Standards materials to familiarise themselves with the expectations of an 'explain' response."

So treat every "explain" as a two-part sentence: cause, then a joining word, then effect.

Linking phrases that force you to write both halves: "…, which means that…", "…, so…", "…, allowing…", "because…", "this lets the reader/user/manufacturer…". If your sentence does not contain one, it probably has only one half.

One more trap. In the 2024 marking instructions for "explain emphasis", emphasis was not accepted as the effect. "The bold title creates emphasis" just repeats the question. Say what the emphasis does: draws the eye first, creates a hierarchy, makes that text stand out from the rest.

The explain ladder: build answers that score

Pick a question. For each line of your answer choose a cause and an effect, then press "Mark my answer". The court shows which lines earn a mark, and why the others do not.

Compare

The principles say a comparison must show "the similarities and/or differences between things, methods, or choices", in context. The usual way to lose marks is to describe only one side. Write each point as a pair: "A raster logo is made of pixels, so it blurs when enlarged for the banner, whereas a vector logo is made of paths, so it stays sharp at any size." The word whereas (or but, while, both) is your proof that you compared.

Justify, give a reason, suggest

You may meet other command words, such as give a reason, justify or suggest. Treat them as general advice in the same spirit: a reason is only a reason if it is tied to the situation in the question. "Because it is better" is not a reason. "Because the brochure is printed on a press, the colours were set up in CMYK so the printed colours match the proof" is.

Worked example 1 · Explain · the master page

The Plan Room's eight-page recruiting brochure was set up with a master page holding the two-column grid, the running headline and the folio. Explain why a master page was used. [2]

Show the marking points [2 marks: any two linked pairs]
  1. The grid and guides are placed once on the master page, so every page shares the same layout, which creates consistency (unity) across the brochure. Cause: the grid is on the master. Effect: the same layout on every page.
  2. The running headline and folio are on the master page, so they appear in the same place on every page without being placed each time, which saves the designer time. A second mark needs a second, different cause. Repeating the same cause in other words earns nothing (the 2024 instructions also allowed only one mark for repeated references to a theme).

Also acceptable, for example: the folio on the master numbers each page automatically, so the page numbers stay correct when pages are added or moved.

The verdict: cause, effect, or both?

Read each candidate's answer to an explain question and give your verdict. After each one the court shows a version that would earn the mark.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I write a really long effect, with loads of detail, surely that's enough?"

Master Shipwright Fraser: "A long effect is still only half the record. 'It draws the reader's eye and makes the poster stand out and look professional and eye-catching' tells the court what happened but not what you did to make it happen. Name the cause first: 'The heading is set in a heavy slab serif, three times the size of the body text…' Then the effect earns its mark."

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • say what state/name/identify, describe, explain and compare each want;
  • write an explain answer as cause + linking phrase + effect, in the question's context;
  • give as many different points as there are marks;
  • spot an answer that is effect only, and repair it.

3 · Using the right terminology: the Articles of the course SPEC · terminology

A court-martial runs on the exact wording of the Articles of War. The marking instructions run on the exact wording of the course specification. When a marker reads your answer, the expected responses in front of them use the specification's terms: shell, radial array, centre axis, pitch, gutter, bleed, hidden detail. If you write the same idea in vague everyday words, the marker has to decide whether you really meant it. Use the term and there is nothing to decide.

⚠ Exam lookout: from the 2025 course report

"There was a marked improvement in candidate's use of terminology, found in the course specification, which allowed many candidates to access marks." The biggest improvement was in the question on assembly constraints. For the question paper the report advises: "Candidates must use the correct terminology, set out in the Higher Graphic Communication course specification." It also found that "the terminology for the helix of pitch and number of revolutions was not used correctly by many candidates."

Say this, not that

Every row below is a real kind of slip: the left-hand answer shows you understand, but it does not use the words the marker is looking for. The right-hand answer leaves no doubt.

The helix row deserves a second look. A helix needs its profile and its position from the axis, then two of three numbers: pitch (the distance moved along the axis in one revolution), number of revolutions (turns) and overall height. They are tied together: height = pitch × revolutions. Give any two and the third follows, so a description that gives "pitch 20, 11 revolutions" has defined a 220 mm tall helix.

Terminology flashcards: spaced drill

Flip each card, then rate yourself honestly. Cards you rate "Again" come back within a couple of cards; "Easy" cards are moved up a box and come back less often. Your boxes are remembered on this device.

Flashcards test recall one term at a time. The matching game tests whether you can tell close neighbours apart (mate and align, gutter and margin, crop marks and registration marks) under a little time pressure.

The matching board: six terms, six definitions

Click a term, then the definition that fits it. A wrong pair flashes and counts as a miss. Clear the board, then deal again.

4 · Question-by-question technique QP · five areas

Each area of the paper has its own favourite kinds of question, and each kind has a shape of answer that collects marks reliably. This chapter goes through them in the order of the specification table: CAD, interpretation, standards, digital technology and DTP.

4.1 CAD: "describe how to model"

These are the biggest single questions on the paper. You are shown a component, usually with dimensions, and asked to describe how it could be modelled. The marking instructions award a mark for each correct, complete step. A complete step has five parts, shown in Figure 4.1.

Points the marking instructions have rewarded in recent years, which you should build into every description:

  • Extrude along a path (sweep): draw the path first, with all its dimensions (lengths, bend radii, angles), then the profile on a plane perpendicular to the path at its start, then sweep. The 2025 report says many candidates "did not include all relevant dimensions for the path".
  • Helix: profile size, its distance from the axis, then pitch and number of revolutions (or overall height).
  • Loft: two (or more) profiles on offset planes, with the offset distance, then loft between them.
  • Shell: wall thickness and which faces are removed. Shell before you add features that must not be hollowed; if a later array cuts through the shell wall, shell first.
  • Arrays: type (linear, box, radial), number of instances, spacing or total angle, and the axis for a radial array. If you do not use a radial array, you must give the angle between copies yourself.
  • Edits through the modelling tree: to change a feature, select its sketch in the modelling tree, edit the dimension or shape, and the model updates. Modelling the part again from scratch does not answer an "edit" question.
  • Assembly constraints: name each constraint (mate, align, centre axis, orientate, offset, tangent) and the two features it joins, with any value: "orientate the arm at 20° to the base".

The modelling plan: describe how to model the deadeye

Add steps from the pool to your plan in the order you would model them. Some steps in the pool are vague, and some are wrong for this part. Then press "Mark the plan". The court awards a mark for each precise, correct step whose earlier steps are already in place.

✎ Technique: write it as a numbered list

Number your modelling steps 1, 2, 3… one feature per line. A list makes it easy for you to check that each line has a plane, a profile with sizes and a value, and easy for the marker to find each point. Sketch the profile if a sketch is quicker than words: a dimensioned sketch of the path is often the clearest way to give "all the dimensions".

4.2 Interpretation of graphic items: trace the lines

Interpretation questions give you a drawing you have never seen (orthographic views, a pictorial, a section, an assembly) and ask you to read it: name the views, find a size, say what a line represents, match features between views. These questions reward spatial reasoning, and spatial reasoning is a skill you can practise. The method is always the same: pick a feature in one view, and follow it along the projection lines into the others.

In third-angle projection, the view from above (the plan) is drawn above the elevation, and the view from the right (the end elevation) is drawn on the right. Every feature lines up: anything in the elevation sits vertically below the same thing in the plan and horizontally level with the same thing in the end elevation. Depths go from the plan to the end elevation through a 45° mitre line.

Three facts that answer most "what does this line represent?" questions. A line in an orthographic view is always one of: an edge where two surfaces meet; a surface seen edge-on (a face parallel to your line of sight shows as a line); or the limit (silhouette) of a curved surface, such as the sides of a cylinder. A face that is flat and square to your line of sight appears at its true shape; a face tilted to it appears foreshortened. An edge tilted to the viewing plane appears shorter than its true length, which you can calculate with Pythagoras from its two projected components.

Trace it: one feature, four views

Choose a feature. It lights up in the pictorial and in all three orthographic views, and the note says how it appears in each. Turn on the projection lines to see how its corners line up between the views.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"The inclined face is a rectangle in two views and a line in the other. How can one face be both?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "It depends where you stand. Look along the slope from the front and you see it edge-on, so it is a line in the elevation. From above or from the side you look at its surface, so it is a rectangle, but a squashed one: neither the plan nor the end elevation shows its true shape, because it is tilted to both. Only a view square to the slope, an auxiliary view, would show it true."

4.3 Drawing standards and symbols: learn them cold

Standards questions ask you to identify a line type, symbol or convention on a drawing, or to apply one: add the right symbol, hatch a section, state tolerance limits, choose a dimensioning method. Nothing here can be reasoned out in the exam room. Appendix 2 of the specification lists what you must use, and it is not allowed in the exam, so it has to be memorised.

⚠ Exam lookout: from the 2025 course report

The two 2025 questions in which candidates "were asked to identify and apply standard symbols, were very poorly attempted", and "Drawing standards, conventions and protocols is an area that some candidates found challenging." The report asks teachers to "refer to past papers, their marking instructions and the appendix at the end of the course specification to support candidates in their understanding of the purposes of various aspects of drawing standards, conventions and protocols."

In this site's view, these are some of the easiest marks on the paper to bank, if you have drilled the symbols.

Sections have their own conventions, and hatching questions follow them closely. Hatching is drawn with thin lines, usually at 45°, equally spaced. Every area of one component is hatched the same way; neighbouring components are hatched differently (the other direction, or a different spacing) so each part reads as one piece. Some features are not sectioned even when the cutting plane passes through them lengthways: webs and ribs, shafts, bolts, nuts, pins and screw threads are drawn in outline, unhatched. In the 2024 marking instructions for a hatching question, consistent hatching within each component, different hatching between components, and leaving the threads and the web unhatched were all marking points; shafts, bolts, nuts and pins follow the same convention.

Symbol quick-recall: line types, dimensions, building symbols

A round is ten symbols. Name each one as fast as you can; the note after each answer gives the convention. Choose a category to drill your weakest set.

Note on the building symbols

The building symbols in this drill are drawn exactly as on the Standards & views deck, from Appendix 2 of the course specification (which takes them from the British Standard). Learn them from the appendix your teacher gives you: that is the version the paper expects.

4.4 Digital technology: a benefit in its context

Questions on digital technology in graphic communication carry only 4 to 12 marks, but the 2025 report says some candidates found them "very demanding". They cover file management, cloud computing and storage, digital rights management, input and output devices, file types, the advantages and limitations of CAD, and the impact of these technologies on industry and society (the paperless office, remote working, communication across international boundaries).

The weak answer names a general benefit ("it is quicker", "it is cheaper", "it saves paper"). The strong answer says what is quicker or cheaper, for whom, and why, in the situation in the question. That is cause and effect again.

TopicWeakStrong (benefit + context)
Cloud storage"You can access it anywhere.""The model is stored on the cloud, so the draughtsman at Leith and the yard at Portsmouth both open the latest version without posting a disc, which lets them collaborate."

Digital rights management"It stops stealing.""DRM controls who may open, copy or print the chart file, so only paying subscribers can use it and the Admiralty's intellectual property is protected."

✎ Technique: one benefit, one line, no repeats

For "describe two benefits" give two different benefits: "share with co-workers" and "collaborate" may be read as the same point. Pick two that are clearly distinct, such as remote access and automatic back-up of earlier versions.

4.5 DTP features, design elements and principles: name it, place it, say what it does

DTP questions show a layout and ask you to identify, describe or explain the features, elements and principles in it. The report says this area is one that "candidates continue to find challenging", that correctly identifying where elements and principles were used proved difficult for a few candidates, and that a question on grid structure was "particularly demanding for many candidates". The cure is a three-part habit:

  1. Name it with the specification's word: drop cap, pull quote, reverse, text wrap, gutter, folio; or the element or principle: contrast, emphasis, unity, alignment, proximity, white space, value, rhythm, dominance.
  2. Place it: say exactly where on the layout ("the heading on page 2", "the image in the top-right corner").
  3. Say what it does, if the command word is explain: the effect on the reader.
#Name itPlace itWhat it does (effect)

Describing a grid structure, the question type the report found demanding, means saying how the grid organises the page: how many columns, whether they are equal, where the gutters and margins are, which items span more than one column, and what lines up with what. "The page uses a grid" is a list item; "the page has a two-column grid with equal columns and a gutter between them; the image spans both columns, and the heading and the body text align to the left edge of column 1" is a description.

Value is another term to get exactly right. It means the lightness or darkness of a colour or tone. The 2024 marking instructions describe a colour with low levels of white as having low value and one with high levels of white as having high value.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • describe how to model a component, giving plane, profile with sizes, feature and value for every step, in an order that can be built;
  • trace a feature between plan, elevation and end elevation in third angle, and calculate a true length;
  • name every line type, dimensioning convention, tolerance method and building symbol in Appendix 2;
  • give a digital-technology benefit with its cause and its context;
  • identify a DTP feature or principle, say where it is, and explain its effect; describe a grid structure.

Errors the marking instructions reveal

Marking instructions also show you how marks are lost, through their "additional guidance" column. Some patterns from the 2024 instructions worth knowing:

  • Repeating one idea. In a question about why fonts suited a theme, multiple references to the theme could attract only one mark. Two marks need two different points.
  • One-word answers where a description is wanted. On 3D-rendering techniques the guidance said not to accept one-word answers: the description had to relate to the model.
  • Vague words standing in for the idea. On watermarking, "theft" or "stealing" without a description was not accepted.
  • Redoing instead of editing. An "edit the model" question gave no marks for modelling the part again from scratch: the edit had to be made through the modelling tree.
  • Order that cannot be built. A shell had to come before the radial array that cut through it to earn that step's mark.
  • Follow-on errors are sometimes allowed. Where a later part depends on an earlier wrong answer, a correct method can still earn marks. Always answer the next part, even if you doubt the last one.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I'm not sure which answer is right, can I write two and let the marker pick?"

Captain Grant: "Do not try that with a court. Positive marking means a wrong extra sentence does not cost you a mark you have already earned, but where you give two contradicting answers to a one-mark 'name' or 'identify' question, the marker cannot tell which one you mean. Choose one. Where the question wants two points, give two clearly separate points, not five half-points in the hope that two count."

  • list the errors the 2025 report found and the fix for each;
  • give two clearly different points for a two-mark question;
  • edit a model through its tree rather than remodelling it;
  • write every answer in blue or black ink.

The court: mark your mini-paper

About the boundaries in the results panel

In 2025 the minimum marks for each grade, out of the 140-mark course total, were A 98, B 84, C 70 and D 56: that is 70%, 60%, 50% and 40%. They are set each year after the exam and can move. 7 · The last week and the day itself PLAN

By the last week you will not learn the course again. What you can still change is how fast and how reliably you get what you know onto the page. Spend the week on recall and practice, not on rereading notes.

The day: a plan for 150 minutes

  • Bring several blue or black pens. A pencil is fine for planning on a spare page, but the answer itself, sketches included, goes in ink.
  • Read the whole question, stem and figure, before part (a). Later parts often tell you what the drawing shows.
  • Circle the command word and note the marks. Two marks: two points. Explain: cause + effect.
  • Sketch when it is quicker: a dimensioned path, a profile, a symbol. Label it; the marks are for the information, not the drawing.
  • Do not stall. If a part has taken twice its time, write what you have, mark it in the margin and move on. Positive marking banks every point already made.
  • Check in the last minutes: every part attempted; every explain has a cause; every CAD step has sizes; every symbol and term is the specification's word; nothing is in pencil.
