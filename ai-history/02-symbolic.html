# Symbolic AI
& the Winters

For thirty years the dominant bet was that intelligence is symbol manipulation — that if you could write enough rules and facts, reasoning would follow. It produced real triumphs and a famous chatbot, then collided with problems it couldn't scale past, and funding froze. Twice. Out of the wreckage came the algorithm that powers everything today: backpropagation.

1956–1969 · the golden years

¶ Good Old-Fashioned AI

The first paradigm had a name in hindsight: GOFAI, Good Old-Fashioned AI. Its creed, later stated sharply by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, was the physical symbol system hypothesis: a system that manipulates symbols has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. Thought is computation over symbols; build the right rules and you build a mind.

The early results were genuinely dazzling. Newell & Simon's Logic Theorist (1956) proved theorems from Principia Mathematica — one proof more elegant than the original. Their General Problem Solver chased goals by reducing differences between a current and desired state. Programs played checkers, solved algebra word problems, and stacked blocks in toy "micro-worlds." McCarthy invented Lisp (1958), the language of AI for decades. Optimism ran hot: Simon predicted in 1965 that machines would, within twenty years, do any work a person could.

1966

¶ ELIZA and the illusion of understanding

Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) parodied a psychotherapist by reflecting your words back as questions — pure pattern-matching, no comprehension whatsoever. To Weizenbaum's alarm, people poured their hearts out to it and insisted it understood them. The ELIZA effect — our eagerness to read a mind into a machine that merely echoes us — is worth remembering every time a modern chatbot sounds profound.

1969 · the first crack

¶ The XOR problem

Meanwhile the rival, connectionist camp had Rosenblatt's perceptron (Chapter 01). In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published Perceptrons, a precise mathematical demolition. A single-layer perceptron can only draw a straight line between classes. Some patterns have no such line — most famously exclusive-or (XOR):

Plot those four points and try to separate the 1s from the 0s with one line. You can't — the classes are diagonally opposite. The fix was known in principle: stack layers of neurons so they can carve curved regions. But nobody had a reliable way to train a multi-layer network. The book's pessimism, fairly or not, helped drain neural-network funding for over a decade.

Go back to the perceptron in Chapter 01 and try to place four points in the XOR arrangement. No setting of the weights will ever classify all four — the single neuron is fundamentally too simple. That wall is the whole reason deep networks exist.

1974 & 1987 · the winters

¶ When the funding froze

AI's promises had outrun its results. In 1973 the UK's Lighthill Report judged the field a disappointment and gutted British funding; US agencies pulled back too. This first AI winter (~1974–1980) was about combinatorial explosion — toy methods that worked on micro-worlds drowned in the complexity of the real one.

A thaw came with expert systems in the early 1980s. These encoded a specialist's rules — if these symptoms then that diagnosis. MYCIN advised on infections; Digital Equipment's XCON configured computer orders and reportedly saved tens of millions a year. An industry bloomed around Lisp machines and knowledge engineering.

Then the second winter (~1987–1993). Expert systems were brittle: expensive to build, impossible to scale, and helpless outside their narrow rules. They had no common sense and couldn't learn. The specialised Lisp-machine market collapsed almost overnight when cheap general workstations caught up. "AI" became a word you avoided in grant applications.

1986 · the quiet revolution

¶ Backpropagation

Even as the symbolic industry cooled, the answer to Minsky & Papert's challenge arrived. In 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams popularised backpropagation — a way to train multi-layer networks. (The core idea had been derived earlier, notably by Paul Werbos in 1974, and is, at heart, the chain rule of calculus.)

The trick: make every neuron's activation smooth rather than a hard threshold, so the whole network is a differentiable function. Define a loss measuring how wrong the output is. Then compute how the loss changes with respect to every weight — propagating the error backward through the layers — and nudge each weight a little downhill. Repeat millions of times.

That "nudge each weight downhill" is gradient descent, and it is the single most important procedure in modern AI. A trillion-parameter language model is trained by exactly this loop, just enormously scaled. The model below lets you watch it happen on a landscape you can actually see.

▸ Roll downhill — gradient descent

Imagine the network's error as a landscape: every possible setting of the weights is a point on the ground, and the height is the loss. Training means finding the lowest valley. Gradient descent does it the way water does — always step in the steepest downhill direction.

Here the landscape is 2-D so you can see it (a real model's is billion-dimensional). Pick a surface, drop the ball, and tune the learning rate — the step size. Too small and it crawls; too large and it overshoots and diverges. Add momentum to power through the ravine.

On the Ravine, crank the learning rate and watch it bounce off the walls and diverge; then add momentum and it sails through. These are the exact knobs practitioners still tune today.
Carry-forward

The symbolic era's lesson is double-edged: hand-written rules don't scale to the messiness of the real world, but its replacement — differentiable networks trained by gradient descent — was sitting ready by 1986. All that was missing was enough data and enough compute. Both were coming.
