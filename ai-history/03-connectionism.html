# Connectionism
& Deep Learning

With backpropagation in hand, a stubborn minority kept building neural networks while the rest of AI looked away. Their nets learned to read cheques, to remember sequences, and — once a giant dataset and gaming GPUs arrived — to see. In 2012 it all detonated.

5 milestones · 1 model LeNet · LSTM · Deep Blue · ImageNet · word2vec · AlexNet live
1989 · seeing

¶ Convolutional networks

At Bell Labs, Yann LeCun built LeNet, a convolutional neural network (CNN) that read handwritten digits. Its key idea, inspired by the visual cortex: instead of connecting every pixel to every neuron, slide small filters across the image that detect local patterns — edges, then corners, then strokes — and stack them so later layers see larger, more abstract shapes. The same filter is reused everywhere, so the network learns far fewer weights and generalises across position.

By the late 1990s LeNet was reading a meaningful fraction of the cheques in the United States. Deep learning's first real-world product was quietly already in your bank.

1997 · two landmarks

¶ Memory, and a defeated champion

Plain neural nets forget: feed them a sequence and early items vanish as the signal fades — the vanishing gradient problem. In 1997 Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber introduced the LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory), a recurrent cell with gates that decide what to keep, forget and output. LSTMs would go on to power a decade of speech recognition and machine translation.

The same year, in the symbolic tradition's last great spectacle, IBM's Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov. But Deep Blue didn't learn — it searched 200 million positions a second with hand-tuned evaluation. It was a monument to brute force, and a hint that games would be AI's proving ground (see Chapter 04).

2009 · the fuel

¶ ImageNet

Algorithms were ready; what they lacked was scale of data. Fei-Fei Li and collaborators built ImageNet: 14 million hand-labelled images across 20,000 categories, annotated via crowdsourcing. The annual ImageNet Challenge became the field's benchmark. For a few years, error rates crept down slowly with clever hand-engineered features. Then the floor dropped out.

enabling tech · data

ImageNet embodies the second great curve: the internet as a training set. None of what follows — not AlexNet, not GPT — happens without someone first assembling data at a scale earlier researchers could only dream of.

2012 · the big bang

¶ AlexNet

In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton entered a deep CNN — AlexNet — in the ImageNet Challenge. It cut the error rate from ~26% to ~15%, an enormous margin. Three things made it work, and all three define the modern recipe:

  • Depth — eight learned layers, far deeper than was thought trainable.
  • GPUs — trained on two consumer gaming cards. The matrix multiplies that bored gamers were exactly what neural nets needed. This is the compute curve arriving.
  • Tricks that scale — the ReLU activation (fast, no vanishing gradient), dropout for regularisation, and data augmentation.

The result was a thunderclap. Within a year, computer vision abandoned hand-crafted features almost entirely. Within three, the entire field had pivoted to deep learning. The winter was over — and the people who'd kept the faith (Hinton, LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio) would share the 2018 Turing Award.

2013 · meaning

¶ Words become vectors

The same year, Tomáš Mikolov's team at Google released word2vec. The idea, rooted in the linguist J. R. Firth's maxim — "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" — was to train a network to predict a word from its neighbours. As a side effect, each word ends up as a point in a high-dimensional space (say 300 dimensions) where distance means similarity and direction means relationship.

The famous demonstration: take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman, and the nearest word is queen. Meaning had become arithmetic. This idea — representing things as vectors in a learned space — is the connective tissue of everything that follows, from translation to the embeddings inside every transformer. Carry-forward

Two ideas dominate from here on: deep networks of simple, reused units learn their own features (no more hand-engineering), and everything becomes a vector in a learned space. The remaining question was sequences and language — and a 2017 paper was about to answer it.
