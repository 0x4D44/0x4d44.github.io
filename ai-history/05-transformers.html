# The Transformer Era

One 2017 paper reorganised the entire field around a single mechanism — attention — and the discovery that you could make models smarter, almost without limit, simply by making them bigger. Everything you call "AI" in 2026 is a descendant of this chapter.

4 milestones · 2 models attention · BERT · GPT-1/2/3 · scaling laws live
the problem with sequences

¶ Why recurrence had to go

Until 2017, language models read text the way you read a sentence: one word at a time, left to right, carrying a memory forward (the LSTMs of Chapter 03). This had two fatal flaws at scale. It was sequential — you couldn't process word 50 until you'd done word 49 — so it didn't exploit parallel GPUs. And it forgot: information from far back degraded with every step.

2017 · the paper

¶ Attention Is All You Need

Vaswani and colleagues at Google proposed the Transformer, which threw out recurrence entirely. Its core operation, self-attention, lets every token look at every other token at once and decide which are relevant. For each token the model forms a query, and every token offers a key and a value; the query is matched against all keys, and the token pulls in a weighted blend of the values:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √d ) · V

Because it's just big matrix multiplications, it parallelises beautifully on GPUs, and because every token can reach every other directly, distance no longer causes forgetting. Stack many such layers, each with multiple attention "heads" that specialise in different relationships, and you have the Transformer. The model below lets you see those heads at work.

Try this: load "the trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big", pick the coreference head, and click it. A real model has to decide whether "it" means the trophy or the suitcase — the famous Winograd ambiguity. These heads are illustrative, but real trained heads really do specialise like this.
2018–2019 · two branches

¶ BERT and GPT

The Transformer split into two families. BERT (Google, 2018) read text in both directions to build rich representations for understanding tasks — it quietly went into Google Search. GPT (OpenAI, 2018) took the decoder half and did one thing: predict the next token, left to right. Both followed the same recipe — pre-train on a vast pile of unlabelled text, then fine-tune — and it generalised astonishingly well.

GPT-2 (2019, 1.5B parameters) wrote paragraphs coherent enough that OpenAI initially withheld the full model as "too dangerous." It also revealed something uncanny: a model trained only to predict the next word had implicitly learned to translate, summarise and answer questions, just by being asked.

Interactive model · 2 of 2

▸ Sample the next token

A language model doesn't "write" — it repeatedly predicts a probability distribution over the next token, picks one, appends it, and predicts again. Below is a tiny model trained on a few sentences. Each step shows its predicted distribution. Two knobs shape the output:

  • Temperature flattens or sharpens the distribution. Low = safe and repetitive; high = surprising and eventually incoherent.
  • Top-k only samples from the k most likely tokens, clipping the long tail of nonsense.
sampler.v1 — trigram language model next-token prediction · temperature · top-k
What to notice: tick greedy (or set temperature near 0) and the model loops on its single most-likely path. Crank temperature past ~1.4 and it spirals into word-salad. Real deployments live in the sweet spot between — that "right" amount of randomness is a product decision.
2020 · the law

¶ Scaling laws & GPT-3

In 2020 OpenAI published scaling laws: model performance improves predictably — as a smooth power law — as you increase parameters, data and compute together. This turned research into something closer to engineering. You could forecast how good a model would be before training it, and the chart said: keep going.

So they did. GPT-3 (2020) had 175 billion parameters, ~100× GPT-2. It introduced in-context learning: show it a few examples in the prompt and it adapts on the fly, no retraining. Capabilities nobody explicitly built — arithmetic, code, translation — emerged from sheer scale. The "bitter lesson" from the hub, vindicated.

Carry-forward

The Transformer + scaling is the master key. The same architecture, made larger and pointed at different data, becomes a chatbot, an image generator, a coding assistant, or a protein predictor. The next two chapters are this key turning in different locks.
