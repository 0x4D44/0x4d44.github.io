# Games & AlphaGo

Games are AI's wind tunnel: clear rules, clear scores, infinite practice. In five years, deep reinforcement learning went from flailing at Atari to playing Go better than any human who has ever lived — and then taught itself, from nothing but the rules.

4 milestones · 1 model DQN · AlphaGo · Move 37 · AlphaZero · MuZero live
2013 · learning from pixels

¶ DQN plays Atari

Reinforcement learning (RL) frames intelligence as an agent taking actions in an environment to maximise reward — trial and error with a scoreboard. In 2013 a small London startup, DeepMind, combined RL with deep networks to create DQN (Deep Q-Network). Fed only the raw screen pixels and the score of Atari games, a single architecture learned to play dozens of them — Breakout, Pong, Space Invaders — at or beyond human level, discovering tactics like tunnelling through the side wall in Breakout entirely on its own.

No one programmed game-specific strategy. The agent learned what to do purely from reward. Google acquired DeepMind in 2014.

2016 · the everest of games

¶ AlphaGo

Go had stood as AI's grand challenge for decades. Its board has more legal positions than there are atoms in the observable universe; brute-force search of the kind that cracked chess is hopeless. Experts thought superhuman Go was a decade or more away.

AlphaGo combined three ingredients:

  • A policy network that proposes promising moves (so you don't search everything).
  • A value network that estimates who's winning from a position (so you don't have to play every game to the end).
  • Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) that uses both networks to read ahead efficiently — the model you'll run below.

In March 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, one of the greatest players alive, 4–1, in front of 200 million viewers.

Move 37

In game two, AlphaGo played a move on the fifth line — Move 37 — that violated centuries of human wisdom. Commentators assumed a bug. It turned out to be brilliant, and won the game. Lee Sedol later responded with his own inhuman "Move 78." For the first time, a machine had shown humans something genuinely new about a game they had studied for millennia.

2017–2020 · from teacher to tabula rasa

¶ AlphaZero & MuZero

AlphaGo had bootstrapped from human games. Its successor AlphaGo Zero (2017) threw those away and learned purely by self-play, starting from random moves and the rules alone. In three days it surpassed the version that beat Lee Sedol. AlphaZero then generalised the same algorithm to master Go, chess and shogi — defeating the best specialised chess engines with a style humans found startlingly aggressive and intuitive.

MuZero (2019) removed the last crutch: it learned a model of each game's dynamics without even being told the rules. The trajectory — from imitating humans, to self-play, to learning the world itself — is one of the most important arcs in AI, and it foreshadows the reinforcement learning from feedback that would later turn raw language models into ChatGPT.

Interactive model

▸ Watch a search tree think

You can't visualise a Go tree, but tic-tac-toe runs the identical algorithm. MCTS repeats four steps thousands of times: select a promising path down the tree (balancing what's known to be good against what's unexplored), expand a new node, simulate a quick playout to the end, and back-propagate the result up the path. Good moves get visited more; the most-visited move is played.

Play a move as ✕ and watch the AI think as ◯. Or hit "Run 1 sim" repeatedly to watch single simulations crawl down the highlighted path and grow the tree. The bars show how the search concentrates its attention on the best moves.

mcts.v1 — monte-carlo tree search Silver et al. 2016 · AlphaGo's core
Policy & value, simplified: by default each simulation finishes with a random playout — slow and noisy, like early Monte-Carlo Go. Tick rollout: value heuristic to replace random play with a smarter evaluator. With a better "value net" the search finds strong moves with far fewer simulations — exactly the leap AlphaGo's neural networks delivered over plain MCTS.
Carry-forward

Games proved that search + learned evaluation + self-play can exceed all human knowledge in a closed domain. The open question — could the same spirit work on the messy, open domain of language? — is answered by the architecture in the next chapter.
