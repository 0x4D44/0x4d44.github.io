# Origins

Before a single neural network ran, the entire enterprise had to be imagined: a machine that could compute anything, a mathematics of thought, a model of the neuron, and finally a name. The founding decade of AI was almost pure theory — and almost all of it still holds.

5 milestones · 1 model Turing · McCulloch–Pitts · Wiener · Shannon · McCarthy · Rosenblatt live
The dream

¶ A calculus of thought

The ambition is old. In the 17th century Leibniz dreamed of a characteristica universalis — a symbolic language so precise that disputes could be settled by saying "let us calculate." In 1854 George Boole turned logic into algebra. But these were notations for human reasoning. The leap that begins our story is the idea that reasoning itself could be mechanised — carried out by a machine following fixed rules, with no understanding required.

That leap was made, almost as a side effect, by a 24-year-old trying to settle a question in the foundations of mathematics.

1936

¶ Turing's universal machine

In On Computable Numbers (1936), Alan Turing imagined an absurdly simple device: a tape of cells, a head that reads and writes one symbol at a time, and a table of rules. He proved two staggering things. First, that some problems are undecidable — no procedure can solve them. Second, and more important for us, that a single universal machine could simulate any other machine, given a description of it on its tape.

This is the seed of everything. A computer is not a calculator for one task; it is a shape-shifter that becomes whatever program you feed it. Every model in this almanac — every perceptron, transformer and diffusion sampler — is ultimately a program running on a descendant of Turing's universal machine.

read the head

The Turing machine is deliberately the dumbest possible computer: read a symbol, write a symbol, move left or right, change state. Its power comes entirely from composition — and from the tape being long enough. "Intelligence" might be the same trick at a vastly larger scale.

1943

¶ The neuron as a logic gate

Where Turing abstracted computation, Warren McCulloch (a neurophysiologist) and Walter Pitts (a self-taught logician, still a teenager) abstracted the brain. Their 1943 paper, A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity, modelled a neuron as a binary threshold unit: it sums weighted inputs, and fires if the sum crosses a threshold.

With these units you can build AND, OR and NOT gates — and therefore, in principle, any logical function. For the first time, "thinking" and "computing" were the same kind of thing. The McCulloch–Pitts neuron is the direct ancestor of every artificial neuron you will train below.

1948

¶ Feedback & information

Two 1948 works supplied the missing vocabulary. Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics studied feedback — systems that sense their own output and correct course, from thermostats to gun-laying radar to, perhaps, goal-seeking minds. The same year, Claude Shannon's A Mathematical Theory of Communication defined the bit and showed how to measure information precisely, independent of meaning.

Feedback gives a machine the notion of an error to reduce; information theory gives it a currency to optimise. Learning, as we will see, is just feedback on an error signal — a thread that runs straight through to backpropagation and RLHF.

1950

¶ Can machines think?

Turing returned in 1950 with Computing Machinery and Intelligence. Rather than define "think," he proposed a test: the Imitation Game. If a human interrogator, exchanging typed messages, cannot reliably tell a machine from a person, then quibbling about whether it "really" thinks is, he argued, beside the point. He also answered, in advance, nine objections — including the one we still argue about, that a machine only does "what we tell it to."

We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done. — A. M. Turing, 1950
1956

¶ The field gets a name

In the summer of 1956, John McCarthy convened a workshop at Dartmouth College with Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon. The proposal contained the field's founding sentence — and its founding overconfidence:

Every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it. — Dartmouth proposal, 1955

They expected significant progress in a single summer with ten people. That estimate was off by roughly seventy years. But the term "Artificial Intelligence" stuck, the founders shaped the field for decades, and the agenda — learning, language, abstraction, self-improvement — is still the agenda.

Interactive model

▸ Train a perceptron

In 1957 Frank Rosenblatt turned the McCulloch–Pitts neuron into something that learns. The perceptron takes inputs, multiplies them by adjustable weights, sums, and fires through an activation function. Crucially, when it gets an example wrong, it nudges its weights toward the right answer. Do that enough times and the weights define a line that separates the two classes.

What to notice: with clean data the line always settles — that's the Perceptron Convergence Theorem. Turn up data noise so the classes overlap and the boundary will jitter forever, never satisfying every point. That single weakness is where Chapter 02 begins.
Carry-forward

Three ideas leave this chapter and never go away: universal computation (a machine can become any program), the artificial neuron (intelligence as weighted sums and thresholds), and learning as error-correction (feedback toward a goal). Hold onto them — every later chapter is a remix.
