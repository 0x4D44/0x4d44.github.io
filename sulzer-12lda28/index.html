Sulzer 12LDA28

Two six-cylinder engines in one welded frame, geared together, breathing through exhaust turbochargers and pushing about 2,600 horsepower into a Brush generator. It hauled the Peaks and the Brush Type 4s for forty years. This page lets you start it, slow it down, pull it apart, and hear it.

How it works

A four-stroke diesel does four jobs over two turns of the crankshaft. Here is one cylinder doing them.

Slow the engine down (Speed menu above) to follow each stroke.
1

Intake

The inlet valve opens and the piston falls. The air is not drawn in but pushed: the turbocharger, driven by the exhaust, compresses it to roughly 2 bar absolute (16 to 18 lb/sq in gauge at full power on the Peaks; the Class 47 manual asks for at least 12), and the intercooler cools it so more oxygen fits in the cylinder.

2

Compression

Both valves shut and the piston rises, squeezing the air to a small fraction of its volume. Compression heats it far above the fuel's ignition temperature. The ratio is not published in any source we found; about 12 to 13 to 1 is typical of the class.

3

Power

Just before top dead centre the pump forces fuel through the injector nozzle straight into the hot air. It ignites on its own. Pressure spikes to roughly 100 to 120 bar (an estimate; no source found) and pushes the piston down, 360 mm, delivering the work.

4

Exhaust

The exhaust valve opens before the bottom of the stroke, so the pressure blows down into the manifold and spins the turbocharger turbine. The rising piston sweeps out the remainder. Inlet and exhaust overlap around top dead centre, letting boost air flush the cylinder.

Why two engines in one?

The 12LDA28 is twin-bank: two independent six-cylinder inline engines side by side, each in its own crankcase and block and each with its own seven-bearing crankshaft. Synchronising gears at the generator end join them and step the speed up by about 1 to 1.44, so the generator turns near 1,080 rpm while the engine turns 750 (a 1,150 rpm rating matches the 800 rpm as-built Class 47). On average one cylinder fires every 60° of crank rotation (even spacing is assumed), which is 75 power strokes per second at full speed.

The all-speed governor

Class 47 drivers had no numbered notches. The power controller has only OFF, ON, ¼, ½, ¾ and FULL, with ON and ¼ notched. Moving it changes a signal to the Vickers-built, Sulzer-designed governor, whose flyweights are driven from the camshaft. The governor moves the fuel racks and, through the load regulator, the generator field, so the engine holds its speed at any handle position while the load follows. Idle is 325 rpm (the manual also quotes 365 and does not say when each applies); full power is 750.

Timing

Twelve cylinders, 720° per cycle, one power stroke every 60°. The firing order shown is an assumption; the valve timing is an engineering estimate. Both are labelled as such.

Firing sequence

Rows are cylinders sorted by when they fire. Orange bars are power strokes; they stagger evenly across the cycle. Assumed order per bank: 1-5-3-6-2-4, bank B following bank A by 60°. No source we reached states the real order.

Valve and injection timing

The wheel shows one 720° cycle. Inlet opens 60° before TDC and closes 40° after BDC; exhaust opens 50° before BDC and closes 50° after TDC; injection starts about 22° before TDC. Estimate: typical 1960s medium-speed practice, not Sulzer data.

What you are hearing

Anatomy of the note

  • Firing frequency. Twelve cylinders fire every two revolutions: 6 × rpm/60. At 750 rpm that is 75 Hz; at 325 rpm idle, 32.5 Hz, the lumpy tickover.
  • Half order. The two banks are not perfectly matched, so energy appears at the bank firing rate, half the firing frequency.
  • Combustion knock. A short burst per firing, strongest at light load where the delayed ignition is abrupt.
  • Exhaust blow-down. A low puff when each exhaust valve opens, shaped by the manifold.
  • Gear mesh. A gear-train whine at tooth-mesh frequency. The tooth count is not published: 36 teeth (450 Hz at 750 rpm) is an assumed value (estimate).
  • Turbocharger. A rising whine as boost builds; the exhaust-driven rotor sings between about 0.7 and 4 kHz in this model.

No recording of the original engine is used; no measured spectrum was available in the sources. The model is tuned by ear to the structure above.

What we could not find

  • Compression ratio, peak firing pressure and injection pressure.
  • Firing order and cylinder numbering; valve and injection timing.
  • Turbocharger model and pressure ratio (the maker, de Havilland, is reported).
  • Intermediate engine speeds for each controller position (only idle 325/365 and full 750 are documented).
  • Field-weakening points; specific fuel consumption; fan and train-heating loads.
  • Any recording or spectrum of the original engine.

The primary sources for these gaps probably sit in the Sulzer instruction book and the 1965 Vickers report. Where this site shows a figure for them it is an estimate, tagged as such.
