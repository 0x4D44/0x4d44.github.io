Chapter 7 · what if, and what came after

The Reckoning

Could the ship have been saved? Could the people? The disaster invites counterfactuals the way the wreck invited salvors — so here they are, tested honestly, followed by the trials, the biggest salvage in maritime history, and what the rulebook learned from thirty-two deaths.

7.1First, the hard truth

Separate two questions that the night usually blurs. Could the ship have been saved? After 21:45:07 — no. A two-compartment ship with five compartments open to the sea has no naval-architecture escape: no pump, no door, no manoeuvre changes the arithmetic of Chapter 2. Every path to saving the ship runs through the seconds before the impact. Could the people have been saved — all of them? Almost certainly yes. The ship took an hour to develop a list that boats couldn't handle, grounded 400 m from a harbour, and stayed half-dry for years. A prompt alarm, an honest report and an early, orderly abandonment put 4,229 people ashore with wet shoes. The 32 deaths were bought minute by minute on the bridge, not at the waterline.

7.2The one everyone asks: why not ram the rocks bow-on?

It sounds mad, and no mariner would choose it in the moment — but the naval architecture is unambiguous, and it is the same lesson taught by the Titanic for a century: big ships are armoured against ends-on collisions and naked against raking ones. The bow is a designed crumple zone backed by the collision bulkhead, the strongest transverse wall in the ship, placed by rule close behind the stem precisely so that a head-on strike crushes storerooms and void spaces and floods, at worst, a compartment or two — well inside the two-compartment envelope. A 15-knot bow-on strike at Le Scole would have hurled people off their feet, wrecked thirty metres of bow, injured dozens — and left the Costa Concordia floating, lit, upright, and probably capable of backing off under her own power to wait for tugs. The fatal choice was not hitting the rock; it was hitting it sideways, which is what a too-late turn does: it swaps a survivable head wound for an unsurvivable opened flank. (To be precise: Schettino was right to try to turn once escape was possible — the criminal error was needing the turn at all.)

7.3The trials

Italy resolved the criminal case in two unequal parts. Five members of the ship's and company's staff were allowed plea bargains in July 2013 — suspended sentences, no prison — which left Francesco Schettino to stand trial alone in Grosseto, in a courtroom that was sometimes a converted theatre. He was convicted in February 2015; the sentence survived appeal in 2016 and became final at the Court of Cassation in May 2017, whereupon he reported to Rebibbia prison in Rome. (He was granted early release in 2025, having served just under eight years.) Costa Crociere the company was never criminally tried: it settled its corporate (administrative) liability for a €1 million fine in April 2013 — a number many survivors found harder to accept than any verdict.

7.4Righting a fallen city: the salvage

What do you do with 114,000 tonnes of capsized cruise ship lying on two rock spurs at the edge of a 70-metre drop, inside a marine national park? The answer took thirty months, some 500 workers from 26 nations, and on the order of €1.5 billion — several times the ship's build cost, and the largest maritime salvage ever attempted. The centrepiece, in September 2013, was the parbuckling: rotating the entire wreck upright in one continuous 19-hour pull, using 36 strand jacks hauling on cables anchored to the seabed and to steel sponsons welded to her port side, easing her down onto six purpose-built undersea platforms. Ten months later, thirty refloated sponsons — fifteen a side — lifted her off the platforms like water wings, and she left Giglio under tow on 23 July 2014, four ships and a convoy of escorts around her, for Genoa and the breakers. The last steel was recycled in July 2017. Nothing remains at Punta Gabbianara but the memorial plaque ashore.

7.5What the rules learned

  • Muster before sailing. The 24-hour drill window died within weeks (industry policy 2012, SOLAS law 2015): every passenger now musters before or at departure. The 696 who had no drill are the reason.
  • Passage plans with teeth. Company and flag oversight of routes, berth-to-berth planning, and explicit authorisation for any deviation — the “salute” as a private captain's prerogative is dead on paper everywhere.
  • Harder ships. SOLAS 2020 damage-stability amendments raised the required subdivision index for new passenger ships — pushing designs toward surviving more, and more awkward, flooding; “Safe Return to Port” (2010) already demanded separated redundancy for new builds. The Concordia's anatomy — every megawatt in five adjacent compartments — is no longer buildable.
  • Count and track everyone. Reforms tightened passenger counting, lifejacket stowage at muster stations and evacuation analysis, after a night on which the shore never once had an accurate number for how many souls it was saving.
  • Bridge discipline. Costa (and the industry) moved to stricter bridge-access rules and BRM enforcement. Whether culture followed paper is the open question every safety regime lives with.
The epitaph

The Costa Concordia was lost to the oldest sequence in seafaring — showing off, too fast, too close — and her people were failed by the newest: an organisation that could not say a true thing quickly. Between the two stands everything this site has tried to show: that a modern ship is a system of margins, and that every margin, steel or human, was spent that night before it was needed. The sea, as ever, billed the difference.
