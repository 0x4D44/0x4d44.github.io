Chapter 1 · 21:00 – 21:45:07

The Salute

Nothing was wrong with the ship. Nothing was wrong with the weather. The Costa Concordia was wrecked by a manoeuvre that had no operational purpose at all: an inchino — a “bow”, a salute — a close sail-past of Isola del Giglio, improvised at night, at nearly sixteen knots, on an open-sea chart, by a captain conning the ship by eye.

1.1A bow to the island

The inchino is an old and genuinely charming Italian custom: a ship passes close to a port or an island, sounds her whistle, lights her decks, and salutes the people ashore — often the home town of someone aboard. Cruise lines liked them; they made for spectacular photographs and warm local relations. The Concordia herself had saluted Giglio before, in August 2011 — but that pass was planned in advance, run in daylight, in the context of a local festival, with the route studied and approved.

The salute of 13 January 2012 was nothing of the kind. It was arranged casually, hours before, as a favour: the head maître d’hôtel, Antonello Tievoli, was a Giglio native (he was called to the bridge to see his island), and Schettino also wanted to salute Capt. Mario Palombo, a retired Costa master who lived on the island. It was mid-January: dark since five o'clock, the island's lights the only reference, nobody waiting on the quay. The planned cruise track passed about 4–5 miles off Giglio. Schettino ordered it re-laid to pass at half a mile — and then, in execution, took the ship inside even that.

The phone call

At 21:37 — eight minutes before impact, with the ship inside the half-mile line and closing — Schettino was on the phone to Capt. Palombo, asking, in effect, how close can I come? Palombo, who was not even on the island that night, told him there was enough water up to 0.3–0.4 miles off. The call ended at 21:38:47. The evasive helm orders began five minutes later. A master conning a 290-metre ship by eye, at night, at 16 knots, half a mile from a rocky shore, was simultaneously holding a social phone call about how much closer he could go.

1.2The wrong chart

The chart on the Concordia's bridge that night was Italian Hydrographic Institute chart 6, scale 1:100,000 — an open-sea passage chart on which the whole of Giglio is a few centimetres tall. The appropriate charts for a half-mile coastal pass — chart 122 at 1:50,000, or better chart 119 at 1:20,000, on which the Le Scole rocks are individually drawn with soundings — were not in use. The ship's ECDIS (electronic chart display) was fitted but the bridge team lacked the required type-specific training to rely on it, so the legal position was paper-chart navigation… on a paper chart five times too coarse for the manoeuvre being flown.

This answers one of the most-asked questions about the disaster directly: yes, the rocks were charted. Le Scole is a named, charted, lit group of rocks — the largest carries a light — marked on the coastal charts and known to every local fisherman. Schettino's early claim that he had struck an “uncharted rock” was rejected by the investigation: the rock was uncharted only on the chart he had chosen to use, at a scale on which the entire final manoeuvre fits inside the width of the course line.

1.3Sixteen knots, by eye

From 21:39:14 Schettino had the con. His technique was the one that had worked in August: aim at the island, judge the moment by eye, and swing the bow north along the shore. But he made three quiet changes to the parameters. The pass was re-planned from 0.5 miles to closer still; the speed was higher — around 15.5–16 knots, partly to make up schedule for Savona; and it was night. Depth perception against a dark island at night is notoriously poor: a shoreline shows you where the water ends, not where the rocks begin.

He also navigated by courses, not by helm orders. Rather than taking the wheel's language — “starboard ten”, “hard to port” — he called compass courses (“three-two-five”, “three-three-five”) for the helmsman to steer, in English, which was neither his first language nor the helmsman's. Helmsman Jacob Rusli Bin, an Indonesian seaman, had already garbled two readbacks during the approach; at 21:43:44 the order “350” was read back as “340” and had to be shouted twice, and the third officer left her plotting position to stand beside him. The ship was closing the rocks at 8 metres per second while the bridge sorted out its own steering commands.

1.4Why the turn came too late

A ship this size does not turn where you turn the wheel. From the order “hard over”, the steering gear takes some 12–15 seconds to lay the rudder across; the hull then takes many more seconds to build a turn rate; and even established, the Concordia's turning circle at 16 knots was roughly 0.35–0.4 nautical miles across. In pilotage terms she carries an advance — the distance she keeps making toward the old course after the order — of several ship-lengths. When Schettino began evading at 21:44:11, Le Scole was about 0.3 nm from the bow: less than the ship's own turning distance. From that geometry the encounter was, in a real sense, already decided; the only question left was which part of the hull would take the rock, and how hard.

1.5The eight seconds — did the helmsman sink the ship?

The last manoeuvre was actually good seamanship — the standard emergency technique for a close rock: first swing the bow clear (hard to starboard), then kick the stern away by reversing the helm (hard to port) as the obstacle comes abeam. It is the stern-swing part that failed. At 21:44:43 the order “port 10” produced only 5° of wheel. At 21:44:45 “port 20” produced — starboard helm. Rusli Bin caught his own mistake and wound the wheel back, but roughly eight seconds went by before the rudder was moving the right way, and “hard to port” at 21:45:05 came two seconds before the rock. The stern never got its kick. The port quarter swung into Le Scole instead of away from it.

So: no error, no wreck? This became one of the sharpest technical disputes of the whole affair, because Schettino's defence rested on it:

  • The court's consultants and the MIT safety investigation concluded the error was not determinant: by 21:44:45 the ship was so close, so fast and so far inside her own turning distance that the contact would have happened anyway — the correct wheel might have changed which frames were torn, not whether.
  • An independent 2015 reconstruction (Gubian, Piccinelli, Neri & Giurlanda), rebuilt from the black box and automation data, argued the court's dismissal was too qualitative: their position analysis put the breach point tens of metres from where the judge's experts had it, close enough that eight seconds of correct stern-kick deserved a quantitative answer rather than a shrug.

The honest summary: the error was real, it cost the one manoeuvre that might have softened the blow, and it probably did not change the outcome — a ship that needs a perfect helm response inside her own turning circle, at night, to avoid rocks she has aimed herself at, has already been wrecked by her captain. The Italian courts agreed: Rusli Bin's error earned a plea-bargained sentence measured in months; the navigation that created it, sixteen years.

Language on the bridge

The working language of the ship was Italian; the helm orders that night were given in English; the helmsman's English was poor enough that readbacks failed three times in six minutes. The MIT report lists among its causes the master's orders “aimed at providing the compass course… instead of the rudder angle”, and a bridge team that did not intervene. None of this is exotic: it is precisely the failure mode that bridge resource management (Chapter 5) exists to catch.
