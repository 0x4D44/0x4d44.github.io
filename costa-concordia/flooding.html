Chapter 2 · frames 52–125, port side

The Wound

The rock did not punch a hole in the Costa Concordia. It unzipped her. Fifty-three metres of shell plating — a fifth of the ship's length — torn open below the waterline, across five watertight compartments, in about three seconds. This chapter is about what that wound was, what lay behind it, and why it was unsurvivable by design.

2.1Raking damage: the Titanic wound

When a ship hits something head-on, the damage is terrible but concentrated: the bow crumples, the collision bulkhead holds, one or two compartments flood. When a ship rakes an obstacle — slides along it at speed, hull against rock — the damage is shallow but enormously long. The Titanic was opened along ~90 metres of her starboard side by an iceberg; the Concordia was opened along 53 metres of her port side by granite. Naval architects have known since 1912 that raking is the wound that kills big ships, because watertight subdivision is transverse — a series of walls across the hull — and a long tear simply crosses as many walls as it likes.

The tear ran from frame 52 to frame 125, about 8 metres below the waterline, with a width varying up to 7.3 metres. It was not a clean slit: plating was peeled, folded and torn away entirely — two long strips of shell were later found on the seabed — and one piece of Le Scole, a boulder of tens of tonnes, broke off and stayed embedded in the hull, protruding from the port quarter like a bullet in a wound. The impact killed the ship's speed from 15.5 to about 8 knots in under a minute and heeled her momentarily toward the rock, then away.

2.2What lived behind that plating

Here is the tragedy inside the tragedy. Of all the places a 53-metre wound could land on a 290-metre ship, it landed on the five compartments that contained every single source of main electrical power and propulsion — and the pumps that might have fought the flooding:

A modern cruise ship is a diesel-electric machine: the six generators make one electrical sea, and everything — the 21 MW propulsion motors, the pumps, the lifts, the lights, the galleys — drinks from it. Splitting the generators between two engine rooms (compartments 6 and 7) protects against a fire in one room. It does nothing against a wound that opens both rooms to the Mediterranean within a minute of each other, along with the compartment housing the propulsion motors and transformers, and both compartments carrying the heavy pumps. Post-Concordia rules (“Safe Return to Port” for newer ships, and stricter damage stability in SOLAS 2020) push exactly the opposite way: separate the vital systems so that one wound cannot reach them all.

2.3The two-compartment standard — and why five is a death sentence

The Concordia was built to SOLAS 90 damage-stability rules as a two-compartment ship: she had to survive, upright enough to evacuate, the complete flooding of any two adjacent main compartments. That is the standard almost every large passenger ship of her generation met. It is a probabilistic bargain: collisions at a bulkhead can open two compartments at once, so two is the sensible minimum; wounds longer than that were judged rare enough to accept.

Flood a third adjacent compartment and the maths turns against you: the lost buoyancy pulls the hull down until the margin line — the line a safe ship must keep above water — goes under, and each extra centimetre of sinkage exposes more openings to the sea. Flood five, and the ship's fate stops being a stability calculation at all; the only question is how fast, and which way she falls. On the Concordia the five breached compartments were amidships and aft, so she settled by the stern, and the water crossing deck 0 spread the flooding beyond the wound itself. The list to starboard — the side away from the gash at first, then increasingly toward the shore she was pushed onto — came from water spreading asymmetrically across the vast open lower decks, and from the shape of the seabed she finally grounded on.

2.4Why the pumps never had a chance

“Why didn't they pump the water out?” is the most natural question in the world, and the answer is arithmetic. Water flows through a breach at roughly Q = C·A·√(2gh) — area times the speed the head of water drives it. Eight metres down, water enters at about 12.5 metres per second. Give the wound even a few square metres of effective opening and the inflow is measured in hundreds of tonnes per minute. The ship's entire bilge-pumping capacity — had every pump been powered, primed and pointed at the right compartments — was on the order of a few hundred tonnes per hour. The sea was ahead of the pumps by a factor of about a hundred before the first switch was thrown.

And the switches could not be thrown. The high-capacity seawater and bilge pumps lived in the flooded compartments and drew their power from the flooded main switchboard — the investigation notes drily that they “could only be supplied by the main switchboard”, which was under water within minutes. The emergency generator (Chapter 3) could never have run them, and was dead anyway. Damage control aboard never really began; there was nothing to do it with.

2.5The watertight doors

The Concordia had 16 watertight bulkheads; nine of them were pierced by a total of 25 watertight sliding doors on the lower decks, so crew could move fore and aft without climbing six flights. Doors like these are the classic soft spot of subdivision, and the rules (SOLAS II-1/13 and MSC.1/Circ.1380) want them shut at sea except for actual passage. On the night, engine-room survivors describe doors being opened and re-closed during the escape from the flooding machinery spaces — men waded out through them with water at their knees.

Did open doors sink her? The investigation's answer is no — and it matters to be precise here, because “the watertight doors failed” is the most common myth about this wreck. The subdivision did not fail at the doors; it was outflanked at the top. The bulkheads terminate at deck 0, the bulkhead deck. Once five compartments' worth of lost buoyancy had dragged the hull down far enough, deck 0 itself went under and water simply travelled over the tops of the bulkheads — through stairwells, alleyways and every non-watertight opening — into compartments the rock had never touched, starting with WTC 3. Doors open or shut, a five-compartment wound sinks a two-compartment ship. What open doors (and the un-fightable flooding) did change was speed — and on a night where every minute of list would be paid for in lifeboats that could no longer launch, speed was everything.

Design note

A ship that heels no more than ~20° while she floods gives her people boats. One that reaches 30° in an hour takes them away. The Concordia's final capsizing to 70° happened because she grounded on a sloping rocky shelf; had she stayed in deep water, free-surface flooding across those open lower decks would very likely have rolled her over entirely — faster, and with everyone still aboard. The wound decided she would be lost; the shore decided how many would die. Chapter 4 takes up that story.
