The Drift

From the moment the propulsion tripped, nobody was driving the Costa Concordia. For fifty-nine minutes a 114,000-tonne hulk coasted, stopped, spun half a circle and blew sideways onto the island that had wounded her. It was the single greatest piece of luck in the whole disaster — and it was luck.

4.1A dead ship is a leaf

Picture the ship a minute after impact. Both propellers are stopped. The rudders are frozen where the last of the emergency power left them — hard to starboard. She is still making close to fifteen knots, bleeding speed slowly, because 114,000 tonnes does not stop for anyone. On that dying momentum she coasts north-north-east, past Le Scole, past the mouth of Giglio Porto — close enough that people on the quay watched her go by, dark — for about a mile and a half.

By 22:11 the log reads 0.3 knots: dead in the water, roughly a kilometre north of the harbour, a few hundred metres offshore. And now the only forces left in play are the breeze and the water. A cruise ship is, aerodynamically, a block of flats lying on its side: the Concordia showed the wind a slab roughly 290 metres long and 40 metres high — the better part of 10,000 square metres of sail — while drawing only 8 metres of water. Even a 14-knot breeze leans on that slab with tens of tonnes of force. The north-easterly took her aback, weathercocked her bow away through half a circle (the jammed starboard rudder helping the swing), and set her down, beam-on, toward the shore at a fraction of a knot. At 22:44 her starboard bilge touched the rocky shelf at Punta Gabbianara, just north of the harbour. She had come back to Giglio to die within 500 metres of where she was wounded.

4.2“I steered her there” — did he?

Schettino would later claim the return to Giglio as his masterstroke — a deliberate act of seamanship that placed the wreck in shallow water and saved thousands. The investigation's reading of the data is colder. After 21:46 the ship had no propulsion and, for all but forty-one seconds, no powered steering; no bow thruster; nothing. A master cannot steer a ship that has no means of steering. The track the AIS recorded is the track of a derelict: a coast, a stall, a weathercock turn and a leeway drift, all pointing exactly downwind. The anchors — the one tool that was genuinely available, and which, dropped smartly at 22:11 with the ship stopped, might have held her off the rocks or at least controlled how she took them — were not let go until 22:47, three minutes after she was already aground. Divers found them lying on the seabed with their flukes pointed upward: dropped straight down under a stationary, grounded ship, they had never dug in at all.

The honest version of the “miracle”

It remains true — and the courts accepted — that the grounding at Punta Gabbianara is why the death toll was 32 and not thousands. The dishonest part is the authorship. The ship was placed on that shelf by the direction the wind happened to blow on the night of 13 January 2012, not by anyone on her bridge. Which invites the obvious, uncomfortable question: what if it had blown the other way?

4.3Spin the wind yourself

Giglio's east coast shelves quickly: a narrow rocky ledge a few metres deep, then 60–100 metres of water within a few hundred metres of shore. A ship in the Concordia's state — five compartments flooding, free surface spreading across her lower decks, list creeping up — had two possible futures. Grounded early on a shelf, she heels against rock and stops: a terrible night, a survivable one. Adrift in deep water, the flooding proceeds to its conclusion: the list accelerates past the point where boats can launch (Chapter 6), and she capsizes — floats on her side or rolls fully over — with most of 4,229 people still aboard, at night, in 14 °C water. Every serious reconstruction of that branch of the night ends in four figures.

4.4The shelf that held her

The second grounding was gentle — a fraction of a knot, a nudge. But it was decisive. The rocky shelf at Punta Gabbianara caught the starboard bilge and gave the flooding hull something to lean on. Over the next hours, as the water spread aft and across, the Concordia rolled slowly onto her starboard side against the slope — 20° by around 23:15, 30° within the hour after that, settling finally against the rock at about 65–70°, her port side in the air, half her superstructure under water, and her keel resting metres from a drop into deep water. Salvage engineers would later spend months terrified of exactly that geometry: the wreck sat on two rock spurs, and had she slipped off them during the two years that followed, she would have taken her pollutants — and, in the first days, possibly survivors and bodies — down 70 metres. Anchor chains and, later, a steel platform were rigged specifically to stop her sliding. The island had caught the ship; it was never entirely clear it would keep her.
