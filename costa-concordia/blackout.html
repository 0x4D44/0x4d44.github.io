The Blackout

A diesel-electric cruise ship is an electric city: no electricity, no propulsion, no steering pumps, no bilge pumps, no lifts, no light. The Costa Concordia went from a 75-megawatt power station to a drifting black hulk in fifty seconds — and her one designed backup lived for exactly forty-one seconds more.

3.1Fifty seconds

The wound of Chapter 2 crossed compartments 5, 6 and 7 — which is to say: the compartment holding both 21 MW propulsion motors and their transformers, and both engine rooms, three main generators in each. Sea water at eight metres' head does not seep; it blasts. Within seconds of impact the aft engine room was taking water over its plates; within tens of seconds it was reaching switchboards, cable runs and the running generators themselves. The VDR fixes the moment precisely: at 21:45:57, fifty seconds after the rock, the main electrical network collapsed entirely. Both propellers had already stopped — the propulsion drives tripped in the first seconds — and the ship, still carrying fifteen knots of way, went dark from funnel to keel.

It cannot be said often enough: no crew error caused the blackout, and no crew action could have prevented it. Six generators in two rooms is genuine redundancy against machinery failure and against fire. Against the sea it was no redundancy at all, because both rooms — and the pumps, and the motors, and the main switchboard — sat behind the same fifty metres of shell plating. The disaster found the one wound that reached everything.

3.2Forty-one seconds of backup

The emergency diesel generator did what SOLAS asks of it. It sat high in the ship, far above any waterline, with its own fuel and its own switchboard, and when the main network died it auto-started within six seconds and closed its breaker — breaker 901 — onto the emergency switchboard at 21:46:03. Emergency lighting flickered back; the steering-gear pumps came alive (the VDR sees them powered); for those forty-one seconds the ship had her designed minimum.

Then breaker 901 tripped. The investigation's electrical annex is unusually frank about why: the emergency switchboard was feeding circuits that ran down into the flooding compartments, and as the sea found them it turned them, one after another, into short circuits — several at once. Protection saw a fault it could not isolate and opened the main breaker (the submerged shore-connection changeover may have contributed). The chief engineer and the electrical officer fought to re-close it; the generator would run, the breaker would hold briefly, trip again. The emergency plant spent the rest of the night delivering moments of power rather than power — enough to tantalise, not enough to work with. The ship's damage-stability computer (NAPA) ran “not constantly” on its dedicated UPS; the engine control room reported no system — balancing pumps, bilge, ballast — can be put into operation.

Was the EDG badly maintained?

It had passed its monthly no-load test five days earlier, in Toulon, breaker closure and all. The court record shows a machine that started and made power on demand. What failed was the concept: an emergency network designed for a ship with a fire or a dead engine room, not for a ship whose lower electrical anatomy was dissolving into sea water. An emergency system that keeps its circuits out of the bilges — or sheds flooded sections instead of dying with them — was one of the quiet lessons of the night.

3.3What the emergency board could never have done

It is important to be honest about what a perfectly functioning emergency generator would have bought. The emergency switchboard feeds survival loads: emergency lighting, alarms and public address, radios, navigation instruments, steering-gear pumps, sprinkler and emergency fire pumps, and the lifts. It does not feed the main bilge and ballast pumps — those hang off the main switchboard, and anyway (Chapter 2) they were outmatched a hundredfold by the inflow and drowned where they stood. The ship sinks with or without breaker 901.

But the evacuation was another matter. With solid emergency power the night looks different: corridors and muster stations stay lit rather than dropping to battery glim; the PA is always available and audible; lifts run for wheelchair users (several of the dead were mobility-impaired); the bridge keeps its instruments, its stability computer and its communications without improvising by torchlight. Nobody can say how many of the 32 that would have saved. More than zero is a defensible guess.

3.4The single point of failure, made law

The Concordia's electrical anatomy was perfectly legal, and that was the problem. The post-2010 Safe Return to Port rules (SOLAS II-2/21-22, for ships built after July 2010 — the Concordia predated them) exist precisely to break this pattern: a casualty in any one compartment must leave a big passenger ship able to make her own way to port, which forces duplicated, separated propulsion and power. The MIT report's recommendations push the same way for the existing fleet: double-skin protection for compartments holding vital equipment, more sub-division of the electrical plant, and interconnections so one flooded zone cannot pull down the emergency network. In the specific matter of breaker 901, the modern answer is selectivity: shed the drowning circuits, keep the board.
