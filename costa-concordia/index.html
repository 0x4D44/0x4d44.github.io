The Wreck of the Costa Concordia

At 21:45:07 on a calm winter night, a 114,000-tonne cruise ship carrying 4,229 people sailed into a charted rock at fifteen and a half knots, three hundred metres from an Italian island. She lost her engines within seconds, her electricity within a minute, and — though almost nobody aboard understood it yet — she was already doomed. This is the full technical anatomy of the disaster: what broke, what flooded, what failed, who knew, and what could have been different.

The short version

The Costa Concordia left Civitavecchia on the evening of 13 January 2012 on a routine seven-day Mediterranean cruise. Her master, Captain Francesco Schettino, deviated from the planned route to perform an inchino — a "salute", a close, showy sail-past of Isola del Giglio. He conned the ship by eye, at night, at almost sixteen knots, using a small-scale open-sea chart, toward a shore he believed he knew. He turned too late. The port side of the hull ran over the outermost rock of the Le Scole group and opened like a tin: a 53-metre gash across five watertight compartments — the five that contained every main generator, both propulsion motors and the ship's main pumps.

The engine rooms flooded in minutes. The ship blacked out in fifty seconds. The emergency generator carried load for forty-one seconds and tripped, never to hold again. Dead in the water, the ship coasted north past the little harbour, stopped, and — by pure good fortune — was pushed back onto the island's shelf by a north-easterly breeze, instead of out into deep water where she would have capsized with catastrophic loss of life. For over an hour the bridge told passengers, and the coast guard, that it was an electrical fault. The general emergency alarm came 48 minutes after impact; the order to abandon ship, 69 minutes after impact — by which time the list was approaching the angle at which lifeboats stop working. 32 people died within sight of a harbour, in water shallow enough that the wreck never fully sank.

Every chapter of this site takes one layer of the disaster apart, with the timings taken from the ship's voyage data recorder and the official Italian Ministry of Infrastructure and Transport (MIT) safety investigation. Where a question is genuinely contested — would the ship have missed the rock if the helmsman had put the wheel the right way? — the evidence on both sides is laid out.

The ship

The Costa Concordia was no relic. Delivered by Fincantieri in 2006 at a cost of some €450 million, she was a thoroughly modern diesel-electric cruise ship — for a time the largest ever built in Italy — with six main diesel generators feeding two 21 MW electric propulsion motors, bow thrusters, stabilisers, an integrated navigation system, and a computerised damage-control suite. Nothing about the hardware was obsolete, and on the night almost all of it was working. The disaster was not a story of a worn-out ship. It was a story of a ship driven, in perfect weather, into a place no ship that size should ever have been — and then of design assumptions, procedures and people failing one after another, each failure amplifying the last.

CHAPTER 1

The Salute

Why a captain aimed a 290-metre ship at an island for show. The wrong chart, the course-by-course approach, the six final helm orders — and the famous 8-second helmsman error, replayed from the voyage data recorder.

 CHAPTER 2

The Wound

A 53-metre tear across five watertight compartments. Explore the hull, flood it in real time, and see exactly why a two-compartment ship cannot survive five — and why every pump aboard was beaten before it started.

 CHAPTER 3

The Blackout

Fifty seconds from impact to darkness. The six drowned generators, the emergency generator that held for 41 seconds, breaker 901, and why the "unsinkable systems" all lived in the same five flooded rooms.

 CHAPTER 4

The Drift

A dead ship is a leaf on the water. How momentum, a jammed rudder and a north-east breeze conspired to bring the Concordia back to Giglio — and how a different wind would have killed thousands.

 CHAPTER 5

The Bridge

The engine room knew in minutes. The shore heard "blackout" for forty. Track who knew what, minute by minute, through the broken chain of command — up to the coast guard call the whole world heard.

 CHAPTER 6

The Abandonment

4,229 people, 26 lifeboats, a growing list. Tilt the ship yourself and watch the davits fail past 20°. Why 600 passengers had never done a drill, and what a passenger can — and cannot — do with a lifeboat.

 CHAPTER 7

The Reckoning

Could she have been saved? Six counterfactuals, tested. Then the trials, the €1 billion salvage — the largest ever attempted — and what the rules of the sea learned from 32 deaths.
