Chapter 5 · the human system

The Bridge

The engine room knew in ten minutes that the ship was finished. The people ashore heard the word “blackout” for forty. Between those two facts lies the part of the disaster that killed most of its victims: not the rock, but the hour of unreality that followed it.

5.1A crowded bridge with no one in command

At the moment of impact the bridge of the Costa Concordia held, among others: the captain, conning by eye while recently off the phone; the first officer, Ciro Ambrosio, nominally senior officer of the watch; the third officer, Silvia Coronica, pulled off her plotting duties to help the helmsman understand his orders; helmsman Jacob Rusli Bin, steering courses shouted in his third language; the hotel director; the ship's maître d', invited up to watch his home island go by; and Domnica Cemortan, a passenger, a guest of the captain, standing by the console. It was, in other words, a social occasion through which a 290-metre ship happened to be being driven at a shore.

Bridge resource management — the discipline, imported from aviation, that says a bridge is a team with defined roles, closed-loop orders and a duty to challenge — existed on paper at Costa. In practice, the investigation found: a con handover that “did not concretely occur”; officers who watched the ship close a half-mile radar ring without a word; position-plotting abandoned at the critical moment; course orders instead of helm orders; no one tracking the turn the master was flying by feel. Not one voice on that bridge said the sentence that would have saved 32 lives: “Captain, we are too close, and I am altering course.” The culture did not permit it. The MIT report lists the bridge team's passivity as a cause of the casualty in nearly the same breath as the master's recklessness.

5.2Who knew what, when

Follow the knowledge through the ship. Below decks, the picture was clear brutally fast: the chief engineer had reported flooding in two compartments within ten minutes, then three, with sea water over the switchboards — information that, held against the ship's own damage manual, spelled lost. On the bridge, that information arrived and somehow did not land: Schettino was still asking “how many compartments?” thirty-five minutes after the impact, still reasoning — aloud, on the phone to the company — that a two-compartment ship with “at least two compartments flooded” might hold. And ashore, the official picture for three-quarters of an hour was a ship with a generator problem. The coast guard did not learn of the emergency from the ship at all: it learned from the mother of a passenger who had phoned her daughter, who phoned the carabinieri in Prato.

5.3Why didn't the bridge understand its own ship?

It is tempting to reduce this to one man refusing reality, and his mental state matters — witnesses describe a captain intermittently frozen, changing plans, sending officers on contradictory errands. But the system around him had failed in ways that would have hobbled a better night:

  • No working damage picture. The NAPA stability computer — the tool that turns “water in compartments 5, 6, 7” into “you have N minutes, abandon now” — ran only intermittently on its UPS after the blackout. The one calculation that mattered was never reliably made aboard.
  • No fast channel for truth. The investigation notes the lack of any wireless intercom between key officers: reports from the flooding machinery spaces travelled by messenger, by shouting up stairwells, by whoever appeared on the bridge wing. Each hop cost minutes and precision.
  • A company loop that muddied rather than cleared. Schettino's first substantial report went not to the coast guard but to Costa's Fleet Crisis Coordinator — who misidentified which compartment was flooded in relaying it onward, while the designated shore emergency role was filled by the wrong person entirely. The safety system's formal chain (master → DPA → resources) ran, in practice, backwards and sideways.
  • The two-compartment anchor. Schettino held onto “the ship can survive two compartments” long after his own engineers had counted three, then five. Psychologists call it anchoring; mariners call it not doing the arithmetic. Either way, the number that mattered — five — was spoken on his bridge at 22:20 and bounced off.

5.4The cost of “it's just a blackout”

Why does the lie to the shore matter so much? Count what the shore controls: patrol boats, helicopters, tugs, ferries, hospital beds, and — crucially — the clock. Livorno's rescue centre began moving assets the moment it smelled the truth (a patrol boat was alongside by 22:39, before the ship declared distress). Every minute of “under control, only a blackout” was a minute of that machine standing down while 4,229 people's margin drained away with the list. The formal distress — by then redundant, declared by the coast guard — came at 22:34, 49 minutes after impact. The evacuation Chapter 6 describes was run, in the end, substantially by the shore: the ship's command had, by act and omission, resigned from it.

The captain's account

Schettino maintained that he did not abandon his ship: that as she rolled, he and other officers were on the high side and he “fell into” a lifeboat when it was lowered; that he then directed the evacuation from the rocks. The courts weighed this against the recorded calls — in which De Falco orders him back aboard eleven times and he does not go — and convicted him, among the other charges, of abandoning ship while passengers remained aboard. Roughly 300 people were still on the wreck when he left the last of them to it.
