A compact disc spends 1 411 200 bits every second describing music. An Opus stream can describe the same music, to most ears indistinguishably, in 96 000. This is the study of what happens to the other ninety-three per cent.

Audio compression is not a trick of packing. It is a theory of your hearing, encoded as arithmetic and run thirty-eight times a second. A codec's real job is to work out, for every fraction of a second and every band of frequency, exactly how much damage it can do before you would notice — and then to do precisely that much, and no less.

Everything on these pages is computed live in your browser. There is not one audio file in this repository: every sound you hear is synthesised, encoded and decoded as you click.

The problem

Raw audio is enormous, and almost all of it is wasted on you.

A compact disc carries two channels, sampled 44 100 times a second, each sample a 16-bit integer. Multiply it out — 44 100 × 16 × 2 — and you get 1 411 200 bits per second: 176.4 kilobytes every second, 10.6 megabytes a minute, about 740 megabytes for a full 74-minute disc. That number is why the CD needed to be the size it was, and why in 1993 a three-minute song was an overnight download.

The obvious response — general-purpose compression — barely helps. Run a PCM file through zip and you will claw back perhaps ten per cent, because the waveform of a cymbal is, statistically, very close to noise. There is no redundancy left to exploit at the level of bit patterns. If you want an order of magnitude, you have to stop asking "what in this file is repeated?" and start asking a different question entirely.

The winning question was never how do I store this signal? It was which parts of this signal is the listener physically incapable of hearing? The perceptual coding bargain

That question has an answer, and the answer is measurable. Psychoacoustics had spent most of the twentieth century measuring it: the quietest tone you can detect at each frequency, how a loud sound at one frequency raises the detection threshold at its neighbours, how long that shadow lasts after the sound stops. By the late 1980s those curves were good enough to drive an encoder. The result was a machine that deliberately adds noise to your music — a great deal of noise, tens of decibels of it — and hides every decibel underneath something louder.

The one idea

Put the error where the ear is already busy.

Every lossy audio codec ever shipped is a variation on four moves, and the chapters that follow take them one at a time.

Move 1 · split time from frequency

Cut the signal into short overlapping blocks and transform each one into frequency components — in practice a modified discrete cosine transform, which packs 2N samples into N numbers and still reconstructs exactly, because the aliasing it introduces cancels against the next block. Chapter 04.

Move 2 · ask how deaf you are, here, now

Run a model of the ear over that block and produce a masking threshold: a curve of the loudest noise, in each critical band, that this particular instant of music can hide. Chapter 02 and Chapter 03.

Move 3 · quantise down to that line

Round each frequency component coarsely enough that its rounding error sits just below the threshold — and no more coarsely, because every extra decibel of margin is bits you have paid for and cannot hear. Chapter 05.

Move 4 · code the survivors without waste

Most of what is left is zero, and the non-zeros are wildly unequal in probability. Huffman codes, arithmetic codes and range coders spend fractions of a bit on the common cases. Chapter 06.

Everything else — and there is a great deal else — is refinement of those four moves under pressure. Block switching exists because move 1 smears a transient's noise backwards in time past the point where move 2's model still holds. Temporal noise shaping exists because you can apply move 1's logic along the frequency axis instead. Spectral band replication exists because at 48 kbit/s you cannot afford to code the top octave at all, so you describe it instead. The bit reservoir exists because move 3's answer varies wildly from block to block while the transmission channel does not.

The individual codecs, then, are not competing theories. They are successive settlements of the same argument, each one made under different constraints of computing power, patent exposure, latency and taste. MP3 settled it in 1993 with 576 spectral lines and a hybrid filterbank inherited from its own Layer II ancestry. AAC settled it again in 1997 with a clean 1024-line transform and a box of new tools. Opus settled it once more in 2012 by refusing to choose between speech and music, and shipping both codecs in one bitstream.

How to read this

The figures are the argument. Use them.

Nothing here is an illustration of a result obtained elsewhere. When a page shows you a masking curve, it is evaluating the spreading function as you drag the masker. When it shows that the MDCT's aliasing cancels, it is transforming a real block, keeping the alias, transforming it back and printing the residual. When it plays you a pre-echo, it has just encoded a castanet at a bitrate you chose, with a block length you chose, and decoded it again.

Three practical notes:

Use headphones
Most of the audible demonstrations are about low-level detail twenty to sixty decibels below the music. Laptop speakers will hide the very artefacts the page is trying to show you, and room noise will mask them exactly as a codec would.
Nothing plays by itself
Every sound requires a click, and starting one stops the last. If a button says ■, it is playing; press it again to stop.
The numbers are live
Bitrates, signal-to-noise figures and bit counts shown in the readouts are measured from the encoder actually running, not typed in. They will differ a little between browsers, because your AudioContext may be running at 44 100 or 48 000 Hz.
A warning about the encoder in these pages

The codec used for the live demonstrations is real but small: an MDCT, Bark-band masking, a global rate loop and an entropy estimate, in about two hundred lines. It is enough to produce genuine pre-echo, genuine birdies and a genuine rate–quality curve, which is what the figures need. It is not LAME or libopus, and its output at a given bitrate is worse than either. Where a page quotes what a real codec sounds like at a real bitrate, that comes from published listening tests, and says so.

Colophon

Built from nothing but arithmetic.

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No framework, no build step, no bundler, no external font, no CDN, no analytics, and — the point of pride — no audio files. The music is a Karplus–Strong string with a synthesised bell and hi-hat; the speech is a glottal pulse train through four formant resonators; the castanets are two decaying sinusoids and a burst of seeded noise. The fast Fourier transform, the MDCT, the Levinson–Durbin recursion, the Huffman builder and the psychoacoustic model are all in assets/dsp.js and assets/audio.js and are perhaps forty kilobytes together.

Elsewhere in this almanac: a 1996 spectrum-analyser codebase takes the FFT apart in C; ropus is the Opus decoder ported to safe Rust and checked bit-exact against the reference; Instruments covers the physics on the other side of the microphone; and the MIDI Observatory is where the synthesis techniques used here get to show off properly.
