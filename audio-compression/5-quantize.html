Rounding is the only step in the encoder that loses anything. Everything else is bookkeeping.

The transform is invertible, the entropy coder is lossless, the stereo matrix is a rotation you can undo. The single valve through which 1.3 megabits a second escape is a division followed by a rounding — and every part of an encoder that anyone has ever argued about exists to choose the divisor, band by band and block by block, about forty times a second.

The only lossy step

Follow the information through an encoder and there is exactly one place it can leave.

The modified discrete cosine transform of Chapter 04 is invertible: the demonstration there reconstructs a signal to within about one part in 106, and the residual is arithmetic rounding, not design. The Huffman and range coders of Chapter 06 are lossless by construction — that is what prefix-free buys. Mid/side stereo is a rotation by 45°. Scalefactors, window-shape flags and table selects are transmitted as integers and arrive as the same integers. If you audit an MP3 or AAC encoder stage by stage, every block is reversible except one, and that one is a rounding.

q=round (XΔ) ,   X̂=q·Δ ,   ε2= Δ212 one spectral coefficient in, one integer out; the error is uniform on ±Δ/2 whenever the step is small against the coefficient

Everything else in this study is a preparation for that line or a consequence of it. The transform exists so that the coefficients X are sparse and unequal, which makes the integers small. The psychoacoustic model exists so that Δ can be chosen per band rather than globally. The entropy coder exists so that the integers cost their information content and not a fixed word length. The quantiser is the valve; the rest of the encoder is the hand on the valve.

Which turns the design problem into an arithmetic one. Chapter 03 produced, for each critical band k of each block, a masking threshold Tk: a noise power per spectral line that the block's own content will hide. Quantisation noise in that band has power Δ2/12. Setting one equal to the other gives the step size that spends exactly the precision the ear can use and not one bit more.

Δk= √12·Tk the entire perceptual coding bargain, in one expression: the divisor is set by the threshold, not by the signal

If that were the end of it there would be no chapter. It is not, because the resulting bit rate is whatever it happens to be — Fig 5.4 measures a factor of seven between the cheapest and dearest of four programmes at one fixed quality — and almost nobody gets to transmit whatever bit rate the music asks for. The rest of this page is what an encoder does when the answer Δk = √(12Tk) costs more bits than it has.

The exchange rate is fixed and was derived in Chapter 01: halving a quantiser step costs one bit per sample and buys 6.02 dB of signal-to-noise ratio. That number recurs on this page in three disguises — as the slope of a rate–distortion curve, as four steps of MP3's global gain, and as the price of one scalefactor step when scalefac_scale is set.

SNR=6.02N +1.76 dB for a full-scale sine into an ideal N-bit uniform quantiser; every bit you remove costs 6.02 dB, wherever you remove it from
The three-quarter law

MP3 and AAC do not round the coefficient. They round its three-quarter power.

A scalefactor band is not one number; it runs from four spectral lines at the bottom of the spectrum to several dozen at the top, and within a single band the lines can easily differ by 40 dB. A uniform quantiser serves the loudest line in the band well and the quietest line badly: the error is the same size everywhere, so the signal-to-noise ratio inside the band falls one decibel for every decibel a line sits below its neighbours. Both MP3 and AAC deal with this by companding — squashing the amplitude range before rounding and stretching it afterwards — and both use the same exponent.

is=nint( ( |xr|2(global_gain−210)/4 )3/4 −0.0946) MPEG-1 Layer III, the quantiser given in the standard's informative encoder annex; the band's own scalefactor is folded into the same exponent, as the requantiser below makes explicit
xr=sgn(is) ·|is|4/3 ·2(global_gain−210)/4 ·2−m(scalefack + preflag·pretabk) and the requantiser, which is normative — every decoder must compute exactly this. m = 0.5 when scalefac_scale is 0, otherwise 1.

AAC uses the identical exponent with a different constant and a different rounding instruction: int(|x|3/4·2−3sf/16 + 0.4054), the MAGIC_NUMBER that has been sitting in FAAC's quantize.c since the late 1990s. The two look like different design decisions and are not. For any non-negative y, rounding to nearest after subtracting 0.0946 and truncating after adding 0.4054 are the same map, because 0.0946 + 0.4054 = 0.5.

nint(y−0.0946) ≡ floor(y+0.4054) ,   y≥0 Fig 5.1 checks this on 4 096 values on every redraw and reports the number of disagreements

What the offset actually does is move the decision boundary. Without it, the companded value 0.5 is the point at which a coefficient stops being coded as zero; with it, the boundary sits at 0.5946. That is a dead zone: a widened interval around zero that throws away slightly more than fair rounding would. It is deliberate and it is nearly free, for two reasons. The reconstruction point |is|4/3 lies above the centroid of the interval it represents, so biasing the decision downwards partly corrects a bias that is already there; and a zero is the cheapest symbol the entropy coder has, so the rate saved by making more of them buys precision somewhere it matters more.

The folk explanation of the exponent — that 3/4 echoes Stevens's power law for loudness — is not something either standard says, and the exponents do not even match: Stevens's for loudness against sound pressure is nearer 0.6. The operational effect of the exponent is precise and testable, and it is not what most summaries claim. Write the quantiser as y = (|x|/s)p and the reconstruction as x̂ = s·y1/p. A rounding error of about 1/√12 in y maps to an error in x that grows as x1−p, so the relative error falls as x−p.

|ε||x| ∝x−p ⟹ SNRdB falls p dB per dB of level p = 1 is a uniform quantiser: 1 dB lost per dB. p = 3/4 loses 0.75. Only p → 0, a logarithmic law, holds the relative error constant.

So the three-quarter law does not give constant relative error, and any text that says it does is quoting the intent rather than the arithmetic. What it gives is a quarter of a decibel back for every decibel of level, and Fig 5.1 measures what that is worth rather than asserting it. Sixty decibels below full scale — about the span that separates the loudest and quietest lines in a busy band — the power law comes out 13.6 dB ahead of a uniform quantiser matched to it at the top: 28.2 dB of signal-to-noise ratio against 14.6. The measured slope is 0.77 dB per dB, against a prediction of 0.75.

Scalefactors are exponents

No encoder transmits a step size. It transmits the logarithm of one, in fixed decibel increments.

Look again at the requantiser above and notice that Δ never appears. What appears is a sum of exponents of two: a per-granule global_gain, a per-band scalefac, an optional fixed tilt pretab, and for short blocks a per-window subblock_gain. Every one of them moves the reconstruction by a fixed number of decibels, and those numbers are the grid on which all noise shaping in MP3 happens.

20log10 21/4= 1.5051 dB,   20log10 21/2= 3.0103 dB,   20log10 2=6.0206 dB the only three step sizes MP3's gain fields can take

One step of global_gain changes the exponent by a quarter, so it moves the step size — and therefore the noise floor of the whole granule — by 1.505 dB. One step of a scalefactor changes it by a half when scalefac_scale is 0, so a band's noise moves by 3.01 dB; setting scalefac_scale to 1 doubles that to 6.02 dB. This is a range-against-resolution trade, because scalefac_compress has already fixed how many bits each scalefactor gets: the coarse setting reaches twice as far down with the same field width. A subblock_gain step subtracts 8 from the exponent numerator, which is 12.04 dB in one go — deliberately blunt, because it exists to pull one of three short windows down out of a transient rather than to shape anything.

MP3 divides its 576-line granule into 21 scalefactor bands for a long block and 12 for each of the three short windows, with slen1 covering bands 0–10 and slen2 bands 11–20. AAC, having no compatibility to preserve, made the whole scheme regular: everything moves in 1.5 dB steps, scalefactors are differentially coded against the global gain rather than transmitted absolutely, and a long block is divided into about fifty scalefactor bands rather than 21 — the exact count varies with sample rate. Finer noise shaping over more bands, from the same psychoacoustic model, is a large part of why AAC beats MP3 at the same rate; Chapter 08 takes the rest of the list.

The direction that catches people out

Amplifying a scalefactor band makes it quieter, not louder. The field appears in the requantiser with a minus sign: raising it shrinks that band's step size, so the band is coded more finely, its quantisation noise drops by 3.01 dB, its integers get bigger and it costs more bits. The reconstructed level of the band does not change at all — the decoder divides the gain straight back out. A scalefactor scales the ruler, never the signal.

The wrong measure improves

Spend the same bits by signal-to-noise ratio and you will measure better and sound worse.

The distortion an encoder is minimising is not mean squared error. It is the ratio of the noise it has injected into a band to the noise that band can hide — the noise-to-mask ratio — and because both are powers, it is a subtraction in decibels.

NMRk= 10log10 ε2kTk =SMRk−SNRk transparent coding means NMR < 0 dB in every band of every block; SMR is the signal-to-mask ratio the psychoacoustic model reports

Those two measures do not merely differ. Under a bit budget they actively disagree, and the disagreement is not subtle. Take the same programme, the same transform, the same entropy coder and the same number of bits, and allocate them two ways: once so that the noise floor follows the masking threshold up and down the spectrum, and once so that the noise floor is flat, which is what minimising mean squared error tells you to do. At 56 kbit/s the flat allocation measures 3.7 dB better in signal-to-noise ratio — 12.1 dB against 8.4 — and it is the only one of the two with any band whose noise rises above the threshold at all.

A codec does not decide what to keep. It decides how coarsely to round, and then finds out what survived. The whole of move three

This is why every serious quality measure in audio is either a listening test or a model of one. A null test — subtract the decoded file from the original and listen to the difference — is the most misleading tool in the amateur toolkit, because it measures precisely the quantity the encoder was deliberately not minimising. The residual of a good 128 kbit/s encode is loud, broadband and recognisably the music, as the figure on the contents page demonstrates by playing it to you. That tells you nothing except that the encoder did its job. What a residual is genuinely good for is locating where an encoder went wrong, and that wants a spectrogram rather than a loudspeaker — the 1996 spectrum-analyser codebase elsewhere in this almanac is about building exactly that tool.

How many bits is a second of music, really?

Johnston's 1988 paper at ICASSP asked the question the right way round: take the masking threshold, work out how many bits are needed to quantise the spectrum so that no band's noise exceeds it, and call the answer the perceptual entropy. For a range of CD-quality material the figure came out around 2.1 bits per sample — against the 16 the disc carries. At 44.1 kHz that is about 93 kbit/s per channel, or 185 for a stereo pair coded independently, which sits squarely in the 192–256 kbit/s range where MP3 transparency is usually placed. It was computed four years before MPEG-1 was published.

Spend the bits yourself

There is one free parameter, and it is measured in decibels.

Return to Δk = √(12Tk) and add the one thing it is missing: a single global offset, in decibels, that says how far above or below the threshold to put the noise in every band at once.

Δk(λ)= √12·Tk·10λ/10 ⟹NMRk≈λ λ = 0 dB means "quantise exactly to the threshold"; λ = −10 dB means "ten decibels of margin everywhere"; the rate loop bisects λ

That is the entire rate control problem in one variable. Bits are monotonic in λ, so a bisection of twenty iterations lands on any achievable budget to better than a tenth of a kilobit, and the value it lands on is directly interpretable: it is the noise-to-mask ratio the encoder was able to afford. On the default programme at 80 kbit/s, λ comes out at −8.5 dB; at 56 kbit/s it is −2.4 dB; and at 48 kbit/s it is −0.2 dB, which is to say the encoder has run out of margin altogether and is coding right on the audibility line.

A real encoder has more freedom than one number, because it can also move individual bands relative to the threshold. That is what the scalefactors of the previous section are, and it is what the figure below hands to you. Drag on the plot to raise or lower the noise you are prepared to accept in each band; the bit count, the number of audible bands and the worst noise-to-mask ratio all recompute from the real coefficients of a real signal as you drag.

What the theory says

Shannon answered this in 1959, and the answer is a water level.

For a memoryless Gaussian source of variance σ2 and squared-error distortion D, the rate–distortion function is exact and has no free parameters.

R(D)= 12 log2 σ2D ,  0<D≤σ2 invert it and D = σ²2−2R: 6.02 dB of distortion per bit, which is where that number comes from in the first place

The useful version is the one for many sources at once. Give a coder a set of independent Gaussian components with variances σi2 — which is a fair description of a block of MDCT coefficients grouped into bands — and ask it to minimise total rate for a total distortion. The answer, the reverse water-filling solution, is startlingly simple: pick a level θ, give every component distortion min(θ, σi2), and spend nothing at all on the components that fall below the water.

Di=min(θ, σi2) ,   R= ∑ni=1 max(0, 12 log2 σi2θ) Cover & Thomas, Elements of Information Theory, §10.3.3 — a flat noise floor at height θ, and silence below it

Read that carefully and you have the red curve of Fig 5.2: minimising mean squared error means a flat noise floor. The perceptual coder's entire departure from information theory is to stop measuring distortion in units of signal power and start measuring it in units of masking threshold. Do that, and the water level becomes θk = Tk·10λ/10: a water level with a shape, poured to follow the threshold rather than to lie flat. Same theorem, different metric, an order of magnitude of bit rate.

In practice a coder does not solve for θ directly; it minimises a Lagrangian and lets the multiplier find the rate.

J=D+λR at the optimum every band sits at the same slope ∂D/∂R; a band where one more bit buys more distortion reduction than elsewhere is a band that has been underfunded
The 1.53 dB nobody gets back

Entropy-coded scalar quantisation — round each coefficient independently, then code the integers — cannot reach the rate–distortion bound. At high rate it sits about 1.53 dB above it, equivalently a quarter of a bit per sample, and the gap is geometric rather than statistical: a scalar quantiser's decision regions are intervals, and intervals are a poor way to tile a space. Vector quantisation recovers it by using rounder cells. CELT, the transform half of Opus, spends that coin in a particularly elegant way — it codes each band's energy explicitly and then its shape as a point on a pyramid of integer vectors, which both recovers some of the space-filling gain and guarantees the band's loudness survives quantisation exactly. Chapter 09 does the combinatorics; ropus contains a working decoder.

The other way to close part of the gap keeps the scalar quantiser and improves the search. Plain rounding picks the nearest reconstruction level for each coefficient independently, which is optimal for distortion alone and not for D + λR: rounding a coefficient down to a cheaper integer sometimes costs less distortion than the bits it saves are worth, especially near a run of zeros where one extra zero shortens a Huffman code. Searching over those choices with a trellis — rate-distortion-optimised quantisation, the same idea video coders call RDOQ — recovers a few per cent of bit rate. FFmpeg's native AAC encoder ships one as the anmr coder, though its own documentation describes it as experimental and currently worse than the default two-loop search, which is a fair summary of how hard the search is to tune.

Two loops, fighting

The reference encoder's structure was two nested loops, and they were not guaranteed to agree.

The informative encoder in the MPEG-1 standard — the dist10 reference source that every early implementation started from — solves the allocation problem with two loops, one inside the other. The inner loop is the rate loop: given the current scalefactors, raise global_gain until the Huffman-coded granule fits the bits available. The outer loop is the distortion loop or noise-control loop: quantise, measure the noise in each scalefactor band, and amplify every band whose noise exceeds its threshold. Then run the rate loop again, because amplifying bands has just made the granule more expensive.

The inner loop always terminates: a large enough step size sends every coefficient to zero, and zeros are cheap. The pair does not. Amplifying a band by one scalefactor step lowers its noise by 3.01 dB and costs bits; the rate loop recovers those bits by raising global_gain, and 3.01 dB is exactly two steps of global gain. On difficult material at a tight budget the two moves cancel to the decibel, the encoder walks up both fields in lockstep, and the noise-to-mask ratio does not move at all. Fig 5.5 lets you provoke that.

Why the reference encoder sounded bad and LAME did not

Everything above is a search strategy, not a standard: the MP3 bitstream is specified, the encoder is not. dist10 was written to demonstrate the syntax, and it shows — a crude psychoacoustic model, an absolute-threshold curve that was close to arbitrary, and loops that gave up early. LAME started in mid-1998 as Mike Cheng's patch against those sources, moved to a rewrite under Mark Taylor, and by LAME 3.81 in 2000 had replaced the last of the ISO code. What it changed was almost entirely on this page: a reworked psychoacoustic model, tuned ATH curves, far better short-block handling, several selectable noise-shaping strategies in the outer loop, and above all the -V variable-bitrate modes that abandon the fixed budget altogether. Version 3.100, released in October 2017, is still the reference implementation people mean when they say "a well-encoded MP3", and -V2 — about 190 kbit/s on average — is the usual answer to "which setting".

Constant bitrate is a fiction

MP3 frames are a fixed size. The audio inside them is not, and does not start where the frame does.

A Layer III frame carries 1 152 samples — two granules of 576 — and at a constant bit rate its size follows directly from the rate and the sampling frequency.

frame= floor( 144·Rfs )+padding bytes 144 = 1152/8; the padding bit adds one byte to some frames so the long-run average is exact

At 44.1 kHz the division almost never comes out whole — 128 kbit/s gives 417.96 bytes — so the padding bit is set on about 96 frames in every 100. At 48 kHz and 128 kbit/s it is exactly 384 and the padding bit is never set. Either way the frame is a fixed size, and the demand for bits is not: a cymbal crash wants several times what the decay after it wants. Layer III's answer is the bit reservoir, and it works by decoupling the frame boundary from the audio-data boundary entirely.

Each frame's side information opens with main_data_begin, nine bits wide, which is a backward byte pointer: it says how far before this frame's header the frame's own audio data begins. Nine bits is 0 to 511 bytes. A frame that needs few bits simply stops early and leaves the remainder for its successors; a frame that needs many reaches back into that unused space. At 128 kbit/s and 44.1 kHz a frame holds 417 bytes of which 4 are header and 32 are stereo side information, leaving 381 bytes of main data — so a full reservoir is about one and a third frames' worth of audio held in reserve.

The reservoir is a fudge with consequences. Because a frame's audio may live several frames earlier in the file, an MP3 cannot be cut at a frame boundary without corrupting the frames that follow — which is why mp3splt exists, why RFC 3119 defines a whole alternative RTP payload format that rearranges the data so each packet is self-contained, and why gapless playback needs the encoder delay recorded in a LAME or Xing tag. Variable bit rate is the honest mode, because λ is the thing a listener cares about and bits per second is not. Fix λ and the encoder spends what each passage needs: Fig 5.4's curves make the argument numerically, since a fixed rate means a fixed x-coordinate on a curve whose shape changes completely between the applause and the castanets. Fix the rate instead and you have promised the network something at the cost of promising the listener nothing. The reason CBR survives is that some networks genuinely do require it, which is why Opus carries a constrained-VBR mode that bounds the buffering delay without going fully constant — and why, per the libopus documentation, the constraint is honoured by the MDCT layer and ignored by the speech layer. What people get wrong

Six corrections, each of which changes what you would expect a codec to do.

"A higher bit rate means a smaller step size"
It means a smaller offset from the threshold. The step size in a band is set first by that band's masking threshold and only then by the budget: at any rate, a band the model says is well masked gets a coarse step and a band it says is exposed gets a fine one. Raising the rate moves λ down and every band with it; it does not make the allocation more uniform.
"The quantiser throws away the quiet sounds"
It throws away precision, and it zeroes lines that are quiet relative to their own band's threshold — which is not at all the same set. A quiet passage has a low threshold and gets fine steps; a quiet line sitting next to a loud one in the same band has a high threshold and gets nothing. That is why the audible failures of low-rate coding are in the presence of loud material, not in the quiet parts.
"The 3/4 law keeps the relative error constant"
It does not, and Fig 5.1 measures the shortfall. Constant relative error requires a logarithmic characteristic, which is what µ-law and A-law telephony use. The 3/4 power gets a quarter of the way there — 0.75 dB of signal-to-noise ratio lost per dB of level rather than a full dB — and its second job, keeping the integers small enough for a 16-entry Huffman table with an escape, matters just as much.
"Signal-to-noise ratio tells you how good a codec is"
At a fixed rate it is very nearly an inverted quality metric, and Fig 5.2 shows the inversion happening: at the default budget the allocation that measures 3.7 dB better is the only one of the two with audible noise. The same objection sinks null tests, waveform difference plots and every "I subtracted the files" argument. Use listeners, or use a model of a listener — Chapter 11 covers both.
"Amplifying a scalefactor band makes it louder"
It makes it finer. The field enters the requantiser with a negative exponent, so raising it shrinks the step, lowers the noise by 3.01 dB and costs bits; the reconstructed level is identical because the decoder divides the same factor back out. The vocabulary is a historical accident and it has confused readers of the standard for thirty years.
"The bit reservoir makes the frames variable-length"
The frames are exactly the length the header says. What moves is the boundary of the main data, which main_data_begin can place up to 511 bytes before the frame that owns it. A constant-bit-rate MP3 really is constant bit rate on the wire; it simply is not self-contained frame by frame.
The rate loop is not an optimiser. It is a bisection search for one number — how many decibels above the threshold you are willing to put the noise — and everything else in the encoder is an argument about how to spend the answer. What forty years of tuning came down to

What comes out of this stage is no longer audio. It is an array of small signed integers, one per spectral line, together with a handful of exponents; and the integers have a distribution that is nothing like uniform. At 64 kbit/s on the default programme, about 73 per cent of the 512 lines in a block quantise to zero, most of the survivors are ±1, and magnitudes of 15 or more are rare enough that MP3's big-value tables do not carry them: 15 is an escape, and the excess follows in a fixed number of linbits. A distribution that skewed has an entropy far below the word length any fixed-width code would need, and the gap between those two numbers is the last large saving in the chain.

That saving is the subject of Chapter 06: Shannon's bound and what it actually promises, Huffman's algorithm and the one-bit ceiling it cannot break through, the 32 code tables MP3 carries and the regions it splits a granule into so it can choose between them, and the arithmetic and range coders that beat Huffman by spending fractions of a bit. The quantiser has decided what the decoder will be told. What remains is to say it in as few bits as the statement is worth.
