Two coders in one bitstream, and five bits that choose between them.

Every codec before it had to decide whether it was modelling the talker or modelling the listener. Opus refuses: it ships a linear-predictive speech coder and a transform music coder in a single packet format, hands both of them the same range coder, and re-decides which one is running at the head of every packet — in under a millisecond of signalling, without renegotiating anything.

The thirty-year split

Speech coding and music coding had been separate trades since the 1970s.

Two traditions, two literatures, two sets of conferences. One of them models the source: speech comes out of a tube of known length driven by a buzzing or hissing excitation, so send the tube and the buzz and let the decoder rebuild the sound. That line runs from the channel vocoder through LPC-10, CELP, G.729, AMR, Speex and Skype's SILK, and it is astonishingly efficient — intelligible speech at 2.4 kbit/s, comfortable speech at 16 — precisely because it is not coding a waveform at all. Play a cello into it and you get a noise that is not a cello.

The other tradition models the listener: transform the signal, compute what the ear cannot hear, and quantise down to that line. That is the machinery of Chapter 04 through Chapter 06, and of MP3 and AAC. It will code anything — cello, applause, a door slamming — and it degrades gracefully with bitrate. Ask it for 8 kbit/s and it produces a gargling wreck, because at eight thousand bits a second there is no honest way to describe a waveform and no source model to fall back on.

Real applications need both. A voice call that occasionally carries hold music, a conference where someone shares a video, a game where a teammate's microphone picks up their stereo: each one either switches codec mid-session — a renegotiation, a gap, a new payload type — or picks one codec and is bad at half its job. In 2010 the IETF chartered a working group to produce a single royalty-free codec that would not have to choose. Skype contributed SILK, its own deployed speech coder; Xiph.Org contributed CELT, Jean-Marc Valin's very-low-delay transform coder. The two were not blended. They were bolted together behind one packet format, and the result was published as RFC 6716 in September 2012.

The argument over whether to model the source or model the ear was settled by shipping both, and letting five bits at the head of each packet say which one is running. The Opus settlement

The operating envelope is deliberately absurd. Bitrates from 6 to 510 kbit/s. Frame sizes of 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 60 ms, with up to 120 ms of audio in one packet. Five audio bandwidths, from a 4 kHz narrowband telephone channel to 20 kHz fullband. Mono, stereo, or arbitrary multistream for surround and ambisonics. Any of those can change from one packet to the next, and the decoder needs no warning beyond the packet itself.

Either way the application hands Opus 48 kHz audio (or 8, 12, 16 or 24 kHz, which the library resamples) and gets it back at whatever rate it asks for.

Royalty-free, and it stuck

Opus was published with a BSD-licensed reference implementation, and RFC 7874 (May 2016) makes it mandatory to implement in every WebRTC endpoint, alongside G.711. That is why it is underneath Discord, WhatsApp, Signal, Google Meet and essentially every browser-based call, and why YouTube serves Opus inside WebM. RFC 8251 (October 2017) folded a set of corrections into the normative decoder; RFC 7845 (April 2016) defines Ogg Opus, which is what a .opus file is. Elsewhere in this almanac, ropus is that decoder ported to safe Rust and checked bit-exact against the C.

SILK: model the talker

A speech coder sends the shape of your throat, not the sound of it.

Strip a vowel down and it is a periodic excitation — the vocal folds slapping shut a hundred-odd times a second — poured through a resonant tube. The tube has no zeros worth speaking of, only resonances, so its transfer function is well modelled by an all-pole filter of modest order. That is the source–filter model, and it is the whole of linear predictive coding.

H(z)=gA(z) , A(z)=1+ ∑pi=1 aiz−i p coefficients and one gain describe the vocal tract; whatever is left over is the excitation

Run the inverse filter A(z) over the speech and what comes out is the residual: the part the tube could not explain. If the model is good the residual is nearly white, which is the point — a white signal has no structure left to exploit, so its only remaining cost is its amplitude. The coefficients come from the Levinson–Durbin recursion on the signal's autocorrelation, the same recursion that Chapter 10 uses to compress audio without losing a bit. SILK uses a short filter: ten coefficients at the narrow bandwidths, sixteen at wideband.

Two things stop that being the whole story. The first is that the coefficients cannot be transmitted. The ai are ferociously ill-conditioned: perturb one of them in the third decimal place and a pole can wander outside the unit circle, at which point the decoder's filter howls instead of resonating. So SILK converts them to line spectral frequencies — the roots of the two auxiliary polynomials formed by adding and subtracting a reversed copy of A(z).

P(z)=A(z)+z−(p+1)A(z−1),  Q(z)=A(z)−z−(p+1)A(z−1) the roots of P and Q lie on the unit circle and interlace; keep them in order and the filter cannot be unstable

Those roots are angles, so they are just frequencies between 0 and the Nyquist rate, and they have two properties a quantiser dreams of: keeping them sorted and distinct guarantees a stable synthesis filter, and an error in one of them perturbs the spectrum only near that frequency, rather than everywhere at once. SILK normalises them (hence NLSFs), predicts them across the frame, and quantises what is left with a two-stage vector quantiser — a coarse stage that picks an entry from a trained codebook, then a residual stage that corrects it. Vector quantisation rather than scalar because the NLSFs of real speech occupy a small, oddly shaped region of the p-dimensional space, and a scalar quantiser would spend most of its codewords on throats that do not exist.

The second is pitch. A short all-pole filter models the envelope, not the harmonic comb underneath it, so the residual of voiced speech is not white at all: it is a train of pulses one pitch period apart. SILK therefore runs a long-term predictor over the residual, with one lag estimated per 5 ms subframe and a five-tap filter at that lag.

e(n)=r(n)− ∑2k=−2 bk r(n−T+k) five taps, not one: a fractional and slowly drifting period, and the shape of the glottal pulse, in the same filter

One tap would only work if the period were an exact integer number of samples and perfectly stable. Five taps interpolate between samples, absorb a drifting period, and incidentally apply a little spectral shaping to the pulse. Finally the residual is quantised inside a noise-shaping loop: the quantiser's own error is filtered and fed back so that the coding noise takes the shape of a smoothed version of the signal spectrum, rather than lying flat. That is the speech-coder's version of the masking threshold from Chapter 03 — arrived at independently, doing the same job, hiding the noise under the formants.

CELT: energy first, shape second

Constrained energy is not a feature of CELT. It is the name of the idea.

CELT is an MDCT coder, so Chapter 04's machinery applies unchanged: overlapping blocks, a lapped transform, time-domain alias cancellation. What is unusual is the geometry of the window. CELT uses a flat-topped window with a fixed 2.5 ms overlap regardless of frame size, where AAC's window overlap is half the block. A 20 ms AAC-LC frame carries about 21 ms of look-ahead in its window alone; a 20 ms CELT frame carries 2.5 ms. That single decision is most of why Opus can hold a conversation.

The second decision is the one the name commemorates. Group the MDCT lines into bands that approximate the critical bands of Chapter 02 — twenty-one of them at 48 kHz, laid out so that the boundaries fall on whole bins at every frame size. Then, before coding anything else, transmit each band's energy: coarsely first, predicted from the previous frame and from the band below, then refined with fine bits if the budget allows. Divide the band by that energy, which leaves a unit-norm vector, and spend every remaining bit on direction only.

Xb=gb ·yb‖yb‖ , gb=‖Xb‖ gain and shape are coded separately, and the shape is forced to unit norm — so the decoded band has exactly the energy that was sent

The consequence is worth stating carefully, because it is the single largest behavioural difference between Opus and its predecessors. In MP3 and AAC, running out of bits means the quantiser step grows until coefficients round to zero, and a band that rounds entirely to zero disappears. That is where birdies come from — a band flickering in and out between frames, which the ear hears as a tinkling above the music (Chapter 11). In CELT, running out of bits means the shape gets cruder. The energy was already sent. A band cannot go silent by accident, because its loudness was never in the quantiser's gift.

Three refinements keep that promise honest at low rates. When a band can afford no pulses at all, CELT does not leave it empty — it folds content from lower in the spectrum into it and scales the result to the transmitted energy, so the band is filled with something spectrally plausible rather than with silence. When a frame is transient and has been split into several short MDCTs, a band whose pulses all land in one sub-block leaves the others at zero; the anti-collapse step injects pseudo-random energy to stop that. And a spreading parameter rotates the pulse vector to smear a small number of pulses across the band, because a handful of isolated spikes sounds tonal when the original was noise.

The two things a 2.5 ms window cannot see

A 2.5 ms MDCT at 48 kHz has 120 bins, which is 200 Hz apart — far too coarse to resolve the harmonics of a voice or a violin. CELT therefore runs a pitch prefilter at the encoder, a comb filter that removes the periodicity before the transform, and the matching postfilter at the decoder to put it back. It also varies the transform: on a detected transient the frame is coded as several short MDCTs with their coefficients interleaved, and the time-frequency resolution can be traded per band, so a band carrying a cymbal can buy time resolution while the band carrying the bass keeps frequency resolution in the same frame.

The pyramid

A codebook of ten billion entries that occupies no memory at all.

Having normalised a band to unit norm, CELT has to describe a point on the surface of an N-dimensional sphere using a known, exact number of bits. Pyramid vector quantisation, published by Thomas Fischer in 1986, does it by replacing the sphere with a diamond — the set of integer vectors whose absolute values sum to a constant K.

S(N,K)={ y∈ℤN : ∑Ni=1 |yi|=K } K "pulses" distributed over N positions with signs; the codeword is the whole vector, normalised at the decoder

Count them and you get V(N,K), which satisfies a three-term recursion — either the last coordinate is zero, or it is non-zero and you either did or did not use a pulse on it:

V(N,K)=V(N−1,K) +V(N,K−1) +V(N−1,K−1) ,  b=log2V(N,K) V(N,0) = 1 and V(0,K) = 0 for K > 0; b is the exact cost of one codeword, in bits

Three properties follow, and all three matter. First, the rate is known before anything is coded: pick K such that log2V fits the bits you have, and the band costs precisely that. There is no rate loop of the kind Chapter 05 describes, no iterating a global gain until the frame fits — CELT computes the allocation and then hits it. Second, the codeword can be enumerated rather than stored: the index of a given y is computed arithmetically from the same recursion, so a 40-bit codebook with a trillion entries costs a few hundred bytes of table and some integer arithmetic. Third, and most usefully, every codeword has the same norm once normalised, so the decoded band's energy is exactly what the gain said it was — however few pulses were affordable.

Work one out by hand and the size is startling: eight coefficients with four pulses between them gives V(8,4) = 2 816 distinct shapes, an 11.5-bit codeword, for a band you could not begin to describe with eleven bits of scalar quantisation. The two ends of the table are the argument. A band of 32 coefficients with one pulse costs exactly six bits — you are told which coefficient is largest and its sign, nothing more, and the decoder fills the rest by folding. The same band with 32 pulses costs 77 bits, about two and a half per coefficient. Everything Opus does between 6 and 510 kbit/s lives on that curve, band by band, frame by frame.

Hybrid, and the seam

Above 8 kHz there is nothing for a vocal-tract model to say.

Hybrid mode stacks the two coders on top of each other with the crossover at 8 kHz. SILK runs at its wideband setting, 16 kHz internally, and codes everything below the crossover; CELT codes the same frame but discards every band below 8 kHz and codes only what is above. The division is not a compromise, it is a reading of the material: speech intelligibility, and almost all of speech's structure, lives below 8 kHz, where a source model buys an enormous amount. Above it there is sibilance, breath, cymbals and room — texture, with no periodicity for an LPC filter to predict, and exactly the sort of thing a transform coder describes for very few bits.

Hybrid exists only in four configurations: super-wideband or fullband, at 10 or 20 ms. That is deliberate. Below about 20 kbit/s there are not enough bits to run two coders at once and SILK alone wins; above about 40 kbit/s the source model stops paying for itself on general audio and CELT alone wins. Hybrid owns the middle, which happens to be where most voice calls actually run.

The two coders write into the same range coder

There is no delimiter between SILK's bits and CELT's, no padding, no byte alignment. Both layers drive one range coder of the kind Chapter 06 describes — 32-bit range and value registers, renormalised eight bits at a time. CELT's uniformly-distributed symbols, which a range coder would only make larger, are written as raw bits packed backwards from the end of the buffer: least significant bit of the first value into the least significant bit of the last byte, and onwards towards the front. The range coder grows forwards, the raw bits grow backwards, and the encoder knows it is out of room at the instant they meet. It is a strikingly tidy way to make a fixed-size packet and waste nothing.

Because the mode is re-declared every packet, an encoder can walk across the whole envelope mid-stream without telling anyone. A call that starts as SILK narrowband at 12 kbit/s because the network is poor can become hybrid fullband at 48 kbit/s two hundred milliseconds later, and then CELT-only at 2.5 ms frames because the application asked for the lowest possible latency. No renegotiation, no new payload type, no gap. What makes that safe is that the decoder is stateless about mode: everything it needs to interpret a packet is in the packet's first byte.

One byte, decoded

The table of contents byte carries every decision the encoder made.

Every Opus packet begins with a TOC byte: five bits of configuration, one stereo flag, two bits of frame count code. The five configuration bits select one of thirty-two combinations of mode, bandwidth and frame size — and thirty-two is not a round number chosen for convenience, it is exactly the number of combinations that are legal.

The stereo flag is one bit and means exactly what it says. Everything else about the channel layout — how many streams, which is which, how they map to loudspeakers — lives outside the packet, in the container's head: Ogg Opus's identification header for a file, or the SDP for a call. A raw Opus packet does not even carry its own sample rate. It carries a duration and a bandwidth, and the decoder outputs at whatever rate the application asked for.

The last two bits, the framing code, decide how many frames the packet carries and how their lengths are found.

510 kbit/s× 20 ms÷8 =1275 bytes the two-byte length code and the codec's stated ceiling are the same fact, seen from two directions

A frame of length zero is legal and means discontinuous transmission: nothing was sent for that frame because nothing was happening. Padding is signalled separately and can be arbitrarily long, which exists so that a constant-bitrate channel can be filled without lying about the audio. And for multistream — surround, ambisonics — each stream's packet after the first uses the self-delimiting framing of RFC 6716's Appendix B, which prefixes the length rather than inferring it, so several independent Opus packets can be concatenated and taken apart again.

When packets do not arrive

A codec judged only on a clean channel is being judged on the wrong axis.

Everything in the first eight chapters of this study assumes the bits get there. On a network they frequently do not: two, five, sometimes twenty per cent of packets are dropped or arrive too late for a jitter buffer that has already played past them. A codec that is a decibel better in a laboratory and falls apart at 5 % loss is the worse codec. Opus therefore carries three separate answers to loss, and they are worth distinguishing because they cost quite different amounts.

The first answer is packet loss concealment, which costs nothing on the wire: the decoder extrapolates the missing frame from what it has. SILK continues the LPC filter with its last coefficients and a pitch-synchronous excitation; CELT repeats the last frame's band energies with a decay and randomises the shapes. In libopus 1.5, released in March 2024, this became a neural network — "Deep PLC" — trained to continue speech rather than to fade it out.

The second is in-band forward error correction, which Opus calls LBRR: low-bitrate redundancy. When the encoder has been told to expect loss, it re-encodes the frame it has just sent at a much lower rate and tucks that copy inside the next packet. Packet n then carries a good copy of frame n and a rough copy of frame n−1, and losing one packet costs you nothing but a moment of reduced quality. The cost is that the redundancy comes out of the same bitrate budget rather than being added to it: switching FEC on makes the clean channel slightly worse in exchange for making the lossy one much better. The encoder only produces it above a configured expected-loss threshold, for exactly that reason.

The third is discontinuous transmission: during silence the encoder stops sending frames altogether and the decoder generates comfort noise from the last parameters it saw. On a two-way call with normal turn-taking that halves the traffic, and unlike G.711, Opus's comfort noise is part of the codec rather than a separate payload type. Opus 1.5 added a fourth, DRED — deep redundancy — which packs up to about a second of heavily compressed speech history into each packet, enough to ride out a burst that would defeat LBRR entirely. Opus 1.6 followed in December 2025 with a machine-learning bandwidth extension that reconstructs the top octave of a wideband signal.

What people get wrong

Six corrections, in rough order of how often they are needed.

"Opus is a speech codec"
It contains one. On music it is a transform coder, and a good one: in the public multi-format listening test run at 64 kbit/s in 2011, Opus placed first with a statistically significant margin over Apple's HE-AAC, with Vorbis and Nero's HE-AAC behind. The confusion comes from the WebRTC association — most Opus streams in the world really are speech, because most of them are phone calls.
"It resamples my 44.1 kHz files to 48, which is damage"
It does resample, and the resampler is good. Set that loss against what the encoder does next, which is to discard most of the signal on purpose. Ogg Opus's identification header carries the original input rate and an output gain precisely so that a decoder can return you to where you started; the decoder will output 8, 12, 16, 24 or 48 kHz on request. If bit-exactness matters, you wanted Chapter 10.
"Two codecs in one packet"
Never two in one frame, and in hybrid mode they are stacked in frequency rather than mixed. The mode is fixed for the duration of a packet and declared in its first byte; a packet containing several frames uses the same configuration for all of them. Switching happens between packets, which is why it is free.
"The bitrate I asked for is the bitrate I get"
Opus is variable-rate by default and the setting is a target its rate controller tracks over time, not a per-packet guarantee. Quiet passages cost far less; DTX costs nothing at all. Constrained VBR and hard CBR both exist, and both are worse at the same average rate, which is the usual trade.
"510 kbit/s must be nearly lossless"
It is not, and nothing in the design is aiming at that. Above roughly 128–160 kbit/s for stereo the returns are already negligible; the ceiling exists because the frame length field goes that high, not because anyone expects it to be used. If you want the file back unchanged the answer is FLAC, at about half size and no further.
"CELT is just AAC with different letters"
Both are MDCT coders and there the resemblance stops. CELT has no scalefactors, no rate loop, no Huffman tables and no psychoacoustic model of the classical kind. It sends band energies first, codes only direction afterwards, and knows the exact cost of every decision before it makes it. The 2.5 ms fixed overlap alone puts it in a different application space from anything in Chapter 08.
A CELT band cannot fall silent by accident. Its energy was transmitted before its shape, and no shortage of bits can take that back. Why Opus does not produce birdies
Where this leaves us

The lossy argument is finished. The other end of the dial is not.

Three chapters have now taken the same four moves and settled them three ways. MP3 settled them in 1993 under the constraint of a filterbank it had inherited. AAC settled them again with a clean transform and a box of tools for the cases the model gets wrong. Opus settled them by observing that the model is not always the right instrument at all — that below 8 kHz, on speech, a description of the source beats any description of the ear, and that the two can share a packet format if you are willing to spend five bits saying which is which.

What all three have in common is that they throw information away and cannot get it back. That is a choice, not a law, and it is worth knowing exactly what the alternative costs. Chapter 10 takes the same signal and forbids any loss whatever: prediction, then Rice coding of the residual, and a compression ratio that stalls at about two to one no matter how clever the predictor gets — for reasons that are about information rather than engineering. Then it goes to the opposite extreme, and asks what is left at seven hundred bits a second.
