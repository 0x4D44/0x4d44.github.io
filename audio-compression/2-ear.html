Before an encoder throws anything away, your ear has already thrown away more.

The cochlea is not a microphone. It is a 35-millimetre filter bank with roughly two dozen usable channels, a compressive amplifier, a hard floor that varies by eighty decibels across the audio range, and two clocks that disagree by two orders of magnitude. Every one of those is a block in an audio encoder, and the encoder's whole job is to lose the same information the ear was going to lose anyway — in the same order, and no faster.

What a second costs

1 411 200 bits a second is a statement about the format, not about the music.

A compact disc spends 44 100 × 16 × 2 bits on every second of programme. Chapter 01 derives that number and shows why each of the three factors is what it is; here it is only the denominator.

R=fs× N×C= 44 100×16 ×2= 1 411 200 bit/s 176 400 bytes a second; 10.6 megabytes a minute

Opus at 96 kbit/s is 6.80 per cent of that. AAC at 128 kbit/s is 9.07 per cent. Even a 320 kbit/s MP3 — the setting people choose when they have decided not to take any chances — is 22.7 per cent. The remaining three-quarters to nineteen-twentieths is not removed by cleverness in the way a ZIP file removes repetition. It is removed because it never reached you.

That is the claim this chapter has to make good, and it is a claim about a physical system with measurable properties. Between the loudspeaker and anything you could call a percept sits an organ about the size of a pea, containing roughly 3 500 inner hair cells — the entire transducer array for one ear — feeding on the order of 30 000 nerve fibres. Before those fibres fire, the signal has been through a resonant tube, an impedance-matching transformer, a bank of overlapping bandpass filters, a compressive level detector with a hard floor, and two quite different temporal integrators. Each stage discards something. By the time there is a neural code at all, the 1.41 megabits are long gone.

A codec does not compress music. It measures a listener, and then declines to transmit whatever that measurement says will not arrive. The perceptual coding bargain, restated

What follows is that measurement, stage by stage, with the arithmetic running in front of you. Nothing here is specific to MP3 or Opus; every number in this chapter predates every codec in this study, and most of them predate digital audio.

The pressure amplifier

The ear's best frequency is a plumbing artefact, not a neural preference.

Sound in air has to drive fluid in the cochlea, and air is a terrible impedance match for water: most of the incident power would simply reflect off a bare oval window. The outer and middle ear exist to recover that loss, and they do it with two entirely mechanical tricks.

The first is resonance. The ear canal is a tube about 25 mm long, closed at the eardrum and open at the world — a quarter-wave resonator.

fr= c4L = 343 m/s4×0.025 m =3 430 Hz c = 343 m/s in dry air at 20 °C; canal length varies between people, and so therefore does this peak

That resonance adds something in the region of 10 to 20 dB around 2–4 kHz, with large individual variation, and the concha adds a second, broader peak somewhere near 4.5–5.5 kHz. The second trick is the middle ear, which is a transformer: a large membrane pushing on a small one, with a lever in between. The effective vibrating area of the eardrum is about 55 mm² — not its full ~85 mm², because the membrane does not move as a rigid piston — and the stapes footplate is about 3.2 mm².

G= AdrumAstapes × lmalleuslincus ≈17× 1.3=22 , GdB=20 log10G= 26.9 dB textbooks quote the area ratio as 17, 18 or 20; at 20 the product is 28.3 dB. Call it 25–30 dB and do not pretend to more precision.

Add the canal resonance to the transformer gain and the system's most sensitive region lands where it does for reasons a plumber would recognise. The dip in the threshold curve at 3–4 kHz that you will meet twice more in this chapter is a 25-millimetre tube, not a decision the auditory system made about speech.

Your inner ear makes sound

The outer hair cells are not receptors; they are motors. Thomas Gold argued in 1948 that the cochlea had to contain an active, energy-supplying element, and was ignored for thirty years — until David Kemp recorded otoacoustic emissions leaking back out of the ear canal in 1978. A standard newborn hearing screen works by playing a click and listening for the ear's reply, which is why it can be done on a sleeping baby who cannot be asked anything.

Frequency is a distance

On the basilar membrane, an octave is about five millimetres.

Georg von Békésy won the 1961 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for working out what the membrane actually does, largely by looking at it. A pressure wave entering at the base sets up a travelling wave that grows as it moves towards the apex, peaks at a place determined by its frequency, and then dies almost immediately past that place. The gradient responsible is mechanical: the membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, with stiffness falling about a hundredfold over 35 mm.

Donald Greenwood put a formula to it in 1961 and re-fitted it across species in 1990. With x the fraction of membrane length measured from the apex, the characteristic frequency of a place is

F(x)=A(10ax−k), x(F)= 1a log10( FA +k) human constants A = 165.4 Hz, a = 2.1, k = 0.88. They were chosen so that F(0) = 19.85 Hz at the apex and F(1) = 20 677 Hz at the base — which is where "20 Hz to 20 kHz" comes from.

Take the derivative and something useful falls out. Well above the Ak term the map is exactly logarithmic, so the distance occupied by one octave becomes a constant:

Δdoct→ L log102a = 35×0.301032.1 =5.02 mm measured directly: 3.31 mm from 200 to 400 Hz, 4.54 mm from 1 to 2 kHz, 4.89 mm from 4 to 8 kHz

Your entire musical range fits inside 35 millimetres of tissue, a distance you could cover with a thumbnail, and it fits at about five millimetres per octave. Drag the slider below from 1 kHz to 2 kHz and watch the peak move less than half a centimetre; drag it from 20 Hz to 200 Hz and watch it move eleven.

The filter bank

Two dozen numbers is the entire frequency resolution of hearing.

Harvey Fletcher's 1940 experiment is the origin of everything in this section. Put a tone in a band of noise and measure the level at which the tone becomes audible. Widen the noise band, keeping its spectral density constant, and the tone's threshold rises — because more noise is getting into the same channel. Then, at a certain width, the threshold stops rising. Widening the noise further adds noise that the listener's channel cannot see. That width is the critical band, and it is a direct measurement of the bandwidth of one filter in the ear's filter bank.

Eberhard Zwicker tabulated 24 of them in 1961 and named the scale the Bark, after Heinrich Barkhausen. Zwicker and Terhardt gave a closed form in 1980, which is the version the MPEG psychoacoustic models use:

z(f)=13 arctan(0.00076f) +3.5 arctan((f/7500)2) Bark arctangents in radians, f in hertz; accurate to better than about 0.2 Bark across the audio range, and not analytically invertible — Traunmüller's 1990 form is the one to use when you need to go back the other way

with an accompanying expression for the width of a band centred anywhere:

ΔfG(f)=25 +75 [1+1.4 (f/1000)2]0.69 Hz 100.7 Hz at 100 Hz · 162.2 Hz at 1 kHz · 685.4 Hz at 4 kHz · 3 468 Hz at 13.5 kHz

Brian Moore and Brian Glasberg arrived at a different ruler in 1990, from notched-noise masking rather than from Zwicker's methods, and fitted a simpler formula. Their equivalent rectangular bandwidth and its integral, the ERB number, are continuous rather than tabulated:

ERB(F)=24.7(4.37F+1) Hz, ERBS(F)=21.4 log10(4.37F+1) F in kilohertz. The ERB number runs from 0.78 at 20 Hz to 15.62 at 1 kHz to 41.65 at 20 kHz — about forty filters, against Zwicker's twenty-four, because they are counting differently.

The two scales agree closely above about 500 Hz and part company sharply below it: at 50 Hz the Bark bandwidth is 100 Hz and the ERB is 30 Hz. That is not a units problem, and it is not a rounding difference. It is an unresolved empirical disagreement about how selective the ear is at low frequencies, and any codec that allocates by band inherits whichever answer its designers picked.

Twenty-four bands over three decades, the lowest nine of them 100 Hz wide and the highest one 3 500 Hz wide, is the entire frequency resolution the receiver has. A 4 096-point FFT at 44.1 kHz resolves 10.8 Hz everywhere. The ear resolves about 100 Hz at 100 Hz and about 3 500 Hz at 13.5 kHz. That mismatch is the reason every codec in this study groups its spectral lines into bands before deciding anything, and the reason those groups are called scalefactor bands rather than bins.

The Bark scale is not a psychological unit. It is a ruler. One Bark is between one and one and a half millimetres of basilar membrane, and "critical band" means nothing more mystical than the same patch of tissue is already moving. Why masking is plumbing

Divide 35 mm by 24 bands and you get 1.46 mm. Measure it properly through Greenwood's map and it varies: about 1.0 mm per Bark around 1–2 kHz, 1.2 mm at 500 Hz and 4 kHz, and 2 mm or more at the extremes where both scales are least certain. The scale was never meant to be exactly uniform in millimetres; it was meant to be uniform in masking behaviour, and it is close enough to a constant distance that the physical reading is the right intuition.

The floor

The threshold of hearing is an eighty-decibel bowl, and encoders carry a copy of it.

Below some level, at each frequency, nothing gets through at all. That is the absolute threshold of hearing, and its practical importance to a codec is blunt: when no other sound is playing, the absolute threshold is the masking threshold. Bits spent describing a −60 dBFS 30 Hz rumble are bits spent on nothing, and an encoder that knows the curve simply refuses to spend them.

Two different curves travel under this name and they are regularly conflated. The first is Terhardt's closed-form approximation, popularised for codec engineers by Painter and Spanias in 2000, and reproduced in essentially every textbook since:

Tq(f)=3.64 (f/1000)−0.8 −6.5 e−0.6(f/1000 − 3.3)2 +10−3 (f/1000)4 dB SPL three terms: a low-frequency roll-off, a Gaussian dip near 3.3 kHz, and a quartic that is meant to close the curve at the top. Minimum −4.98 dB SPL at 3 324 Hz.

It is one line of code, which is why it won. It is also wrong at both ends, and spectacularly wrong at the top: that quartic term predicts +51 dB SPL at 15 kHz, +105 dB at 18 kHz and +160 dB at 20 kHz, the last two of which are not thresholds but arithmetic running away. Every honest implementation clips it. The toy encoder used throughout this study caps its own threshold at 80 dB SPL for exactly this reason: believe the formula and you throw away the top octave of a loud recording.

The second curve is the one in the standard. ISO 226:2003 tabulates a threshold row — conventionally written Tf — at 29 frequencies from 20 Hz to 12.5 kHz, and it reaches −6.0 dB SPL at 3 150 Hz. The two disagree by up to about 8 dB. Where they disagree, the standard is the measurement and the formula is the convenience.

Loudness is not level

Turn it down and the bass leaves the room.

Fletcher and Munson measured, in 1933, how much sound pressure each frequency needs in order to seem as loud as a 1 kHz tone. The family of curves they produced was re-determined by Robinson and Dadson in 1956 under free-field conditions, standardised as ISO 226, replaced in 2003 after a pooled international re-measurement, and revised again in 2023 by amounts no larger than about half a decibel over most of the range. The 2003 coefficients are what this page uses.

The unit on those curves is the phon: a tone has a loudness level of N phon if it is as loud as an N dB SPL tone at 1 kHz. By construction every contour passes through its own number at 1 kHz. The ISO equation builds each contour from three tabulated per-frequency constants — an exponent αf, a transfer magnitude LU, and the threshold row Tf:

Af=4.47×10−3(100.025LN−1.15) +[0.4×10(Tf + LU)/10 − 9]αf
Lp= 10αf log10(Af)−LU+94 dB SPL valid 20 Hz–12.5 kHz and 0–90 phon (0–80 above 4 kHz). Evaluated at 1 kHz it returns the input to within 0.01 dB, which is the check worth running on any implementation.

A trap sits in that equation. The standard's threshold contour is the tabulated Tf row, not the result of evaluating the formula at LN = 0. The two differ by up to about 2 dB — 78.5 against 76.6 at 20 Hz. Plot Tf.

Phons measure loudness on a scale where equal steps are equal levels, which is not how loudness behaves. The sone, proposed by S. S. Stevens in 1936, fixes that: one sone is 40 phon, and every further ten phon doubles it.

S=2(LN − 40)/10 forLN≥40, S=(LN/40)2.642 below so 20 phon is 0.160 sone, not the 0.250 the doubling rule would predict — loudness grows faster than a doubling per ten phon near threshold

The consequence that matters for everything downstream is in the shape of the family, not in any one curve. The contours flatten as the level rises. At 20 phon, 20 Hz needs to be 69.6 dB louder than 1 kHz to match it. At 100 phon it needs only 28.4 dB. The mix does not change when you turn the volume down; your frequency response does, by forty decibels at the bottom end. That single fact explains the "loudness" button on a 1970s amplifier, the habit of checking a master quietly, and a good deal of the loudness war.

Where this reappears

The K-weighting filter at the heart of loudness normalisation — ITU-R BS.1770, and therefore EBU R 128, and therefore the target every streaming service normalises to — is a direct descendant of these curves, with a head-shadow shelf bolted on the front. Chapter 12 takes it apart. The reason level-matching is not pedantry but a precondition for any comparison at all is in the flattening described above: a louder version of the same file is a version with more apparent bass and treble, and it will win a casual A/B on that alone.

Two clocks

You hear a three-millisecond hole, but you need two hundred milliseconds to decide how loud something is.

The ear's temporal behaviour is not one number, and the gap between the two numbers it actually has is where lossy codecs live.

Ask a listener to detect a silent gap in a band of noise and the threshold comes out at roughly 2–3 ms for broadband noise at a moderate level — worse at low frequencies, worse near threshold, but of that order. Ask the same listener how loud a burst is and the answer keeps changing until the burst is about 200 ms long: detection threshold falls by roughly 3 dB for every doubling of duration from about 10 ms up to about 200 ms, and then stops. Two clocks, two orders of magnitude apart, in one organ.

Masking inherits the same asymmetry, and this is the part that matters for encoding. A loud sound raises the threshold for a *later* quiet sound for something like 100–200 ms — forward masking, and it decays smoothly. It raises the threshold for an *earlier* quiet sound too, which is stranger, but only briefly: backward masking is weak and short. The codec literature conventionally quotes about 20 ms; careful psychophysics on trained listeners gives 5 ms or less. Both numbers are in circulation and the difference between them is an argument about listener training, not about physiology.

Now put the two clocks together with a transform. An encoder cuts the signal into overlapping blocks and quantises each block's spectrum; the quantisation error it introduces is spread more or less evenly across the whole block in time. MP3's long window spans 1 152 samples — 26.12 ms at 44.1 kHz. Put a castanet strike near the end of such a block and the noise added to code it is smeared backwards across the preceding 26 milliseconds, into a stretch of near-silence where backward masking covers at most the last 5 to 20.

This is pre-echo, and this is why block switching exists

The result is a short burst of hiss audibly preceding the attack — the most recognisable of all lossy artefacts and the one people describe as "smearing" or "swishing" on applause and castanets. The fix, in every transform codec since, is to notice the transient and switch to short windows: MP3's short blocks are a third of the long window, about 8.7 ms, which brings the smear inside what temporal masking can hide. Chapter 04 shows the smear appearing as you lengthen the window; Chapter 07 shows the window-switching state machine; Chapter 11 provokes the artefact on demand so you can learn the sound of it.

Two ears, and phase

If location is carried by a handful of cues, it does not need two channels to transmit.

Lord Rayleigh's duplex theory of 1907 divides the job between two mechanisms. Below about 1.5 kHz, where a wavelength (23 cm at 1.5 kHz) is comparable to or larger than a head, the difference in arrival time between the ears is unambiguous and the auditory system uses it. Above about 2 kHz the head casts an acoustic shadow and the level difference takes over. In between, both are poor, which is why localisation is worst around 2–3 kHz.

The maximum interaural time difference follows from the geometry. Woodworth's spherical-head model gives the extra path length round a sphere of radius r:

ITD(θ)= rc (θ+sinθ) = 0.0875343 (π2+1) =656 µs at 90° azimuth; 261 µs at 30°. Measured values scatter over 0.6–0.7 ms, so the model is the right size rather than the right answer.

Add the precedence effect — Wallach, Newman and Rosenzweig described it in 1949, two years before the paper it is usually named after, by Haas — and the picture is of a system that resolves location from the first arrival and then ignores a great deal of what follows, over lags of a few milliseconds for clicks and up to tens of milliseconds for speech and music. Six hundred microseconds and a few decibels of level difference, per band, is close to the whole of it.

That is an enormous invitation to a codec, and every codec accepts it. Mid/side coding transmits sum and difference instead of left and right because the difference channel is usually small and quiet. Intensity stereo goes further and transmits one spectrum with a per-band panning coefficient above some crossover. Parametric stereo goes further still, sending a mono downmix plus a handful of interaural parameters per band per frame. Each of those is a bet that the listener's spatial percept is lower-bandwidth than the two waveforms carrying it — and the bet is usually good, which is why the failures are so recognisable when they come.

Phase is the same story told monaurally. Ohm's acoustic law — stated by Georg Simon Ohm in 1843 and endorsed by Helmholtz in 1863, over August Seebeck's objections — holds that perception depends on the amplitude spectrum and not on the phases. Manfred Schroeder showed in 1959 that this is not exactly true: a harmonic complex given quadratic phase has a flattened temporal envelope and audibly does not sound like the same complex in cosine phase. The correct statement is narrower and more useful. The ear is largely deaf to slowly varying phase within a critical band; it is not deaf to phase changes that alter the envelope within a band; and it is acutely sensitive to interaural phase below about 1.5 kHz. Codecs take precisely that licence. MDCT coefficients carry phase only as a sign and a quantised magnitude, noise filling invents phase outright, and spectral band replication synthesises the top octaves from the ones below with no phase relation to the original at all. It works — until it changes an envelope, and then it is audible.

Whose ear?

Six things that are repeated about hearing, and are not true.

The claims below are all common, all nearly right, and all wrong in ways that change what an encoder should do.

"The ear does a Fourier transform"
It does something weaker and much more useful: a bank of two to four dozen broad, overlapping, asymmetric, level-dependent bandpass filters, with compressive gain and no phase output. A 4 096-point FFT at 44.1 kHz has 10.8 Hz of resolution at every frequency. The ear has about 100 Hz at 100 Hz and about 3 500 Hz at 13.5 kHz, and its filters get wider as the input gets louder.
"Humans hear 20 Hz to 20 kHz"
That is a nominal range for a young, undamaged ear at a sufficient level. A 20 Hz tone needs about 78 dB SPL before it is audible at all, so the bottom of that range is not a frequency limit but a level limit. At the top, audibility of 20 kHz in adults is rare at any level.
"Bark and ERB are two names for the same scale"
They agree above about 500 Hz and diverge below it — 100 Hz against 30 Hz of bandwidth at 50 Hz. Fig 2.2 shows the divergence directly. It is an open empirical question about low-frequency selectivity, not a choice of units, and it is the reason different codecs draw their lowest band edges in different places.
"A-weighting models human hearing"
A-weighting is a fixed approximation of the 40-phon contour and nothing else. At 100 dB the real contour is far flatter, which is why an A-weighted reading understates the annoyance of loud low-frequency noise by tens of decibels. B and C exist because one curve cannot do the job; K-weighting exists because none of the three does it well enough for programme material.
"Phase is inaudible"
Within a band and slowly varying, yes. Change the phase enough to change the envelope within a band and it is plainly audible; change the interaural phase below 1.5 kHz and the image moves across your head. The useful version of the rule is a licence with conditions attached, and codecs violate the conditions only where they can afford to.
"Hearing loss just makes everything quieter"
Lose the outer hair cells and you lose the compressive amplifier with them. Soft sounds disappear while loud sounds stay exactly as loud as they were, so loudness grows abnormally fast above threshold — recruitment — and the usable dynamic range collapses from something like 120 dB towards something like 40 dB. This is why turning the volume up does not repair a sensorineural loss, and why a hearing aid has to compress rather than amplify.

Which brings up the awkward question that the rest of this study has to live with. Every curve on this page is a median. The 0-phon contour describes otologically normal listeners aged 18 to 25 — a demographic that includes almost none of the people arguing about bitrates. ISO 7029 tabulates how the threshold drifts with age, and the drift is not uniform: presbycusis takes the high frequencies first, and takes them by a lot. By 60, the median male's threshold at 8 kHz has risen on the order of twenty-odd decibels relative to a young reference.

What that does to a listening test

The top octave is exactly the region where lossy codecs are most aggressive: it is where band-limiting happens, where noise substitution and spectral band replication operate, and where the birdies live. So "can you hear the difference between 128k and 320k?" is not one question. It is one question per listener, and for a large fraction of the population the honest answer is no — not because the encoder is good, but because the receiver has already stopped reporting that part of the signal. This is why formal tests (ITU-R BS.1116, BS.1534) screen their listeners, level-match their stimuli and hide their references, and why Chapter 11 makes you run an ABX with the statistics done properly rather than asking you what you think you heard.

At the threshold of hearing near 3 kHz, the eardrum is moving by less than the diameter of a hydrogen atom, and 20 µPa is about five parts in ten thousand million of atmospheric pressure. The organ that does this is also the one whose limitations make a 96 kbit/s stream sound like a compact disc. Both facts are the same fact

Everything needed for a psychoacoustic model is now on the table: a place map, a filter bank with measured widths, a threshold in quiet, a loudness scale, and the two temporal constants. What is missing is the one mechanism that does the actual work of compression — what happens to the threshold in a band when something loud is already playing in the band next door. That is masking, it is worth tens of decibels rather than the single-digit savings everything in this chapter would buy on its own, and Chapter 03 spends its whole length on it. The spreading function it derives is the asymmetric travelling-wave envelope of Fig 2.1, turned into arithmetic; the band edges it uses are the twenty-four rows of the table above; and the floor it starts from is the curve in Fig 2.3. The ear has been measured. Next it gets modelled.
