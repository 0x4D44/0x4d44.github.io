Refuse to lose anything and the file still halves — but it will not halve twice.

A lossless coder has no psychoacoustic model, no masking threshold and nothing to throw away. It has one move: guess the next sample from the ones before it, and pay only for the difference. That move is worth about a factor of two on music and almost nothing on a bad recording, and the reason is arithmetic rather than engineering. At the opposite end of the same dial sits the other refusal — coders that give up on the waveform entirely and transmit a description of a mouth.

The shape of the problem

Every lossless audio coder ever shipped is the same four stages.

Cut the stream into blocks of a few thousand samples. Decorrelate the channels, because the left and right of a stereo mix are usually near-copies of each other. Run a predictor over each channel and keep only the error. Entropy-code that error. Decoding is the same four stages run backwards, and because every step is exact integer arithmetic the samples that come out are the samples that went in — not approximately, not perceptually, but bit for bit.

That constraint is what makes the design so different from the nine chapters before it. A lossy encoder spends its intelligence deciding what to discard; the quantiser is where the bits are won and the entropy coder merely tidies up afterwards. A lossless encoder discards nothing, so it has no quantiser at all. Its only weapon is the predictor, and the predictor's only job is to make the numbers it hands the entropy coder smaller — not fewer, smaller. A sixteen-bit sample costs sixteen bits. The difference between two adjacent sixteen-bit samples of a cello note costs about nine.

There is one further asymmetry worth stating up front, because it governs the whole family. The specification is a decoder specification. RFC 9639 defines exactly how a FLAC frame is to be unpacked; it says almost nothing about how an encoder should choose its predictor, its block length or its partitioning. Two encoders can produce completely different files from the same input and both be correct, and both files decode to the identical PCM. Every compression-level knob you have ever turned on a lossless encoder is a search-effort knob. It changes the file size and the encoding time. It cannot change the audio.

Why zip does not work

Chapter 06 made the point in passing and it is worth making again here: a general-purpose compressor looks for repeated byte patterns, and a waveform has almost none. The redundancy in audio is numerical — sample n is close to sample n−1 — and a dictionary coder cannot see that at all, because "close to" is not "equal to". Modelling first, then coding, is the entire trick. Run a PCM file through gzip and you will claw back a few per cent; run it through FLAC and you will halve it, using an entropy coder that is weaker than gzip's.

Four subtractions

Most of the compression is available from five predictors that have no parameters at all.

Tony Robinson's Shorten, written at Cambridge in 1993 and described in a 1994 report on simple lossless and near-lossless waveform compression, established the template the whole field still uses. Its cheapest predictors are polynomial: fit a polynomial of degree p−1 through the last p samples, extrapolate it one step, and take the difference. For p = 1 that is "the next sample will be the same as this one". For p = 2 it is "the signal will carry on at its current slope". The arithmetic is a handful of adds, there are no coefficients to transmit, and the predictors are identical in every encoder and every decoder ever written.

e(p)[n]= x[n]−x̂(p)[n] , A(z)=(1 −z−1)p the order-p residual is the p-th finite difference: the coefficients are the binomial coefficients, with alternating signs
x̂(0)=0 ; x̂(1)=x[n−1] ; x̂(2)=2x[n−1] −x[n−2] ; x̂(3)=3x[n−1] −3x[n−2] +x[n−3] ; x̂(4)=4x[n−1] −6x[n−2] +4x[n−3] −x[n−4] Shorten defined orders 0 to 3; FLAC added the fourth. A FIXED subframe spends three bits saying which of the five it used

Read A(z) = (1 − z−1)p as a filter and you can see immediately what it does to the spectrum and therefore what it costs. Each factor of (1 − z−1) is a first difference: a high-pass with a zero at DC and a response rising at 6 dB per octave. Order 2 rises at 12 dB per octave, order 4 at 24. On a signal whose energy is concentrated at the bottom — which is nearly all music — that tilt removes a great deal of amplitude. On a signal that is already bright, or noisy, it adds amplitude at the top faster than it removes it at the bottom, and the residual comes out larger than the input. That is why the order is chosen per block and why order 0, which is no prediction at all, has to remain on the menu.

The audible half of that figure is the part worth sitting with. The residual is not noise and it is not silence: it is the programme with its low end differentiated away, thin and hissy and perfectly recognisable. Nothing has been lost — add the prediction back, sample by sample, and the original returns exactly. All that has happened is that the information has been moved into numbers that are, on average, five or six binary orders of magnitude smaller than the ones it arrived in.

A filter per block

Fitting the predictor to the block buys another bit, and then charges you for it.

The fixed predictors assume the signal is locally a low-order polynomial. Real audio is locally a handful of resonances, so a filter matched to this block will beat any fixed one. That filter is found exactly as it is in speech coding: form the autocorrelation of the block, solve the normal equations with the Levinson–Durbin recursion, and take the resulting all-pole predictor. It is the same machinery Opus uses inside SILK and the same recursion described in Chapter 09; the only difference is what happens to the residual afterwards, which here is kept rather than modelled. One refinement matters enough to be visible in the figure below: the autocorrelation is taken over an apodised copy of the block — FLAC's default is a Tukey window with a half-cosine taper — so that the fit is not distorted by the discontinuity at the block edges. Without it, a decaying note can fit so badly that a parameterless fixed predictor beats the fitted one outright.

e[n]=x[n] + ∑pi=1 aix[n−i] , G= σx2σe2 prediction gain: the factor by which the predictor shrinks the variance of what has to be coded

The coefficients cannot be sent as the real numbers Levinson–Durbin produces, because the decoder must reproduce the prediction exactly in integer arithmetic or the reconstruction drifts. FLAC therefore quantises them: four bits in the subframe carry the coefficient precision (one less than the real value, so up to 15 bits each), five bits carry a right-shift, and the prediction is formed as an integer dot product shifted down. The encoder is free to choose that precision block by block, which is one of the places Shorten's fixed-for-the-whole-file approach left value on the table.

The bill arrives immediately. An order-p predictor at 15-bit precision costs 15p bits of coefficients, plus p warm-up samples stored raw at the full sample depth, per block per channel. At order 32 and 16-bit audio that is 480 + 512 = 992 bits before a single residual has been coded. Spread over a 4 096-sample block it is a quarter of a bit per sample — cheap. Spread over a 256-sample block it is nearly four bits per sample, which is more than the prediction can possibly save. The optimum order is therefore not a property of the music; it is a property of the music and the block length, and it moves.

Two details in that figure repay attention. The first is how flat the gold curve is past its minimum: between order 8 and order 32 there is usually less than a tenth of a bit per sample in it — on the string phrase at a 4 096 block, order 8 costs 10.03 bits a sample and the nominal optimum at order 17 costs 9.93. Lossless encoders compete over that tenth of a bit, and pay for it with encoding time that scales with the number of candidate models they try. The second is that the blue curve — residual cost alone — keeps falling right to order 32 on tonal material and flattens almost at once on noisy material. The predictor can only exploit structure that exists. Where the signal is already white, no order helps, and the honest answer is a VERBATIM subframe that stores the samples unchanged. Try Applause: thirty-two fitted coefficients buy about a decibel of prediction gain, and the gold curve is flat from end to end.

Pricing the residual

Rice coding is a Huffman code you never have to transmit.

A prediction residual is not distributed like the symbol alphabets of Chapter 06. It is a two-sided, sharply peaked, roughly Laplacian distribution centred on zero, and its shape barely changes between one piece of music and the next — only its width does. That is an enormous simplification: instead of measuring a histogram, building a Huffman tree and sending the code lengths, you can pick a code from a one-parameter family that is near-optimal for that shape, and send the parameter.

Golomb's construction, specialised by Robert Rice to powers of two, does exactly that. Fold the signed residual to a non-negative integer, split it into a quotient and a remainder by 2k, write the quotient in unary and the remainder in k raw bits.

u= 2e (e ≥ 0) , −2e−1 (e < 0) ; len(u)= ⌊u/2k⌋ +1+k zig-zag folding, then a unary quotient, a stop bit and k raw bits — the whole codec, in one line

The entire decoder is: count zeros until a one, shift, read k bits, unfold. There is no table, no tree and no state. And the price it charges is close to the information-theoretic one, because a Laplacian residual with mean absolute value b has a differential entropy that is itself a logarithm of b.

H≈ log2(2eb) = log2b+2.44 bits , b=E|e| the Laplacian entropy, in bits per sample, when the residual is discretised to integers — the floor that Fig 10.3 measures the Rice code against

Halve the residual's mean magnitude and you save exactly one bit a sample. That is the governing relation of this entire chapter, and it is worth holding on to when the compression ratios start arriving: a lossless coder's whole achievement is measured in binary orders of magnitude of residual, and each one is one bit out of sixteen — six and a quarter per cent of the file.

The optimum k is close to the base-two logarithm of the mean folded magnitude, but no encoder bothers with the approximation: evaluating the exact cost for every candidate k is a handful of shifts per sample and is done by brute force. The parameter is then sent in four bits (or five, in the alternate residual-coding method), and because the best k drifts across a block as the music changes, FLAC splits the residual into 2order equal partitions and sends a parameter for each. A partition whose residual is so wild that Rice coding would lose is escaped: the parameter field goes to all ones and the partition is stored as fixed-width raw values instead.

FLAC, field by field

The format is small enough to hold in your head, which is why it won.

Josh Coalson began FLAC in 2000. The bitstream format was frozen with version 0.9 of the reference implementation on 31 March 2001, and version 1.0 followed on 20 July that year; the project joined the Xiph.Org Foundation in 2003, alongside Vorbis and later Opus. Files written by that 2001 encoder still decode correctly today, which over a quarter of a century is an unusually strong record for a media format. The IETF's CELLAR working group turned the existing documentation into a formal specification, published as RFC 9639 in December 2024 — a description of what had been deployed for two decades rather than a revision of it.

A file is a four-byte magic number, fLaC, then a chain of metadata blocks, then frames until the stream ends. The first metadata block is always STREAMINFO, and it is 34 bytes of bit-packed fields.

CONSTANT stores one sample value and nothing else, which is what digital silence costs: a minute of it is a few hundred bytes. VERBATIM stores the block unchanged and is the guaranteed worst case — it is the reason a FLAC file can never be more than a fraction of a per cent larger than the PCM it came from, whatever you feed it. FIXED and LPC are the two predictors of the last two sections. A real encoder tries several and keeps whichever produced the smallest subframe, which is why the compression presets are search-breadth settings: the default -5 tries a modest set of predictors at a 4 096 block size, -8 tries more of them with more windowing functions, and -0 restricts itself to the fixed predictors at a 1 152 block, running several times faster for files roughly five percentage points larger.

The stereo decorrelation happens before any of this, per frame, and the encoder may change its mind every frame. Four assignments are available: independent left and right; left/side, sending L and L−R; right/side, sending R and L−R; and mid/side, sending (L+R)>>1 and L−R. The side channel needs one extra bit of depth, because a difference of two n-bit numbers is an (n+1)-bit number — and the encoder takes that deal whenever the side channel is quiet enough to pay for it, which on most commercial mixes is nearly always.

The MD5 is the point

The 128 bits at the end of STREAMINFO are an MD5 of the raw interleaved PCM, written by the encoder. Running flac -t decodes the entire file and compares. That is a verifiable claim of bit-exactness, which is precisely the thing no lossy format can offer and precisely the reason archives use this one. MD5 is long broken as a cryptographic hash; as an accidental-corruption check over a file you already possess, it is entirely adequate, and RFC 9639 documents it as such.

Why it stops at half

The compressed size tracks the noise floor, not the music.

Published figures for FLAC on ordinary commercial music cluster between roughly 50 and 70 per cent of the source PCM, with the mid-50s to low-60s typical; one public benchmark puts FLAC at about 61 per cent and WavPack at about 61 per cent on the same material. Solo piano and chamber recordings do better, sometimes dramatically; loud, limited, heavily saturated modern masters do worse and can approach 80 per cent. The spread is large, but the ceiling is remarkably consistent, and no amount of encoder cleverness has moved it in twenty-five years.

The reason follows directly from the Laplacian entropy above. Combine it with the definition of prediction gain and the relation collapses to something you can do in your head.

Δbits= log2 σxσe = GdB6.02 every 6.02 dB of prediction gain is exactly one bit per sample saved — the same constant as the quantiser of Chapter 01, arriving from the other direction

Sixteen-bit audio gives you sixteen bits to save. Halving the file means finding eight bits, which means 48 dB of prediction gain. Music does not contain 48 dB of prediction gain, because underneath it sits a noise floor — the microphone's self-noise, the room, the preamplifier, the dither applied at the final mastering stage — and a noise floor is by construction unpredictable. Whatever the predictor achieves above that floor, the floor itself must be coded at its own entropy, sample for sample, for ever. A recording with a −90 dBFS noise floor has, very roughly, about a bit and a half of genuinely random content per sample that no model can touch. A recording with a −60 dBFS floor has six.

Two consequences fall out of that slope, and both are counter-intuitive enough to be worth stating plainly. The first is that a 24-bit master usually compresses worse in percentage terms than the 16-bit version of the same recording. The extra eight bits are almost entirely noise — that is what the extra headroom is for — so they arrive at the entropy coder incompressible, and the ratio moves towards 1 : 1 as the depth grows. A 24/96 album is not merely three times the size of its CD counterpart before compression; it is rather more than three times afterwards.

The second is that the practical ceiling is set by the recording, not the codec. Monkey's Audio at its slowest settings will beat FLAC by around five per cent on typical material, and OptimFROG is in the same territory; both pay for it with decode complexity that rules them out of battery-powered playback. Five per cent is a real number and a real engineering achievement. It is also, against the 40-odd per cent that prediction already bought, a rounding error — and it is what is left after the entropy of the residual has taken its cut.

The rest of the family

Everyone arrived at the same architecture; they differ in what they will spend.

Apple's ALAC is structurally so close to FLAC that a description of one nearly describes the other: blocking, stereo decorrelation with a weight and a shift, an adaptive FIR predictor, Rice coding of the residual. It was reverse-engineered by David Hammerton and Cody Brocious in 2005 and then open-sourced by Apple under the Apache licence in October 2011, after which the argument between the two formats became entirely one of ecosystem. WavPack takes a different view of the same problem and adds a genuinely distinct mode: a hybrid, where a small lossy .wv file is playable on its own and an optional .wvc correction file restores exact losslessness, with an overhead typically under one per cent against a pure lossless encode. MPEG-4 SLS, OptimFROG DualStream and DTS-HD Master Audio all use the same layered idea from different directions.

What lossless is actually for

Three jobs, and only three. Archiving, because you cannot know what codec you will want in 2050 and re-encoding from a lossy source compounds the damage. Mastering and editing, because every generation of a lossy round trip moves the spectrum and every cut lands on a different block boundary. Transcoding, because a lossless master can be turned into any lossy format at any rate without the generation loss described in Chapter 11. Listening is not on the list, and the listening tests in Chapter 12 explain why.

The other end of the dial

Below about four kilobits a second, nobody is sending a waveform any more.

Turn the dial the other way and the sequence of surrenders is instructive. The first thing to go is uniform quantisation. G.711, standardised in 1972 and still the interoperability floor of the world's telephone network, samples at 8 kHz and spends eight bits a sample — 64 kbit/s — but spends them logarithmically. Two curves are defined: μ-law in North America and Japan, A-law almost everywhere else.

F(x)= sgn(x) ln(1+μ|x|)ln(1+μ) , μ=255 μ-law companding; A-law uses a linear segment below |x| = 1/A and a logarithm above it, with A = 87.6

The point of a logarithmic curve is that the quantisation step grows in proportion to the signal, so the signal-to-noise ratio stays roughly constant instead of collapsing as the talker gets quieter. Uniform eight-bit coding manages a theoretical 49.9 dB on a full-scale sine and loses a decibel for every decibel the input drops; μ-law tops out near 38 dB and is still delivering about 30 dB forty decibels below full scale, where the uniform coder has collapsed to about five. That is roughly four extra bits of usable dynamic range for nothing, which in 1972 was the difference between a workable trunk line and an unworkable one.

After companding, the next surrender is sending sample values at all. ADPCM sends the difference between the sample and a prediction, with a step size that adapts to recent history — the same predict-and-code architecture as the first half of this chapter, but with the residual quantised rather than kept. IMA/DVI ADPCM spends four bits a sample, a straight 4 : 1 on 16-bit input, using an 89-entry step-size table and an index adjustment of {−1, −1, −1, −1, 2, 4, 6, 8} that backs off gently and attacks quickly. ITU-T G.726 does the same job at 40, 32, 24 and 16 kbit/s, and G.722 splits the band into two halves with a quadrature mirror filter pair and runs an ADPCM coder in each, delivering 7 kHz audio within the same 64 kbit/s a plain telephone channel already occupied.

Below that, the waveform itself goes. G.729 codes 8 kbit/s with conjugate-structure algebraic code-excited linear prediction: 10 ms frames of 80 bits each, a 5 ms look-ahead, and therefore 15 ms of algorithmic delay. What it transmits is a short-term LPC filter, a pitch lag, and an index into an algebraic codebook of sparse excitation vectors — not the signal, but the settings of a machine that will make a signal like it. AMR generalised this into the mode sets that GSM and 3G actually used: eight narrowband rates from 4.75 to 12.2 kbit/s, and nine wideband rates from 6.6 to 23.85 kbit/s in AMR-WB, switchable frame by frame as the radio channel deteriorates.

At the bottom of that table the ratios stop being interesting and the change of kind becomes the point. Codec 2 at 700 bit/s is compressing CD stereo by a factor of about two thousand, but it is not compressing it — it is discarding the recording entirely and transmitting a parametric description of a vocal tract, from which the decoder synthesises something new. The speaker is recognisable, the words are intelligible, and no sample in the output was ever in the input. MELPe at 600 bit/s does the same job under a military specification. Google's Lyra v2 does it with a learned encoder and a residual vector quantiser at 3.2 kbit/s, and reports quality comparable to Opus at around 10 — which is the honest measure of what a learned model buys at the very bottom of the dial, and is picked up again in Chapter 12.

What people get wrong

Six corrections, in rough order of how often they are needed.

"FLAC sounds better than WAV"
It decodes to the same integers. That is not a claim of taste, it is a checkable property: the MD5 in STREAMINFO is computed over the unencoded PCM, and flac -t will tell you whether the file you have reproduces it. If a FLAC and a WAV of the same master sound different on your system, something downstream of the decoder differs — a resampler, a volume setting, a DSP chain — and that is worth finding.
"Compression level 8 is higher quality than level 0"
Every level produces a file that decodes to the identical samples. The levels are search-effort settings, controlling the maximum LPC order, the block size, the windowing functions tried and whether the encoder exhaustively evaluates candidate models. Level 8 is smaller and slower to encode; decode speed barely differs. The spread between -0 and -8 on typical material is a few per cent of file size.
"Lossless means two files of the same album are identical"
It means the audio is identical. Two FLACs of the same CD rip will differ in bytes if they were made by different encoder versions, at different presets, with different tags or different padding. Comparing them requires decoding both and comparing the PCM — which is exactly what the stored MD5 lets you do without keeping the WAV.
"24-bit/192 kHz files compress better because there is more redundancy"
The opposite, and Fig 10.4 shows why. The extra depth is mostly noise and the extra bandwidth is mostly empty, but the noise is incompressible and must be coded at its own entropy. A 24-bit master typically compresses to a larger fraction of its source than the 16-bit version of the same recording does — the ratio falls towards 1 : 1 as you add bits of dither.
"You can losslessly shrink an MP3 by re-encoding it to FLAC"
You will usually make it three to five times larger. Decoding an MP3 produces a full-rate PCM stream whose residual entropy is no lower than real music's — the quantisation noise the encoder added is itself unpredictable, and the sharp band-limit does not help a time-domain predictor. FLAC of a decoded MP3 is bit-exact with respect to that decode, and carries none of the information the MP3 encoder discarded. The original is gone.
"Someone will invent a lossless codec twice as good"
Not without changing what is being compressed. The floor is the entropy of the prediction residual, and the residual is dominated by the recording's own noise. Twenty-five years of increasingly heavy adaptive filtering — Monkey's Audio, OptimFROG, TAK, and lately learned predictors — has bought something in the region of five to ten per cent over FLAC, at large multiples of the decode cost. That is the shape of a problem near its bound, not one awaiting a breakthrough.
Where this leaves us

Both ends are now measured. What remains is the middle, where the damage is audible.

The dial has been walked from one stop to the other. At the top, exactness costs a factor of two and cannot be made to cost much less, because the residual's entropy is a property of the recording rather than of the coder. At the bottom, a vocoder discards the recording completely and rebuilds something plausible from a few dozen parameters per frame. The nine chapters before this one live in between, and everything they do is a negotiation between those two refusals: keep the waveform and pay for it, or model it and accept what the model gets wrong.

What the model gets wrong is the subject of Chapter 11. Every mechanism argued about so far — the block length that smears a transient backwards, the band that is zeroed and then reappears a frame later, the ceiling above which nothing is coded at all, the side channel that has been collapsed to save bits — has a sound. Each has a name, a characteristic programme that provokes it, and a bitrate below which it becomes unmistakable. The next chapter provokes each one on demand from a clean source, and then asks you to prove, under proper statistics, that you can actually hear it.

Throwing Sound Away — an interactive study of audio compression. Every sound on these pages is synthesised in your browser; there is not one audio file in the repository. No external scripts, fonts or trackers.
