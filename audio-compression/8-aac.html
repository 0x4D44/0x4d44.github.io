MP3 had to stay compatible with 1990. AAC did not.

Every awkwardness in the previous chapter — the hybrid filterbank, the 576 lines, the granule, the side-information layout — exists because Layer III had to be a modification of Layer II. Advanced Audio Coding began from a blank page in 1994 and shipped in 1997, and almost every difference between it and MP3 is a decision that MP3 was not allowed to make. The interesting part is not the clean transform. It is the four tools that come after it, each of which stops coding the signal and starts describing it.

Starting again

The first thing AAC threw away was the polyphase filterbank.

MP3's analysis chain is two transforms stacked: a 32-band polyphase quadrature filterbank inherited wholesale from Layer I, then an MDCT of 18 (or 6) points inside each of those 32 bands. The arrangement works, and chapter 07 takes it apart, but it costs three things. The PQF's bands overlap, so a pure tone leaks into its neighbour and must be coded twice. The two stages together are more arithmetic than one big transform. And the resolution is fixed at 576 lines per granule — about 38 Hz per line at 44.1 kHz, which is coarse where the ear is finest.

AAC deletes the first stage. There is one transform: a modified discrete cosine transform over a 2048-sample window, hopping 1024 samples, giving 1024 spectral lines per frame. The window spans 46.4 ms at 44.1 kHz and 42.7 ms at 48 kHz; each line is about 21.5 Hz wide at 44.1 kHz, nearly twice MP3's resolution. When a transient arrives, the encoder switches to a sequence of eight short windows of 256 samples, producing eight sets of 128 lines — still 1024 numbers, but sampled eight times as often in time and eight times as coarsely in frequency. Two transition window shapes, LONG_START and LONG_STOP, carry the overlap across the change so that the aliasing still cancels.

The rest of the 1997 standard is a box of tools, and this is where the chapter's argument lives. MPEG-2 AAC defined three profiles — Main, Low Complexity and Scalable Sampling Rate — differing in which tools a decoder must implement. MPEG-4 recast them as numbered object types and kept adding. Sample rates run from 8 to 96 kHz and channel counts to 48, neither of which MP3 could reach. The contributors were Fraunhofer IIS, AT&T, Dolby, Sony and Nokia; the format was declared an international standard in April 1997, and MPEG's own verification testing reported quality described as indistinguishable under the ITU-R scale for five full-bandwidth channels at 320 kbit/s.

A numbering trap

The two-bit profile field in an ADTS frame header is the object type minus one: 01 means AOT 2, Low Complexity. Two bits can only name four object types, so ADTS has no way to say SBR. An HE-AAC stream in ADTS is therefore labelled as plain Low Complexity, and the SBR payload rides inside the raw data block as an extension element that an LC-only decoder is required to skip. That is not a bug — it is precisely the mechanism that makes HE-AAC backward compatible, and it is also why an old decoder plays an HE-AAC stream as a dull, band-limited version of itself rather than refusing it.

A lapped transform cannot use any window it likes. For the time-domain aliasing to cancel on overlap-add — the property chapter 04 demonstrates numerically — the window must satisfy the Princen–Bradley condition: the squares of the two halves that overlap must sum to one, everywhere.

w[n]2+ w[n+N]2 =1 , 0 ≤n<N the Princen–Bradley condition. The sine window satisfies it by a trigonometric identity; the Hann window, which is what an FFT usually wants, does not

That leaves a family, not a single answer, and AAC ships two members of it. The sine window is MP3's, defined directly as sin(π(n+½)/2N). The Kaiser–Bessel-derived window is built by cumulatively summing a Kaiser window of parameter α and taking the square root, which forces the condition to hold by construction; AAC uses α = 4 for the long window and α = 6 for the short ones.

wKBD[n]= √ ∑ni=0W[i]∑Mi=0W[i] ,  W[i]=I0 (πα √1−(2i/M−1)2) with M = N, mirrored into the second half; I0 is the modified Bessel function of the first kind

The choice is a real trade and the figure measures it rather than asserting it. Close to a spectral peak the sine window is sharper: one bin away it is about 2.6 dB further down than KBD at α = 4. Far from the peak the ordering reverses, and not slightly — eight bins out and beyond, KBD at α = 4 rejects about 28 dB more than the sine window does, and at α = 6 about 40 dB more. Eight bins is 188 Hz on a long block at 48 kHz, which is more than a critical band wide below about 500 Hz. Leakage that lands that far away lands in a band whose masking threshold has nothing to do with the tone that produced it, so the encoder must either code the leak or accept audible noise. The window shape is signalled per block by a single bit, and because only the overlapping halves must match, the left half of a block inherits the shape of the block before it — so the encoder can change its mind every 1024 samples without a discontinuity.

Linear prediction along the frequency axis is noise shaping along the time axis.

Temporal noise shaping is the cleverest thing in the standard and its name reads backwards until you see the duality. A transform coefficient describes a whole 46 ms window, so quantisation error added to a coefficient is spread evenly over those 46 ms. If the window contains a castanet strike there is nothing to mask that error during the silence before the attack, and you hear pre-echo — the artefact chapter 11 provokes on demand. Block switching answers this by shortening the window. TNS answers it without shortening anything.

Take the 1024 coefficients of one frame and treat them as a signal indexed by frequency. Fit a linear predictor to that sequence exactly as chapter 10 fits one to a waveform, and filter the coefficients with it. Quantise the residual instead of the coefficients. The decoder runs the inverse, all-pole filter to recover them.

r[k]=X[k]+ ∑pi=1 ai X[k−i] ⟶ Q(·) ⟶ X̂[k]=r̂[k]− ∑pi=1 ai X̂[k−i] analysis and synthesis run along k, the frequency index, not along time. The coefficients ai come from Levinson–Durbin on the autocorrelation of the coefficient sequence

Quantisation error is roughly white in whatever domain you quantise, so the error added to r is flat across frequency. The decoder then pushes it through 1/A(z), which multiplies it by a frequency-dependent gain. And because the axis is frequency, a gain that varies along frequency is a gain that varies along time within the block — the two are Fourier duals. The all-pole filter fitted to the spectrum is the temporal envelope of the block. Filter the noise with it and the noise acquires the block's own temporal envelope: loud where the castanet is, and tens of decibels quieter in the silence before it.

Block switching moves the noise by making the window shorter. Temporal noise shaping leaves the window alone and moves the noise inside it. Herre and Johnston, 1996

The prediction gain is the second dividend. A residual with 8 to 20 dB less energy than the coefficients it came from needs fewer bits to reach the same accuracy, so TNS pays for itself on exactly the material that needs it. The figure measures both effects on one block and then on a whole excerpt you can listen to.

The bitstream syntax is modest. A long window may carry up to three TNS filters covering different regions of the spectrum; a short window carries at most one. The order is capped at 12 for Low Complexity and 20 for Main and LTP, and at 7 inside a short window. Each filter carries a direction bit, because the filter may be run up or down the spectrum and the choice decides which end of the block the noise is pushed towards. Coefficients are sent as quantised reflection coefficients — three or four bits each — so a complete TNS filter is perhaps fifty bits, against the thousands that a short-block sequence costs in extra side information. TNS is particularly effective on speech, where the glottal pulse train produces a sharp temporal structure inside every block and block switching would fire constantly.

Below about 64 kbit/s the top octaves stop being coded and start being described.

Run the arithmetic of chapter 05 at 48 kbit/s for a stereo pair and the problem is plain. Half the spectral lines of a 44.1 kHz signal live above 11 kHz. Coding them even crudely — one bit a line, which is barely a sign — would cost more than 20 kbit/s, and they are the lines the ear cares least about individually. Every low-rate codec before 2003 dealt with this by lowpass filtering: it simply threw the top away, which is why a 64 kbit/s MP3 sounds as though a blanket has been thrown over it.

Spectral band replication, contributed by Coding Technologies and standardised into MPEG-4 in 2003, takes a different view. The high band is not independent of the low band: a cymbal's 12 kHz content and its 6 kHz content are the same physical event, and their fine structure is similar even though their levels are not. So do not code the high band. Copy it up from the low band — which the core decoder has already reconstructed, for free — and then send just enough side information to make its envelope right.

Xhi[k]= gb· Xlo[k0+((k−kc) mod W)] ,  gb= √Etarget,bEpatch,b a patch of width W taken from below the crossover kc and repeated upwards, then scaled band by band so its energy matches the envelope the encoder transmitted

That is the whole idea, and the payload is startlingly small: an energy per band per frame, delta-coded in frequency or in time, plus a noise floor and a handful of flags for added sinusoids. In the figure below the envelope data comes out at about three kilobits a second for eight bands, which is in the range published for real HE-AAC encoders. For that you get the top octave and a half of a stereo programme.

The standard's machinery is heavier than the arithmetic suggests, and for a good reason. SBR does not work on MDCT coefficients: it works in a 64-band complex-exponential quadrature mirror filterbank, which is oversampled rather than critically sampled, so transposing a patch upwards does not drag an aliasing image with it. The system is dual-rate: the AAC core runs at half the output sample rate and produces, say, a 24 kHz signal, and the SBR stage runs at the full 48 kHz and fills in the top. That is why an HE-AAC decoder has roughly twice the arithmetic of an LC one, and why a core-only decoder that ignores the SBR extension plays the stream at half rate and sounds dull rather than broken.

Where the honest boundary is

SBR is not free and it is not always a gain. The side information costs bits, the transposition invents fine structure that is wrong, and the dual-rate structure adds delay. Below about 48 kbit/s stereo the trade is overwhelming: HE-AAC at 32 kbit/s is listenable where AAC-LC at 32 kbit/s is not. Above roughly 80 to 96 kbit/s it inverts, because at that budget the encoder can afford to code the top octaves properly and a real one sounds better than a described one. Good encoders switch automatically on the target rate, which is why the same encoder configured at 64 and at 128 kbit/s may produce streams a decoder must treat quite differently.

If a band is noise, sending its noise is a waste of bits.

Perceptual noise substitution is the smallest of AAC's description tools and the easiest to state. Consider a scalefactor band whose content is noise — the wash of a cymbal, rain, applause, the turbulent part of a fricative. The ear reads such a band by its energy and its bandwidth. It does not, and cannot, track the individual coefficients: the auditory filter at 10 kHz is well over a kilohertz wide, so a hundred MDCT lines arrive inside one channel and are perceived as one rough texture. Sending those hundred numbers is spending bits on a detail no listener can hold.

So do not send them. Signal the band with Huffman codebook 13 — the reserved NOISE_HCB — transmit one value for its energy in place of a scalefactor, and let the decoder fill the band with its own pseudo-random numbers scaled to that energy. The cost falls from dozens or hundreds of coefficients to a single differentially coded number per band per frame.

X̂[k]= √Ebnb ·u[k] , k∈b with u a zero-mean unit-variance pseudo-random sequence generated in the decoder. Everything transmitted about the band is the single number Eb

The danger is obvious and the standard's answer is a gate. Substitute a band that contains a tone and you replace a pitch with a hiss, which is audible instantly and sounds like a fault rather than a compromise. An encoder therefore measures each band's tonality before deciding — the spectral flatness measure of chapter 03 is the usual instrument — and substitutes only where the band is already noise-like. The figure lets you set that threshold and get it wrong.

The bit figures in that readout deserve a caveat. They compare the cost of coding those bands at the fidelity the demonstration uses against the nine bits a substituted band costs per frame, and the ratio is large. A real encoder working to a tight budget would already have quantised most of those high lines to zero, so PNS's true saving is smaller than the comparison suggests. Its value is as much about what it prevents: without it, a rate loop starved of bits produces a high band that flickers on and off from frame to frame, which is the mechanism behind birdies. A band that is declared noise is at least stable.

Two channels from one

Stereo is the last thing a low-rate codec codes and the first thing it describes.

Two channels of music are rarely two independent signals. Most of the time they are one signal plus a difference, and the difference is small. AAC exploits that at three escalating levels of violence, and — this is the real advance over MP3 — it chooses between them per scalefactor band rather than once per granule.

Mid/side coding is lossless: send M = (L + R)/√2 and S = (L − R)/√2 instead of the two channels. When the channels are similar, S is near zero and costs almost nothing, so the pair is cheaper; when they are not, it is more expensive and the encoder sends L/R instead. One bit per band decides. Intensity stereo is not lossless: above some frequency the encoder sends only the summed channel plus a per-band direction, on the grounds that above about 2 kHz the ear localises by envelope rather than by waveform. It is signalled by using codebooks 14 and 15 on a band, and it is where the image narrowing and instability of low-rate stereo comes from. Parametric stereo, added in 2004, goes the whole way: transmit one mono downmix and describe the stereo image with four kinds of parameter — inter-channel intensity difference, coherence, phase difference and an overall phase difference that says how to divide the phase correction between the two ears. The decoder synthesises a decorrelated companion signal and mixes it in.

The music the decoder produces is, in a strict sense, largely not the music that was encoded. It is a reconstruction that is intended to be perceptually close, and at 24 kbit/s it is astonishingly close — but it will not survive an analysis that compares waveforms, which is one of several reasons why chapter 11 insists that signal-to-noise ratio is a worthless measure of a perceptual codec.

Seven bytes of header, and an argument about how much that costs.

AAC's raw output is a sequence of raw_data_blocks with no framing of their own, which is the right decision for a format that expects to live inside MP4. When it has to be streamed on its own — into a decoder that may join mid-stream and must resynchronise — it is wrapped in the Audio Data Transport Stream: a seven-byte header per frame, nine if a CRC is carried, beginning with a twelve-bit syncword of all ones exactly as MP3's does. The alternative wrapper, LATM/LOAS, separates the configuration from the payload so the configuration need not be repeated in every frame, and is what broadcast transport streams generally use.

The header is worth decoding field by field, because two of its decisions have consequences. The sample rate is a four-bit index into a fixed table of thirteen values, so an AAC stream cannot be at 22.05 kHz by accident — it is index 7 or it is not legal. And aac_frame_length is thirteen bits counted in bytes including the header, which caps a frame at 8 191 bytes: at 44.1 kHz and 1024 lines per frame that is a ceiling of about 2.75 Mbit/s, which no one has ever met.

Rheader= 56× fs1024 =56× 43.07= 2 412 bit/s at 44.1 kHz — 1.9% of a 128 kbit/s stream, but 7.5% of a 32 kbit/s one, which is why low-rate AAC is not carried in ADTS if anything else is available

One detail of the header is a direct inheritance from chapter 07: adts_buffer_fullness is the bit reservoir, alive and well. AAC keeps MP3's trick of letting a frame that needs more bits than its share borrow from the unspent budget of the frames before it, and the field tells the decoder how full the encoder's notional buffer was — which is what lets a decoder with a fixed input buffer know it will not underrun. Set it to 0x7FF and you have declared the stream variable-rate and the question moot.

Corrections

Five things that are repeated about AAC and are not true.

"AAC is Apple's format"
AAC was an ISO/IEC standard in April 1997, developed by Fraunhofer IIS, AT&T, Dolby, Sony and Nokia. Apple adopted it for the iTunes Music Store in 2003, six years later, and its visibility on iPods did more for the format's reputation than the standard ever did. The related confusion is that .m4a names AAC: it does not. It names an MP4 container, which may hold AAC-LC, HE-AAC, xHE-AAC or Apple Lossless, and the extension tells you nothing about which.
"AAC is MP3 with a better psychoacoustic model"
Neither standard contains a psychoacoustic model. Both standardise only the bitstream and the decoder; the model lives entirely in the encoder and is where every vendor's differentiation sits, which is why LAME at 192 kbit/s beats a 1996 MP3 encoder at 256. What AAC changed is the machinery the model gets to drive: one transform instead of two, 49 scalefactor bands instead of 21 on a long block, per-band stereo decisions, and the four description tools above — none of which MP3 can express at any bitrate.
"Temporal noise shaping shapes the noise across frequency"
It applies a filter along frequency in order to shape the noise in time. The confusion is understandable, because the filtering happens to a frequency-indexed array. The point is the duality: a gain that varies along the frequency index is, after the inverse transform, a gain that varies along time inside the block. Figure 8.2 measures the resulting temporal envelope rather than asserting it.
"HE-AAC is better than AAC-LC"
Only below roughly 80 kbit/s for stereo. SBR buys bandwidth by spending accuracy, so above the rate at which the core could have coded the top octaves honestly, adding SBR makes the result worse as well as more complex. A 256 kbit/s HE-AAC file is a contradiction in engineering terms. Encoders that pick the profile from the target rate are doing the right thing.
"SBR is just upsampling, or interpolation"
Upsampling invents nothing and adds no information; it produces a band-limited signal with a mathematically empty top end. SBR copies real coded content from the low band into the high band and then corrects its energy, its noise floor and its tonality with transmitted parameters. What it gets wrong is the fine structure — harmonic relationships that were true at 5 kHz are not true at 12 kHz — which is a different and more interesting kind of wrong, and is audible as shimmer rather than as dullness.
What AAC became

The second generation ended by admitting that speech and music needed the same decoder.

By 2008 the AAC family had a member for every constraint. AAC-LC for anything with bits to spare; HE-AAC and HE-AAC v2 for broadcast and for the bottom of a streaming ladder; AAC-LD and AAC-ELD for conversation, at about 20 and 15 milliseconds of algorithmic delay against AAC-LC's roughly 130. What none of them did well was speech at very low rates, because a transform coder is the wrong instrument for a glottal pulse train and a vocal tract — the right instrument is linear prediction with an excitation codebook, which is what the telephony world had been using since G.729.

Unified Speech and Audio Coding, ISO/IEC 23003-3, standardised in 2012 and shipped as xHE-AAC, resolves this by carrying both. A frame is coded either by a transform path descended from AAC or by an ACELP speech path, and the encoder switches between them according to what the signal is, inside one bitstream that one decoder handles. Enhanced SBR and an MPEG Surround stage sit in front of both. It is useful from about 12 kbit/s and remains so up through the range where AAC-LC lives, and it ships with MPEG-D dynamic range control metadata, which is how a streaming service delivers one encode that plays sensibly both in a quiet room and on a bus. Netflix and several other services use it at the bottom of their ladders.

That is also, precisely, the design Opus arrived at by a different road and two years earlier — a linear-predictive speech coder and a transform music coder in one packet format, with the switch made by the encoder. Chapter 09 takes that apart: the SILK half and its line spectral frequencies, the CELT half and its pyramid vector quantiser, the range coder underneath both, and the single table-driven byte at the front of every packet that tells the decoder which of thirty-two configurations it is about to run. The difference is not really technical. AAC arrived at unification after fifteen years of adding tools to a 1997 standard; Opus was designed as two codecs from the first commit, and gave the result away.
