Every bit a codec saves is bought from a threshold the music itself pushed up.

Chapter 02 ended with a floor: the quietest sound you can detect in an empty room. Music is not an empty room. Play a 1 kHz tone at 70 dB SPL and the floor 200 Hz above it rises from 2.7 to 35.5 dB SPL — thirty-three decibels of new deafness, arriving free with the note. Lossy audio coding is the exploitation of that one fact, and very nearly nothing else.

The floor that moves

The absolute threshold is a special case: it is what the masked threshold becomes when the masker is silence.

Robert Wegel and Charles Lane published the first systematic measurement in 1924: play one pure tone, then find how loud a second tone must be before the listener can hear it at all. The curve that comes out — detection threshold against probe frequency, for one fixed masker — is a masking pattern, and it dominates the loud tone's neighbourhood by tens of decibels while leaving the far ends of the spectrum untouched. Harvey Fletcher read those patterns as evidence of a bank of overlapping filters and, in 1940, named the width of one of them the critical band. The whole of perceptual audio coding is downstream of those two papers.

Four words, used precisely throughout this chapter. The masker is the loud thing that is already playing. The probe (or maskee) is the quiet thing whose audibility is in question — in a codec it is never music, it is always quantisation noise. The masked threshold is the level the probe must reach to be detected in the masker's presence. And the signal-to-mask ratio is the gap between a component's own level and the threshold at its frequency:

SMR=Lsignal −LTg both in dB SPL. Positive means the component sticks out of its own shadow; negative means the signal is inaudible where it stands, and a coder may spend nothing on it at all

SMR is the only number a bit allocator really wants, and the rest of this chapter is about computing it. Start by looking at one. In figure 3.1 the gold curve is the masked threshold produced by a single masker, evaluated live from the spreading function of §3 and power-summed with the threshold in quiet from chapter 02. The probe sits at 1.2 kHz and 30 dB SPL: twenty-seven decibels clear of the absolute threshold, and inaudible anyway.

What "masked" means, operationally

Not "less noticeable". Every number in this chapter comes from a detection experiment — typically a forced choice between intervals, one of which contains the probe — and the threshold is the level at which the listener is right some fixed fraction of the time. A probe below its masked threshold is not quiet or unobtrusive; it is undetectable under test, which is a much stronger claim and the only one strong enough to build a codec on. It is also why every honest statement about codec quality ends in a listening test rather than a spectrum plot: see chapter 11.

Why it leans upward

The shape of the shadow is the shape of the travelling wave, turned into decibels.

Masking is not a quirk of attention or a failure of concentration. It is mechanical. A 1 kHz tone does not excite a point on the basilar membrane; it excites a patch. Take Zwicker and Terhardt's critical bandwidth

CB(f)=25 +75(1 +1.4fk2)0.69 Hz, with fk the frequency in kHz: 104 Hz at 250 Hz, 162 Hz at 1 kHz, 685 Hz at 4 kHz

— and run its edges through Greenwood's place map from chapter 02, and 162 Hz at 1 kHz comes out as 1.03 mm of membrane out of 35. Anything whose own patch overlaps that millimetre is competing for a structure that is already moving, and it loses.

The patch is not symmetrical. The travelling wave builds gradually from the base, peaks, and then dies within a fraction of a millimetre — a gentle basal skirt and an abrupt apical cutoff. Since the base is the high-frequency end, a masker's excitation reaches a long way up in frequency and hardly at all down. That is the upward spread of masking, and it is the single most consequential asymmetry in audio coding: a bass note buys you cover across the midrange, and a cymbal buys you nothing below it.

Worse — better, for a codec — the upward spread grows with level while the downward spread does not. Zwicker's two-slope description makes the asymmetry explicit. Below the masker the pattern falls at a fixed 27 dB per Bark. Above it, the slope depends on both the masker's frequency and its level:

Slower=+27 dB/Bark
Supper=−24 − 230fm +0.2Lm dB/Bark fm in Hz, Lm in dB SPL. At a 1 kHz masker the frequency term is 0.23 dB/Bark and at 4 kHz it is 0.06 — negligible. The level term is not: −20.2 dB/Bark at 20 dB SPL, −12.2 at 60, −4.2 at 100

Read that last line again, because it is what makes loud music cheap to encode. A quiet masker casts a shadow that falls away at twenty decibels per Bark; a very loud one casts a shadow that is nearly flat for several Bark. Double the level of the orchestra and you have not merely raised the threshold under the instruments, you have widened it. The usual mechanical account is cochlear compression: the response to a quiet probe grows compressively with level — measured exponents cluster around 0.2 to 0.3 dB per dB — while the masker's upward-spreading tail grows linearly, so the probe loses ground steadily as the masker gets louder.

One distinction worth keeping straight, because the two curves are drawn on the same axes and mean opposite things. A masking pattern fixes the masker and sweeps the probe: it is what figure 3.1 plots, and it is what a codec needs. A psychophysical tuning curve fixes the probe at a few decibels above its own threshold and asks how loud a masker at each frequency must be to just cover it: that one is a picture of an auditory filter, and it leans the other way. Mistaking one for the other flips the asymmetry, and the mistake is common enough in secondary sources to be worth naming.

The masking threshold is not a property of the music. It is a property of a model of you, evaluated on the music — and the models in shipping encoders disagree with each other by more than fifteen decibels at the masker's own frequency. Why two encoders at the same bitrate do not sound the same
Four curves, one phenomenon

Every codec draws the spreading function differently, and all of them draw it too narrow.

A spreading function is the masking pattern reduced to its shape: how many decibels below the masker the threshold sits, as a function of distance along the Bark axis, with the masker's own level factored out. Four of them are in circulation, and figure 3.2 draws all four at once.

The most-cited is Schroeder's, from Schroeder, Atal and Hall's 1979 paper on hiding quantisation noise in speech coders — the paper that arguably started perceptual coding, eleven years before there was an MPEG audio committee to start:

SF(Δz)=15.81 +7.5(Δz+0.474) −17.5√1+(Δz+0.474)2 dB one line, no level dependence. Its maximum is −0.0016 dB at Δz = 0.0003 — the 0.474 offset exists precisely to put the peak on the masker — and it is 3 dB down at −0.62 and +0.80 Bark, 10 dB down at −1.13 and +1.73. Asymptotically it rises at 25 dB/Bark below and falls at 10 dB/Bark above

Its virtue is that it is one line of arithmetic and its derivative is continuous, so convolving a whole spectrum with it is cheap and well behaved. Its vice is the missing level dependence: it draws the same curve for a 20 dB masker and a 100 dB one, when the measurements say the upper slope should flatten by sixteen decibels per Bark across that range. Encoders that use it compensate by being pessimistic elsewhere.

MPEG-1's psychoacoustic model 1 takes the opposite approach: four straight segments, a kink at every join, and the masker's sound pressure level X written into two of the four slopes. It is defined only for −3 ≤ Δz < 8 and contributes nothing outside that span.

SF(Δz)=17Δz −0.4X+11 ,  −3≤Δz<−1
SF(Δz)=(0.4X+6)Δz ,  −1≤Δz<0
SF(Δz)=−17Δz ,  0≤Δz<1
SF(Δz)=(0.15X−17)Δz −0.15X ,  1≤Δz<8 continuous at every knot: both branches give −0.4X − 6 at Δz = −1, zero at Δz = 0 and −17 at Δz = +1. The far-upper slope 0.15X − 17 runs from −14 dB/Bark at X = 20 dB to −2 dB/Bark at X = 100 — the level dependence of §2, built straight into the constants

The fourth curve is what an encoder people actually use does. LAME pre-warps the Bark distance before applying Schroeder's formula — by a factor of three above the masker and one and a half below — and then subtracts a notch of up to 8 dB just above it:

t=3Δz (Δz≥0) ,  t=1.5Δz (Δz<0)

Narrow is safe, wide is cheap

The two failure modes are not symmetrical, which is why the published curves and the shipped ones differ so much. A spreading function that is too wide claims masking that is not there, and the encoder puts audible noise into a band it believed was covered — a defect the listener hears immediately and blames on the format. One that is too narrow simply spends bits it did not have to spend, and the listener hears a slightly larger file. Faced with that asymmetry every encoder author has narrowed the curve, LAME most of all. Its effective slopes, three times steeper than Schroeder's, are not a claim about hearing. They are a safety margin with a formula wrapped round it.

The twenty-decibel question

Noise hides a tone almost perfectly; a tone hides noise badly.

Everything so far has treated a masker as a level at a frequency. It is not enough, and the gap is worth about twenty decibels — more than the difference between any two of the spreading functions in §3.

The textbook pair of measurements: a band of noise one critical band wide at 80 dB SPL will just mask a 410 Hz tone at about 76 dB, a signal-to-mask ratio of roughly 4 dB. A 1 kHz tone at 80 dB SPL will just mask critical-band noise at about 56 dB — an SMR of roughly 24 dB. Same bandwidth, same level, same ear, and twenty decibels of difference depending on which of the two is doing the masking. The usual account is that a tone produces a steady, phase-locked excitation against which the probe's own fluctuation stands out, while noise produces exactly the sort of fluctuating envelope that the probe is trying to hide in.

The coding literature names the two cases TMN (tone masking noise) and NMT (noise masking tone), and every psychoacoustic model carries a table of offsets that interpolates between them. MPEG-1's model 1 folds the offset into a per-masker masking index av, negative and slowly frequency-dependent; model 2 computes a tonality index tb per partition and interpolates directly:

avtonal=−1.525 −0.275z −4.5 ,   avnoise=−1.525 −0.175z −0.5
O(z)=tb·TMN(z) +(1−tb)·NMT ,   TMN=max(15.5+z,24.5) ,  NMT=5.5 the textbooks give the tonal branch as 14.5 + z instead, with no floor; at 1 kHz that is 23.0 dB against the reference code's 24.5, and the measured value is about 24. Both are in print, both are defensible, and the difference is 1.5 dB of bitrate

Which leaves the real question: how does an encoder decide, thirty-eight times a second and without a human in the loop, whether what it is looking at is a tone? Two answers ship. Johnston's is the spectral flatness measure, the ratio of the geometric to the arithmetic mean of the power spectrum, in decibels:

SFM=10 log10 (∏Pi)1/N1N∑Pi ,   α=min(SFM/−60,1) 0 dB means perfectly flat, so α = 0 and the model treats the band as noise; −60 dB or below gives α = 1 and full tonal treatment. Everything in between is a linear blend, which is a convenience rather than a measurement

The second is MPEG model 2's unpredictability measure, and it is the more elegant of the two. The practical consequence is that the tonal parts of a signal — a sustained violin, an organ pedal, a synthesiser pad — are expensive at high frequencies and the noisy parts are cheap everywhere. Cymbals and applause are, band for band, the easiest material in music to code, and they are also the material everyone points at when a codec fails. Those two facts are not in tension: the failure is temporal, and §7 is about that.

Assembling the threshold

Between a spectrum and a bit allocation there are seven steps and no magic.

The complete psychoacoustic model, of the kind that shipped inside every MP3 encoder, is a short pipeline. Nothing in it is deeper than the preceding four sections; the work is in the bookkeeping.

1 · transform
A windowed FFT of the current block — 1 024 points in MPEG's model 2 — giving magnitude and phase per bin. The codec's own transform is a different one, for reasons chapter 04 goes into.
2 · group
Sum the bin powers into partitions on the Bark axis. Below about 500 Hz a critical band is narrower than the FFT's resolution, so the partitions there are single bins and the grouping is nominal.
3 · classify
Per partition, a tonality index from spectral flatness or from unpredictability, and from it the offset O(z) of §4.
4 · spread
Convolve the partition energies along the Bark axis with the spreading function of §3. This is the step that takes a spectrum and turns it into an excitation.
5 · offset
Subtract the tonality-dependent offset. Five and a half decibels if the band is noise, two dozen if it is a tone.
6 · floor
Power-sum with the threshold in quiet, so that no band is ever asked to code below what the ear cannot hear anyway.
7 · remember
Clamp against the previous block's threshold, which is forward masking implemented as a memory. §7.

Step 6 is the one that gets written down wrongly most often. Thresholds add as powers, not as decibels:

LTg=10 log10 (10Tq/10 +∑j 10LTj/10) dB SPL two equally strong maskers therefore raise the threshold by exactly 10 log102 = 3.01 dB, and a hundred of them by 20 dB

That linear power sum is the model's most conservative assumption and it is known to be wrong in the safe direction. Combine two maskers in a real experiment and the measured threshold usually rises by more than 3 dB — "excess masking", large for non-simultaneous maskers, and read as evidence of the same cochlear compression that produced the level-dependent upper slope in §2, with exponents typically estimated between 0.2 and 0.35. The models ignore it. An encoder that assumed the extra masking was there would be claiming cover it might not get.

There is one trap in step 4 that every implementer meets. Having convolved the spectrum with the spreading function, it is tempting to deconvolve afterwards — the spreading has raised the whole curve, and undoing it would recover a per-band threshold that was not inflated by its neighbours. Do not. The convolution is ill-conditioned: inverting it amplifies small errors without limit and routinely returns negative thresholds, which are meaningless. Shipping implementations renormalise instead, dividing out the spreading function's own gain, and accept that the result is approximate.

A unit sleight of hand that every model performs

Look closely at step 6 and it mixes two quantities that are not the same kind of thing: the total energy summed across a critical band, and the threshold in quiet for a pure tone at that band's centre. A band's energy depends on its width; a tone's threshold does not. Every shipping psychoacoustic model does this anyway, and absorbs the discrepancy into the offsets of §4 — which is part of why those offsets were tuned by listening rather than derived. It is worth knowing that the bookkeeping is approximate before you spend a week trying to make two implementations agree to a decibel.

What a decibel buys

Masking is money, and the exchange rate is 6.02 decibels to the bit.

Three ratios, constantly confused, all measured in decibels and all defined against the same global threshold LTg:

SMR=Lsignal−LTg signal-to-mask: how far the programme sticks out of its own shadow. It sets the demand for bits. NMR=Lnoise−LTg noise-to-mask: the one that decides audibility. Negative is inaudible; positive is a defect. MNR=−NMR=SMR−SNR mask-to-noise: the headroom left over. A rate loop's job is to keep it just barely positive in every band.

The conversion into bits is the uniform quantiser's signal-to-noise ratio, derived in chapter 01: each extra bit halves the quantisation step and so buys 6.02 dB.

SNR≈6.02b +1.76 dB ⇒ b≈⌈SMR6.02⌉ a band with 24 dB of SMR needs four bits per line; a band whose signal sits below its own masked threshold needs none at all, and gets a run of zeros that chapter 06 codes for almost nothing

Integrate that demand over the whole spectrum and you get James Johnston's perceptual entropy, proposed in 1988: the number of bits per sample needed to keep the quantisation noise in every band exactly at its masked threshold, and therefore a floor under what any transparent coder can achieve under that model.

bi= log2(2⌊√6ei/Ti⌋+1) ,   PE= ∑nibiN ei is the energy in band i, Ti its threshold and ni the number of spectral lines in it. The √(6e/T) is simply the quantiser step that places the error power Δ²/12 exactly on the threshold

Johnston's measurements put a wide range of CD-quality material at about 2.1 bits per sample. Multiply by 44 100 and that is 92.6 kbit/s for one channel, against 705.6 kbit/s of linear PCM: a factor of 7.6, before entropy coding has removed anything and before a stereo pair has been exploited. The figure above, running a cruder model on a synthetic phrase, lands at 2.5 bits a sample — the same neighbourhood by a different route. The familiar claim that 128 kbit/s stereo is transparent is not marketing. It is this integral, plus Huffman coding, plus the fact that two channels of music are not two independent signals.

Three honest qualifications. Perceptual entropy is a lower bound under one particular masking model, not a theorem about hearing: change the spreading function to LAME's and the number moves. It is an average over programme, and the spread is enormous — the speech phrase in figure 3.4 costs a third of what the music costs, and a solo instrument a fraction again. And it says nothing about the bits a real bitstream spends on side information: scalefactors, window switches, Huffman table selectors and a frame header, which at low rates can be a fifth of the budget. Chapter 05 spends those bits properly.

The ratio that matters at the other end of the pipeline is NMR. A coder is doing its job when NMR ≤ 0 dB in every band of every block, and the objective quality measures standardised as ITU-R BS.1387 — the PEAQ family — are built on exactly that comparison, computed in an ear model rather than on the encoder's own assumptions. Chapter 11 takes them apart, along with the reasons a good NMR and a good listening-test score are not the same thing.

Masking in time

The model is generous in frequency and simply wrong in time, in one direction.

Masking does not stop when the masker does. Chapter 02 measured the ear's two clocks; here is what they cost. After a loud sound ends, the threshold stays raised and decays over something like 100 to 200 ms — forward or post-masking, and it is substantial, worth tens of decibels in the first few milliseconds. The parametric form used in the coding literature, after Jesteadt, Bacon and Lehman, is linear in the logarithm of the delay:

M(Δt)=0.2 (2.3− log10Δt) (Lm−20) dB Δt in milliseconds, Lm the masker's level in dB SPL. Since log10200 = 2.301 the fit decays to exactly zero at 200 ms, which is where the psychophysics puts the end of post-masking. It is a fit over a range that starts a few milliseconds out, so it understates the masking immediately after offset — an error in the direction a codec wants

Before a loud sound, the ear is also briefly deaf, which is stranger. Backward or pre-masking exists, but it is small and short: the coding literature has traditionally quoted up to 20 ms, careful psychophysics gives something nearer 2 to 5 ms, and the amount depends heavily on how practised the listener is — well-trained listeners show little or none, which suggests that much of what is measured in naive listeners is confusion rather than masking. For an encoder the safe number is small. Call it five milliseconds and do not plan around more.

Now put the two numbers next to the arithmetic of a transform codec, and the problem appears immediately. An MP3 long block covers 1 152 input samples, which at 44.1 kHz is 26.12 ms. Quantisation error introduced in that block is spread across all of it, more or less evenly, because a coefficient is a statement about the entire window. Put a castanet strike three-quarters of the way through, and twenty milliseconds of the noise needed to code that strike lands before it, in silence, where the ear will forgive perhaps five. The result is pre-echo: a short burst of hiss that audibly precedes the attack.

Pre-echo is not a defect of the transform. It is the ear declining to extend backwards the generosity it extends forwards — and every block switch, every temporal noise shaping filter, every transient detector in every codec ever shipped is a response to that one asymmetry. A threshold computed per block cannot see inside a block.

Forward masking, implemented as a memory

There is one cheap trick every MPEG-style model carries, and it is the only place temporal masking appears in the model at all. Before using this block's threshold, clamp it against the previous block's, with a floor 25 dB below:

T=max( min(Tnow,Tprev) ,0.00316Tnow) 0.00316 = 10−2.5, i.e. −25 dB

Read it as a one-pole memory. A block that suddenly becomes loud does not get to claim its full new threshold immediately — it inherits the quiet block's caution, which is precisely the pre-echo case — while a block that goes quiet keeps some of the previous block's generosity, which is forward masking. Two comparisons, and it buys most of what a proper temporal model would. Chapter 04 shows the smear appearing as the window lengthens, chapter 07 the window-switching state machine that MP3 answers it with, chapter 08 the temporal noise shaping that AAC added, and chapter 11 what the artefact sounds like when the machinery fails.

Two ears spoil it

A monaural model cannot see the fifteen decibels your second ear gives you.

Everything above assumes one ear. Present the same masker to both ears and the same probe to both, then invert the probe's waveform at one ear, and the probe becomes 12 to 15 dB more detectable — the binaural masking level difference. The release is largest at low frequencies, around 15 dB near 250 to 500 Hz, and collapses to two or three decibels above about 1.5 kHz, where the wavelength is short enough that interaural phase stops being a usable cue at all. It is the same machinery that lets you follow one voice in a room full of them.

For a codec this is bad news, and it is the reason joint stereo used to have such a poor reputation. Mid/side and intensity coding both change the relationship between the noise in the left channel and the noise in the right. Quantisation noise that is identical in both channels images at the centre of the stereo picture and is fairly easy to hide; noise that is uncorrelated between channels, or inverted, is not. Each channel's monaural NMR can be comfortably negative and the artefact audible anyway. Encoders answer with a frequency-dependent penalty. LAME's is a raised cosine, fitted to the shape of the BMLD curve rather than derived from it:

mld(f)=12.5 (1− cos πmin(z,15.5)15.5) −25 dB −24.8 dB at 100 Hz, −19.7 at 500 Hz, −10.6 at 1 kHz, −1.4 at 2 kHz and exactly 0 from 15.5 Bark — about 3.2 kHz — upward. Below that the stereo threshold is dragged down towards the monaural one by up to 25 dB

The practical upshot is that the bass is where stereo coding has to be careful and the treble is where it can be reckless, which is exactly the opposite of the intuition most people start with and exactly what intensity stereo does — it discards the phase relationship only above a frequency where the ear has stopped using it. Chapter 07 and chapter 08 take the joint-stereo tools apart; chapter 09 shows what a codec designed after the argument was settled does instead.

What people get wrong

Six corrections, in rough order of how much damage they do.

A codec does not delete the masked sounds. This is the commonest account and it is not what happens. Nothing is removed for being masked. The spectrum is quantised — every line of it — with a step size chosen per band so that the resulting rounding error sits just under the masked threshold. Masked components do survive, coarsely; lines whose entire magnitude is below the step round to zero, which is a consequence of the arithmetic rather than a deletion rule. The masking threshold is a noise budget, not a delete list. Figure 3.4's gold curve is a ceiling on error, not an outline of what is kept.

Masking is not a matter of attention. What has been measured throughout this chapter is energetic masking: physical overlap of excitation on the basilar membrane, present in an anaesthetised animal and in a listener attending as hard as they can. It has a cousin — informational masking, where the target is perfectly audible in the peripheral sense but lost among a competing sound it resembles — which is central rather than cochlear, varies with training and expectation, and is not modelled by any shipping codec. Confusing the two produces the belief that you can train yourself past masking. You can train yourself past the informational kind; the energetic kind is plumbing.

MP3 does not "cut everything above 16 kHz" because of masking. Encoders do often low-pass, sometimes at 16 kHz and lower at low bitrates, and Karlheinz Brandenburg has recommended the practice — but as a rate strategy, not a psychoacoustic finding. The top octave is expensive, contributes little to most programme and is where most listeners' hearing is worst, so at a tight budget it is the first thing to trade. That is an economic decision made by the encoder's author, it is adjustable, and it has nothing to do with the threshold curves above.

Two maskers do not add in decibels. They add in power: 70 dB and 70 dB give 73.01 dB, not 140 and not 70. An encoder that summed thresholds in the log domain would overestimate its budget by tens of decibels and sound like a broken radio. Real ears, as §5 notes, add slightly more than the power sum, which is why the models' arithmetic is safe rather than exact.

The masking curve is not a property of the recording. It is the output of a model of a listener. Figure 3.2 shows four such models disagreeing by 38 dB two Bark above a masker, and figure 3.1's table shows two of them disagreeing by 14.6 dB at the masker itself. When two encoders at the same bitrate sound different, this is usually where the difference started — long before the quantiser, in a judgement about how far a shadow reaches.

And "transparent at 128 kbit/s" is a claim about a population, not about you. The perceptual entropy calculation gives a lower bound under one model and an average over programme; it says nothing about the hardest two seconds of the hardest track in your collection, played on equipment good enough to expose it. The only instrument that settles that question is a properly conducted listening test, which is what chapter 11 hands you.

Where this goes next

The model is finished. It now needs somewhere to apply itself.

What §5 assembled is a curve: for this instant of music, in each band, the loudest noise that will not be heard. It is the target that the rest of the encoder exists to hit, and on its own it can do nothing at all. A threshold defined per critical band is a statement about the spectrum, and a codec is handed a waveform — so before any of this arithmetic can be spent, the signal has to be taken apart into frequency components that can be quantised independently, cheaply, in overlapping blocks, and put back together without a seam. That turns out to be a harder problem than it sounds: the obvious transforms either cost fifty per cent more coefficients than samples, or leak so badly that a tone smears across half the bands it was supposed to be isolated in, and figure 3.5 has already shown what happens when the block is the wrong length. Chapter 04 builds the answer — windows, leakage, the polyphase bank and the modified discrete cosine transform, whose aliasing cancels exactly, demonstrated numerically rather than asserted. After that, in chapter 05, the two curves finally meet and the bits get spent.
