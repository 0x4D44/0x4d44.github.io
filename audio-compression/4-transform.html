The transform is where a codec decides what it means by "now".

A masking threshold is a statement about frequency, and a microphone hands you time. Everything between those two facts — the block, the window, the leakage, the overlap and the peculiar non-invertible transform that every modern codec uses anyway — is this chapter. The central trick is that the MDCT deliberately manufactures a large error, because it knows the next block will manufacture its exact negative.

The cut

A block is a multiplication, and multiplication in time is convolution in frequency.

Chapter 03 ended with a curve: the loudest noise, band by band, that a given instant of music can hide. That curve is indexed by frequency, and the samples arriving from the converter are indexed by time. Before a single bit can be saved, the encoder has to move from one index to the other — and it has to do so on a signal that never stops, using a transform that only works on finite pieces. So it chops.

Chopping is not a neutral act. Taking L consecutive samples and calling them a block is multiplying the infinite signal by a rectangular box, and the Fourier transform turns that multiplication into a convolution:

y[n]=w[n]·x[n] ⟺ Y(f)=(X∗W)(f) every spectral peak in the signal is replaced by a scaled, shifted copy of the window's own transform — leakage is the window's sidelobes

There is therefore no such thing as an unwindowed analysis. There is only an analysis with a rectangular window, and the rectangular window's transform is the Dirichlet kernel: a narrow main lobe, a first sidelobe just 13.26 dB down, and a far field that decays at a miserable 6 dB per octave. Convolve a pure tone with that and the tone stops being at one place.

How badly depends entirely on where the tone falls relative to the analysis grid. A tone sitting exactly on a bin centre completes a whole number of cycles in the block; its periodic extension is seamless, and the rectangular window leaks nothing at all. Move it half a bin and the block no longer joins up with itself, and the discontinuity has to be paid for in spectrum. At length 64, with the tone half a bin off centre, 9.81 % of its energy lands outside the three bins nearest its true frequency. The same tone through a Hann window leaks 0.09 % — a factor of a hundred, bought by doubling the width of the peak.

For a codec the consequence is not cosmetic. The psychoacoustic model reads this spectrum and decides, from it, how loud each band is and how tonal. A tone that appears to be everywhere looks like a masker that masks everything, the model raises the threshold across the spectrum, and the rate loop in Chapter 05 spends its bits protecting an artefact of the analysis. If the FFT machinery itself is unfamiliar, the spectrum-analyser write-up elsewhere in this almanac takes it apart in C; what follows here is the quantitative side.

The window zoo

Every window offers the same bribe: sidelobe suppression, paid for in main-lobe width.

Most of the windows in use are generalised cosine sums — a constant plus a few cosine harmonics of the block period, with coefficients chosen to cancel sidelobes against one another:

w[n]= ∑Mm=0 (−1)mam cos( 2πmnN ) Hann: a = 0.5, 0.5 · Hamming: 0.54, 0.46 · Blackman: 0.42, 0.5, 0.08 · Blackman–Harris: 0.35875, 0.48829, 0.14128, 0.01168

Four numbers decide whether a window is any good, and they pull against each other. The peak sidelobe level says how much a strong tone contaminates its immediate neighbourhood. The main-lobe width, quoted either null-to-null or at −3 dB, says how close two tones can be before they merge. The asymptotic rolloff says what happens far away. And the equivalent noise bandwidth says how much wider than one bin the window really is when it is measuring noise rather than tones.

The rolloff column is where the intuitive ranking breaks. Hamming has a first sidelobe at −42.66 dB against Hann's −31.47, which looks like a rout; but Hamming's coefficients do not sum to zero at the endpoints, so the window has a step discontinuity where it starts and stops, and a step decays at only 6 dB per octave. Hann reaches zero smoothly and decays at 18. Cross them somewhere around eight bins out and the ranking reverses permanently: at 32 bins Hamming is still leaking tens of decibels above Hann. A window whose endpoints are non-zero is a window with a hidden rectangular component.

Gabor's floor

You cannot buy your way out, because it is Heisenberg's theorem with different units.

Dennis Gabor's 1946 paper Theory of Communication introduced the "logon" — an elementary quantum of information occupying a fixed area in the time–frequency plane — and with it the inequality that every codec designer has been arguing about ever since:

Δt·Δf≥ 14π =0.0795775… Δt and Δf the standard deviations of |x(t)|² and |X(f)|²; equality only for a Gaussian. Written in angular frequency it becomes Δω·Δt ≥ ½ — the same statement, different units, and a common source of factor-of-2π confusion

The proof is the same proof as Heisenberg's, which is worth saying plainly because engineers sometimes treat this as a limitation of current analysis technique. It is not. No window, no basis, no clever adaptive scheme moves the floor; they only move you along it. Gabor, incidentally, got his Nobel Prize in 1971 for holography, not for this.

The practical form is almost insultingly simple. A block of L samples at rate fs lasts L/fs seconds and produces bins spaced fs/L hertz apart, so their product is 1 for every L and every rate — the hyperbola in person:

T·Δf= Lfs · fsL =1 1024 samples at 44 100 Hz = 23.220 ms and 43.066 Hz, exactly

Two reconstruction conditions

There are two ways to reconstruct from overlapping blocks, and the window everyone reaches for passes only one.

Windowing fixes leakage but destroys the ends of every block, so blocks have to overlap and be added back together. The short-time Fourier transform advances by a hop R and analyses each position:

Xm[k]= ∑L−1n=0 w[n]·x[n+mR] ·e−2πikn/L

If the window is applied only on analysis and the blocks are simply added on synthesis, the requirement is constant overlap-add: the shifted copies of the window must sum to a constant. Hann at 50 % overlap satisfies it trivially, because 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 at every sample. But an MDCT-based codec applies the window twice — once going in, once coming out, so that quantisation noise is tapered at the block edges too — and then the condition changes to the power-complementary one that Princen and Bradley wrote down:

COLA: ∑m w[n−mR]=1    PB: w2[n]+w2[n+N]=1 left, for a window applied once; right, for a window applied twice, with w of length 2N

Evaluate the right-hand condition on a Hann window and the error is not small: it is exactly 0.5, which Fig 4.2's last readout will confirm for you. Hann is not a near miss for MDCT use, it is a different kind of window. Its square root, however, passes to within 4×10⁻¹⁶ — and the square root of Hann is, by one line of trigonometry, the window every codec actually uses:

w[n]= sin( π2N (n+½)) , sin2θ= 1−cos2θ2 ⇒ w2=Hann the sine window is the square root of the Hann window, evaluated at half-shifted points — a COLA window's square root is a power-complementary one
Why the half-sample offset

MDCT windows are indexed at n + ½ rather than n. That makes them symmetric about the centre of the window rather than about a sample, which is what the cancellation in the next two sections needs: it requires w[2N−1−n] = w[n] exactly, and a window defined on integer points is symmetric about a point that does not exist. It is a half-sample of bookkeeping that the entire proof rests on.

The 32-band bank

MPEG's polyphase bank was a good filter bank and a poor model of the ear.

Before the MDCT won, the standard front end was a bank of band-pass filters: MPEG-1 Audio's 32-band pseudo-QMF, used unchanged by Layers I, II and III. A single 512-tap prototype low-pass is modulated to 32 positions across the spectrum,

hk[n]=h[n] ·cos( π32 (k+½)(n−16) ) 0 ≤ k ≤ 31, 0 ≤ n ≤ 511; each subband output is then decimated by 32, so the bank is critically sampled and each filter is lapped by 512/32 = 16

The bands are of uniform width, fs/64 — at 44.1 kHz that is 689.0625 Hz each. The composite response of analysis followed by synthesis is flat to better than 0.07 dB, which is excellent; the stopband rejection is commonly quoted as around 96 dB, though that figure is harder to pin to a primary source than the ripple one.

The second problem is the one that decided the argument. Uniform bands are a poor match to the ear. Chapter 02's critical bands are roughly 100 Hz wide at the bottom of the spectrum and several kilohertz wide at the top, so MPEG's band 0 — a single number covering 0 to 689 Hz — swallows something like six critical bands, and the encoder must set one noise floor for all of them and therefore obey the strictest. Band 31, meanwhile, covers 21.4 to 22.05 kHz, where nobody is listening at all. Layer III bolted an 18-line MDCT onto each subband to claw back resolution, which is why MP3's filter bank is called hybrid and why it needs those butterflies; Chapter 07 takes that apart. AAC threw the whole stage away.

2N in, N out

The matrix cannot be inverted, and it is inverted anyway.

The modified discrete cosine transform takes 2N windowed samples and returns N coefficients. Written out, it is an ordinary-looking cosine sum with one unusual phase term:

X[k]= ∑2N−1n=0 w[n]x[n] cos[ πN (n+½+ N2 )(k+½)] k = 0 … N−1
y[n]= 2N ∑N−1k=0 X[k] cos[ πN (n+½+ N2 )(k+½)] n = 0 … 2N−1. The 2/N scaling is the convention that makes windowed overlap-add come out at exactly 1×; texts that put √(2/N) on both transforms will give you different numbers for the same signal

Count the dimensions. Two thousand and forty-eight numbers go in; one thousand and twenty-four come out. As a matrix it is 2N × N, its null space has dimension N, and it therefore cannot have an inverse. Applying the formula above to a single block's coefficients does not return the block. It returns this, with the 2N input split into four quarters a b c d of length N/2 and rev meaning time-reversed:

IMDCT(MDCT([a b c d])) = [a−rev(b) |b−rev(a) |c+rev(d) |d+rev(c)] with x = [1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8] and N = 4, this is [−3 −1 1 3 13 13 13 13] — check it against the transform running in Fig 4.4

Each half of the output is the true signal plus a time-reversed copy of itself, mirrored about the quarter point — minus in the first half, plus in the second. This is time-domain aliasing, and the name is exact rather than metaphorical. Chapter 01 established that undersampling in time folds frequencies back on themselves; undersampling the transform, by keeping N outputs for 2N inputs, folds time back on itself in precisely the same way. The two phenomena are the same theorem seen from the two ends.

The MDCT takes 2N samples and returns N numbers. As a matrix it cannot be inverted. It is inverted anyway. Princen and Bradley, 1986, in effect

The idea arrived twice. John Princen and Alan Bradley published Analysis/synthesis filter bank design based on time domain aliasing cancellation in IEEE Transactions on ASSP in 1986, and with A. W. Johnson turned it into the oddly-stacked, critically-sampled system everyone now calls the MDCT at ICASSP in 1987. Separately, around 1990, Henrique Malvar arrived at the same equations from the lapped-transform side and called the result the Modulated Lapped Transform. Two communities invented the same object and named it twice; the papers are not variations on a theme, they are the same transform.

Cancellation

The garbage cancels to fifteen decimal places.

Take a signal, cut block A from samples [0, 2N) and block B from [N, 3N), so they overlap by half. Transform each, invert each, window each again, and add them in the region they share. In that region, block A contributes its own second half — true signal plus its alias — and block B contributes its first half, which carries the same alias with the opposite sign. Write the two contributions at offset i into the overlap:

A:w[N+i](w[N+i]x[N+i] +w[2N−1−i]x[2N−1−i])
B:w[i](w[i]x[N+i] −w[N−1−i]x[2N−1−i]) the first term in each bracket is signal; the second is the time-domain alias

Add them and look at the alias terms alone. Their coefficient is w[N+i]w[2N−1−i] − w[i]w[N−1−i], and if the window is symmetric that is w[N+i]w[i] − w[i]w[N+i] = 0. The alias is not attenuated or smoothed away; it is subtracted from itself. What remains is the signal, weighted by w²[N+i] + w²[i], which is 1 by Princen–Bradley. Two conditions, two lines, exact reconstruction:

w[2N−1−n]=w[n]  and  w2[n]+w2[n+N]=1 symmetry kills the alias; power-complementarity restores unit gain. Neither alone is enough

One more thing falls out of the same algebra, and it is the reason any of this ships. The 2N-point sum in the definition costs 2N² multiply-accumulates per block — about 2.1 million at N = 1024. But the folding identity above can be run forwards: fold the 2N windowed samples down to N first, then take a DCT-IV of length N, and that DCT-IV can itself be computed with an N/2-point complex FFT and a pair of twiddle passes offset by an eighth of a bin. Sine or KBD

Choosing a window shape is a question about whom you are trying to mask.

Once the window must be symmetric and power-complementary, the family of legal choices is small but not trivial. AAC ships two of them and signals the choice with a single bit per frame. The sine window has the narrower main lobe and the better first sidelobe, −23.0 dB. The Kaiser–Bessel-derived window at α = 4 is worse near in — a first sidelobe of only −19.6 dB — and has a wider main lobe as well. On the table above, sine wins outright. The question an encoder is really asking is therefore not "which window is better" but "where does the noise I am about to inject need to stay?" If the spectrum is dense and the neighbouring bands are close in level, leakage into the bin next door is what matters and the sine window's narrow lobe wins. Switching the blocks

Eight short blocks cost exactly zero extra coefficients.

A transform coefficient describes its whole window. Quantise it coarsely and the error you introduce is spread evenly across all 2N samples, with the envelope of the window rather than of the signal. For 46 ms of sustained violin that is exactly what you want. For a castanet strike at the end of the window it is a catastrophe: the noise added to code the attack appears across the whole span, including the near-silence that preceded it.

Chapter 03 priced that. Backward masking — a sound concealing noise that arrived before it — covers about 2 ms; Painter and Spanias put it at "no significant coding noise longer than approximately 2.0 ms before onset", other sources give figures between 0.5 and 5 ms, and it is the least robust and most training-dependent of all the masking phenomena. Forward masking, by contrast, runs for 10 to 50 ms and by some accounts far longer, which is why nobody has ever complained about post-echo.

Every frame is hostage to its neighbour

Because each output sample is the sum of contributions from two overlapping blocks, no frame can be decoded on its own. Lose one packet and you lose not just its own 1024 samples but half a window on either side of them, which is why every transform codec ships some form of loss concealment and why the decoder must be told the window sequence before it can invert anything. The same coupling that makes the transform critically sampled makes it unforgiving of gaps.

The arithmetic checks: 1024 + 448 + 128 + 448 = 2048, and 448 + 8×128 + 128 + 448 = 2048. These offsets are the layout given in every secondary account of the standard and are self-consistent, though this study could not verify them against the ISO text directly. What matters is the last column. An eight-short frame emits 1024 coefficients, exactly as a long frame does, and advances the stream by the same 1024 samples. Eight times the time resolution, bought with eight times worse frequency resolution, at zero cost in coefficients. What it does cost is coding efficiency — a tonal signal analysed at 128-line resolution spreads its energy over more lines, and the side information is repeated per group, which is usually put at something like ten to thirty per cent more bits on the affected frames, varying with content.

Block switching is the first of four countermeasures, and the only one that changes the transform. The second is temporal noise shaping, introduced by Jürgen Herre and James Johnston at the 101st AES Convention in Los Angeles in November 1996: run an open-loop linear predictor across frequency rather than across time, and the prediction residual shapes the quantisation noise's envelope in time, hiding it under the attack without shortening the window. The third is the bit reservoir, which lets the frame containing the transient borrow bits from its quieter neighbours. The fourth, gain control, appeared in MPEG-4 AAC's scalable sample rate profile and is rarely seen. Chapter 08 takes all four apart.

What people get wrong

Seven things that are said about transforms and are not true.

"The MDCT is a DCT with a window."
A DCT of 2N samples returns 2N coefficients. The MDCT returns N. It is a non-square transform, and the window is not an accessory to it: without a power-complementary window there is no inverse at all, as Fig 4.4's Hann setting will demonstrate.
"Overlapping by 50 % doubles the data."
For an STFT, yes. For the MDCT, no — it is critically sampled. 44 100 samples a second go in and 44 100 coefficients a second come out, with every sample appearing in two blocks. This is the thing to be astonished by, and it is what "lapped transform" means.
"Time-domain aliasing is a bug that TDAC cleans up."
It is load-bearing. The transform discards half its outputs and uses the aliasing to carry what it discarded. Compute a "clean" inverse that suppresses the alias and you cannot reconstruct anything.
"Use a Hann window; it's the standard one."
Hann fails the Princen–Bradley condition by exactly 0.5. Its square root — the sine window — passes to within 4×10⁻¹⁶. Hann is a COLA window, for analysis only; the MDCT needs a window that survives being applied twice.
"KBD is simply better than sine, which is why AAC uses it."
KBD α = 4 has a worse first sidelobe than sine, −19.6 dB against −23.0, and a wider main lobe. It wins only beyond three or four bins. AAC ships both and spends a bit per frame on the choice precisely because neither dominates.
"Short blocks cost bitrate."
Eight AAC short blocks produce 1024 coefficients and advance the frame by 1024 samples, identically to one long block. What they cost is coding efficiency on tonal material, not coefficients.
"The MDCT gives you the spectrum."
It gives you a real-valued, phase-entangled projection. There is no magnitude-and-phase split and a coefficient's sign is not a phase. That is why MP3 and AAC encoders run a separate FFT purely to feed the psychoacoustic model, and why MDCT coefficients are so awkward to plot.

Notice what has not happened. Nothing in this chapter has saved a single bit. The polyphase bank is critically sampled; the MDCT is critically sampled; 44 100 numbers a second went in and 44 100 came out, in a different basis, at higher precision than they started. The transform is pure overhead until something downstream exploits it. What it has done is arrange the signal so that the masking curve from Chapter 03 can finally be spent: a threshold defined per critical band can now be applied to the lines that fall in that band, and the coefficients that sit below it can be rounded into oblivion. Chapter 05 does the rounding — the power-law quantiser, the two nested loops that fight each other over the same bit budget, and the bit reservoir that lets a frame spend what its neighbours saved.
