The last stage of an encoder is the only one that gives back exactly what it was given.

Everything up to this point has been a decision about what to destroy. Entropy coding destroys nothing. It takes the integers the rate loop has already settled on and writes them down in fewer bits than a fixed-width field would need, and it is exactly reversible. On real programme material it saves roughly forty to sixty per cent of what a fixed-width field would have spent — which, handed back to the quantiser, is ten decibels of signal-to-noise you did not have to pay for.

The floor

What the rate loop hands over is a very lopsided stream of very small integers.

By the end of Chapter 05 the encoder has produced, for every block and every band, a list of integers: the quantised MDCT coefficients. That list is the entire payload. Everything else in the bitstream — scalefactors, window flags, table selects, the frame header — is bookkeeping around it. The question this chapter answers is how many bits those integers cost, and the answer has a hard lower bound that Claude Shannon fixed in 1948, in the same paper that gave the word bit its technical meaning.

Treat the quantised values as symbols drawn from a source with probabilities p1 … pK. The entropy of that source is

H=− ∑Ki=1 pi log2 pi bits per symbol — the average surprise of a draw from the source

Shannon's source-coding theorem says two things about that number, and both matter here. No uniquely decodable code can average fewer than H bits per symbol; and codes exist whose average approaches H as closely as you like, if you are willing to code long enough blocks at once. The first half is a wall. The second half is a promise, and the rest of this chapter is about how expensive the promise turns out to be to keep.

The individual quantity behind the sum is the one worth carrying around. A symbol of probability p ought to cost

ℓ=− log2 p so p = ½ costs 1 bit, p = 0.8 costs 0.322, and p = 0.001 costs 9.97

Note what that licenses. If a symbol turns up four times in five, its honest price is about a third of a bit. No code that writes whole bits one symbol at a time can charge a third of a bit, which is the crack that arithmetic coding later prises open. And note what it forbids: if the alphabet is flat, entropy coding has nothing to sell. Sixteen equiprobable symbols cost four bits each however you write them.

That is the whole reason a lossless general-purpose compressor barely dents a PCM file, and the figure below measures it rather than asserting it. Pick the 16-bit PCM samples stream: for the plucked-string programme the sample values, histogrammed, come out at a shade under fourteen bits of entropy out of sixteen. A ratio of about 1.15 to one, which is roughly what a general-purpose compressor manages on a WAV file. Then pick quantised MDCT lines and watch the same audio, after the transform and the rate loop, drop to about four bits per line, with well over a third of the lines exactly zero. The transform and the quantiser did that. The entropy coder is merely about to collect.

What the encoder on these pages actually counts

The rate loop in assets/audio.js prices a band as its zero-order entropy times the number of lines, plus eight bits of scalefactor overhead, plus a real escape cost for any value outside ±255. That is the same accounting a production encoder's inner loop does, minus the table lookup — which is why the bitrate readouts across this study land within a couple of per cent of what you asked for. It is an estimate of the code, not the code itself.

Kraft's budget

Any set of code lengths is realisable if, and only if, it fits inside one.

Before choosing lengths you need to know which sets of lengths are legal. A code is prefix-free if no codeword is the beginning of another; that property is what lets a decoder read a bitstream with no separators and no lookahead, stopping the instant it has seen a complete codeword. Prefix-free codes are exactly the codes you can draw as a binary tree with the symbols at the leaves, and the constraint on their lengths is a single inequality, stated by Leon Kraft in 1949 and extended by Brockway McMillan in 1956 to every uniquely decodable code, prefix-free or not.

∑Ki=1 2−ℓi ≤1 Kraft–McMillan: a prefix code with lengths ℓᵢ exists precisely when this holds

The picture behind it is a budget. Think of the unit interval as the whole of code space. A codeword of length ℓ claims a slice of width 2−ℓ and, being a prefix, forbids anything inside that slice. One bit costs you half of everything. Two bits cost a quarter. Ten bits cost a thousandth, which is why long codewords are nearly free to hand out and short ones are ruinous. You may spend the budget however you like; you may not overspend it.

Two consequences run through the rest of the chapter. First, the reason the ideal length is −log2p is that spending your budget in proportion to probability is what minimises the expected length subject to Kraft — the optimum of the continuous relaxation sits exactly there. Second, because Kraft's inequality constrains only the lengths, a decoder never needs the tree. It needs the list of lengths, from which the tree is reconstructible. That observation is worth several kilobytes in every codec that transmits its tables, and it is the subject of the section after next.

Huffman, and his ceiling

Huffman's algorithm is provably optimal, and that is precisely its limitation.

In 1951 David Huffman was a graduate student in Robert Fano's information theory class at MIT, offered the usual choice between a final examination and a term paper. The paper's topic was to find the optimal prefix code. What Fano did not mention was that he and Shannon had already attacked the problem and produced only a good heuristic — the top-down Shannon–Fano split — and that the optimal construction was open. Huffman worked at it, was about to give up and revise for the examination instead, and then saw that the tree had to be built from the bottom.

The algorithm is four lines. Put every symbol in a pool with its weight. Repeatedly remove the two lightest entries, join them under a new node whose weight is their sum, and return that node to the pool. When one node remains, label every left branch 0 and every right branch 1; each symbol's codeword is the path to its leaf. The proof of optimality is an exchange argument: in any optimal code the two least likely symbols can be taken to be siblings at maximum depth, so merging them reduces the problem to one symbol smaller without loss.

Optimal, though, means optimal among codes that assign a whole number of bits to each symbol. That qualification is the whole story of its limits. Every codeword is at least one bit long, so

H≤L< H+1 and more tightly, the redundancy L − H is at most p₁ + 0.086, with p₁ the probability of the commonest symbol

When the alphabet is well conditioned — a dozen symbols, none of them dominant — that gap is small change. The textbook six-symbol example built into this study's DSP kit has an entropy of 2.2195 bits and a Huffman average of 2.24: nine-tenths of one per cent of waste. But a quantised MDCT band at a low bitrate is not well conditioned. It is mostly zeros. When p(0) reaches 0.9 the entropy of a binary zero/non-zero decision is 0.469 bits and Huffman must still spend a full bit, so more than half of what you pay buys nothing. Drag the figure's decay control to the right and watch the two numbers separate.

The one-bit floor is not a rounding error

The moment any single symbol takes more than half the probability mass, a symbol-by-symbol code is guaranteed to waste bits, and the waste grows without limit as the distribution sharpens. At p = 0.99 the entropy of the binary decision is 0.081 bits and Huffman spends 1.000: a factor of twelve. Codecs dodge this in three ways, all of which show up later — code several coefficients as one symbol so the combined alphabet is flatter (MP3's pairs and quadruples, AAC's 2- and 4-tuples), run-length the zeros so they stop being symbols at all, or give up on whole bits and use an arithmetic coder.

Lengths are the table

No decoder stores a tree. It stores a sorted list of lengths.

A Huffman tree is not unique. Swapping any node's children relabels codewords without changing a single length, and the lengths are all that affect the compressed size. That freedom is worth exploiting: fix a convention, and the codes become derivable from the lengths alone.

The convention is the canonical code. Sort the symbols by code length, breaking ties by symbol order. Give the first symbol of the shortest length the all-zeros codeword. Thereafter, add one to the previous codeword; whenever the length increases by d, shift left by d first. That is the whole algorithm, and it is what DSP.canonical in this study's kit does — the code list printed under Fig 6.2 is its output, regenerated every time you move a control.

Two properties fall out and both are exploited by real decoders. Codewords of the same length are numerically consecutive, and every codeword of length ℓ is numerically smaller than every codeword of length ℓ+1 taken over the same number of bits. So a decoder can hold two small arrays — the first code of each length, and the index of the first symbol of each length — and decode by reading bits one at a time while comparing against a single integer per length. No pointers, no tree nodes, no cache misses walking a structure. Deflate, JPEG and Vorbis all transmit their tables this way; Vorbis packs its codebooks into the third header packet of every stream, so the codebook lengths themselves are what travel.

Fixed tables age; transmitted tables do not

MP3 and AAC hard-code their tables in the standard, so a decoder written in 1994 decodes a file made in 2026. The cost is that the tables were fitted to whatever material the committee had, and no later encoder may improve them. Vorbis chose the other side of the trade and ships its codebooks in the stream: encoders were free to retune, and did, which is why late Vorbis encoders sound considerably better than early ones at the same bitrate while the format never changed. Opus, as the last section explains, sidesteps the question by not having tables at all.

MP3's thirty-two tables

576 lines, three regions, and a table select for each of them.

A Layer III granule is 576 spectral lines. Coding all 576 with one table would be a poor fit, because the statistics of the bottom of the spectrum and the top of it have nothing in common: low lines are large and dense, high lines are small and mostly zero. So the standard cuts the granule into three regions, from the top down, by what is in them.

Work backwards from line 575. Everything from the last non-zero line to the end is rzero and costs nothing at all; its extent is implied by what comes before it, and not transmitted. Below that, as long as the values are confined to −1, 0 and +1, they are coded four at a time in the count1 region. Everything below that is big_values, coded in pairs, and it is the only region whose size is transmitted: a 9-bit field giving the number of pairs, so at most 288, so at most 576 lines. The three sizes therefore partition the granule exactly.

The big-values region is then split again into three sub-regions with independent table selects, at boundaries that must land on scalefactor-band edges — a 4-bit region0_count and a 3-bit region1_count naming how many bands each of the first two covers. Each sub-region carries its own 5-bit table_select. Those are the thirty-two tables of the title, indexed 0 to 31, of which 4 and 14 are unused and 0 means "this region is empty"; a separate 1-bit count1table_select chooses between the two quadruple tables, A and B.

The escape mechanism in the last two families is worth dwelling on, because it is the standard answer to a distribution with a long tail. A Huffman table with a codeword for every possible magnitude would be enormous and almost entirely dead weight. So the table tops out at 15, and the value 15 is not a value but a flag: read linbits further bits and add them. With linbits at its maximum of 13 the representable magnitude reaches 15 + 8191 = 8 206. The escape is not free — you are paying a fixed-width field for exactly the values whose probability you have the least information about — but it bounds the table size at 256 entries and it never fails.

Note also what the tables code. A big-values codeword names a pair of magnitudes; the signs follow as one raw bit per non-zero value, outside the Huffman code entirely. A count1 codeword names four magnitudes at once, each of which is 0 or 1, so the codeword is really a 4-bit occupancy pattern, again with raw sign bits appended. Coding in pairs and quadruples is the trick from the previous note: it flattens the effective alphabet and buys back some of the one-bit floor. Four independent binary decisions at p(0) = 0.9 have a joint entropy of 1.88 bits, and a quadruple code can spend 1.88 where four separate codes would be forced to spend 4.00.

The region boundaries are constrained to scalefactor-band edges because the scalefactors are applied band by band, and a table select that straddled a band would be pricing two different noise floors with one distribution. For a long block at 44.1 kHz those edges are fixed by the standard:

AAC's twelve codebooks

Five years later the same problem was solved with sections instead of regions.

AAC keeps Huffman coding and keeps fixed tables, but rebuilds everything around them. Instead of three regions with hard-coded semantics there are sections: runs of consecutive scalefactor bands that share one codebook. The section data is an alternating sequence of a 4-bit codebook index and a run length, the run length being built from increments five bits wide for a long window and three bits wide for a short one. An encoder is free to use one section for the whole spectrum or a new one every band, and it decides by measuring — the cost of a section header against the saving from a better-matched table.

There are eleven codebooks for spectral data, plus codebook 0, ZERO, which says a section contains nothing and so costs only its section header. The eleven differ along three axes: how many coefficients a codeword covers, the largest absolute value it can express, and whether the signs are inside the code or appended as raw bits.

Codebook 11 is the escape book and carries the whole tail on its own. The value 16 in a decoded pair is not a magnitude but a signal that an escape sequence follows: a unary prefix giving the word length, then that many bits of magnitude. It is a Golomb-like construction rather than MP3's fixed linbits field, which means the cost grows gracefully with the value instead of being set once per region — an encoder no longer has to choose an escape width in advance and live with it.

Two further pieces of the AAC bitstream are entropy coded and often forgotten. Scalefactors are not sent as absolute values: each is coded as a difference from the previous active scalefactor, with the first taken relative to the frame's global_gain, and the differences go through a dedicated 121-entry codebook spanning −60 to +60. That is delta coding feeding entropy coding, and on smooth spectra it is worth a great deal, because the difference sequence is far more concentrated than the scalefactors themselves. And mid/side stereo, decided per scalefactor band, is in effect an entropy-coding trick too: when the channels are correlated the side signal's coefficients quantise to zero far more often than either channel's would, so the alphabet sharpens and the code shortens. Chapter 08 covers both, and the parametric tools that replace coefficients with descriptions.

Run-length and zero-run coding, quietly everywhere

Of the three regions in an MP3 granule, only two carry codewords. rzero is a run length, and a run length that is not even transmitted — it is whatever is left. AAC's ZERO codebook does the same job per section. This is the cheapest entropy coding there is: a symbol that never appears in the stream costs nothing, and at 64 kbit/s the majority of an MP3 granule's lines are handled this way. When Fig 6.1 reports a zero fraction of forty per cent it is understating the case, because it counts zeros the real bitstream would never spell out.

Fractions of a bit

Arithmetic coding gives up on codewords and encodes the whole message as one number.

Huffman's ceiling comes from insisting that each symbol have its own codeword. Remove that insistence and the ceiling goes. The idea, developed independently by Richard Pasco at Stanford and Jorma Rissanen at IBM and published by both in May 1976, is to represent the entire message as a single number in the unit interval.

Begin with the interval [0, 1). Divide it into sub-intervals in proportion to the symbol probabilities, and keep the one belonging to the first symbol. Divide that in the same proportions, and keep the one belonging to the second symbol. Repeat. After n symbols you hold an interval whose width is the product of the probabilities of the symbols you saw, and any number inside it identifies the message uniquely.

w= ∏ni=1 p(si) ⟹ B= ⌈−log2 w⌉ +1 bits to name a point inside an interval of width w

Take the logarithm of the product and the sum of −log2p reappears: the ideal length of the message, in one piece, with no per-symbol rounding anywhere. The only rounding left is at the very end, where the final interval has to be named by a finite binary fraction, and that costs at most two bits for the entire message rather than up to one bit for every symbol in it. Code a thousand symbols and the overhead is two bits, not a thousand.

The practical difficulty is that the interval shrinks exponentially and no machine has unbounded precision. The fix is incremental output with renormalisation: as soon as the top bit — or, in a byte-oriented implementation, the top byte — of the interval is determined, emit it and rescale by two (or 256). The awkward case is an interval that straddles the midpoint so narrowly that the leading bits are still undecided, which is underflow; implementations either count pending bits and flush them when the ambiguity resolves, or, as the range coder in the next section does, buffer the outstanding byte and propagate a carry into it later.

None of that is difficult, and it was all understood by 1979. What kept arithmetic coding out of mainstream standards for two decades was patents. IBM held a thicket of them around efficient binary implementations — the Q-coder of Arps, Pennebaker, Mitchell, Langdon and colleagues, filed from 1986 — and although JPEG standardised an arithmetic-coded mode from the start, effectively no encoder shipped it, so essentially every JPEG in the world is Huffman-coded. The same caution is the usual explanation for MPEG audio's layers staying with Huffman tables, and bzip2 famously dropped arithmetic coding for Huffman over the same worry. By the time the patents had lapsed the tables were in a hundred million decoders.

Opus's range coder

A range coder is arithmetic coding in integers — and in Opus it is also the bit budget.

A range coder is the same algorithm with the unit interval replaced by a large integer range and the renormalisation done a byte at a time rather than a bit at a time. G. Nigel N. Martin described it at the Video and Data Recording Conference in Southampton in July 1979, apparently unaware of Pasco's work three years earlier; the two are the same coder in different clothes, and the byte-oriented form is faster at a compression cost usually quoted in hundredths of a per cent.

Opus uses one for everything. RFC 6716 section 4.1 specifies a coder with a 32-bit range register rng, renormalisation in 8-bit steps, and the update

r= ⌊rngft⌋ ,   low+= rng−r(ft−fl) ,   rng=r(fh−fl) for a symbol occupying [f_l, f_h) out of a total frequency f_t; renormalise while rng ≤ 2²³

Two details of the Opus design are unusual enough to be worth the space. The first is that not everything goes through the coder. Uniformly distributed values — sign bits, some PVQ indices — are written as raw bits, packed backwards from the end of the frame while the range coder's bytes accumulate forwards from the start. The two streams grow towards each other and the packet is full when they meet. This costs nothing in efficiency, saves the coder's multiply and divide on values it could not compress anyway, and has a robustness benefit the RFC calls out: a bit error in the raw region corrupts a value but does not desynchronise the range decoder.

The second is subtler and more important. The decoder, at any point, can compute exactly how many bits the coder has consumed so far — Opus's ec_tell, which is the running total of bytes emitted less the log of the current range, and its ec_tell_frac sibling, which reports the same quantity to an eighth of a bit.

tell= nbits− log2 rng bits consumed, computable identically by encoder and decoder, without transmitting anything

Because both ends can compute it, CELT does not transmit a bit allocation at all. It codes the band energies first, then both encoder and decoder run the same bit-exact allocation function over the number of bits still unspent, and arrive at the same answer. Where MP3 sends scalefactors and AAC sends section data, Opus sends nothing and infers — the entropy coder's own state is the side information. That is only possible because the coder's consumption is a deterministic function of what has already been decoded, and it is why Opus's overhead at 20 ms frames is small enough to make very short frames practical at all.

Opus's own bitstream is taken apart in Chapter 09, and if you would like to read a decoder rather than a description, ropus elsewhere in this almanac is the reference decoder ported to safe Rust and checked bit-exact against the C.

What it is worth

Between a third and a half of the file — which is ten decibels.

The honest way to price a lossless stage is to hold the file size fixed and ask what the audio does. Give the same encoder the same number of bytes and let it spend them two ways: once with the entropy coder, once with a per-band fixed-width field of exactly the width the largest value in that band needs. The quantiser, the masking model, the transform and the noise shaping are identical. Only the accounting changes, and the accounting changes what the rate loop can afford.

Notice what the comparison does not say. It does not say entropy coding is where audio compression happens. The move from 1 411 kbit/s to 128 is overwhelmingly the work of the transform and the quantiser, and had already happened before this chapter began. What the entropy coder does is recover the difference between a description and an efficient description of the same thing — a factor of between about 1.6 and 2.4 on what remains, every time, for no perceptual cost whatever.

Every codec in this study spends its life lowering the entropy rather than beating the bound. The bound does not move. Why the transform comes first

Five things people get wrong about this stage

That Huffman coding damages the audio. It does not, and cannot. Entropy coding is a bijection between integer sequences and bit strings; the decoder recovers the encoder's integers exactly, on every sample of every frame. Everything audible that a codec does to your music happened in Chapter 04 and Chapter 05. If a file sounds wrong, the entropy coder is the one stage that can be ruled out.

That arithmetic coding is dramatically better than Huffman. On a well-conditioned alphabet it is worth a few per cent, not a factor. Its advantage becomes large in exactly two circumstances: when a single symbol takes more than half the probability mass, where Huffman's one-bit floor bites; and when the model is adaptive or context-conditioned, where an arithmetic coder can follow a probability that changes every symbol and a static table cannot. xHE-AAC's context-adaptive arithmetic coder, which conditions each 4-tuple of coefficients on neighbours in both frequency and the previous frame, is a good example of the second — the win comes from the context model at least as much as from the fractional bits.

That MP3 files carry their Huffman tables. They do not. All thirty-two big-value tables and both count1 tables are printed in ISO/IEC 11172-3, and a frame transmits only the 5-bit index of which one to use. Vorbis is the format that ships its codebooks, in the setup header, which is why a Vorbis file has a larger fixed overhead and why Vorbis encoders were able to keep improving without a format change.

That you can shrink an MP3 losslessly by recompressing it. Barely. Running a finished MP3 through zip gains almost nothing, because the payload is already close to incompressible — that is what the entropy coder achieved. Tools that repack the bitstream can recover a small percentage by re-optimising table selects and the bit reservoir, but they cannot change a single quantised value, and the ceiling is low.

That a zero costs nothing. A zero inside a coded region costs its codeword, which at a zero fraction of 0.4 is about 1.3 bits. Only a zero in a run-length region is free. This is why the ordering of the spectrum matters so much: MP3 and AAC both arrange their coefficients so that the zeros collect at the top, where a run length can dispose of them wholesale rather than one codeword at a time. For short blocks MP3 goes to the trouble of interleaving the three sub-block spectra back into frequency order precisely so that the run is contiguous.

That is the machinery complete. Chapter 01 turned pressure into numbers, 02 and 03 measured the listener, 04 moved the signal somewhere the measurement applies, 05 spent the bits, and this chapter wrote them down. Every lossy audio codec that has ever shipped is those five things in some order, under some constraint. The next four chapters take the important ones apart and show what each committee actually built, beginning with the one that took the whole apparatus out of the laboratory: MP3, where 576 lines, a 4-byte header, seventeen bytes of side information and a bit reservoir that points backwards past the frame boundary all have to be made to fit.
