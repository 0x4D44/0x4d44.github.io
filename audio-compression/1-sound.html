A sound is one number that changes, and there are far too many of them.

Sampling in time is free: below half the sample rate, the numbers are not an approximation of the waveform, they are the waveform, and it can be recovered exactly. Rounding those numbers to a finite grid is not free, and the way engineers bought their way out of that — by adding noise on purpose, and then by making the step size depend on the signal — is the first move of every codec in this study.

A pressure, and a number

Sound is a very small wobble in a very large pressure.

Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101 325 pascals. A conversation at one metre modulates that steady weight of air by roughly 0.02 Pa — two parts in ten million. The quietest sound a healthy young ear can detect at 1 kHz is smaller again: 20 micropascals, which the acoustics community fixed as the zero of its scale, and which is about one five-billionth of the pressure it sits on top of. Everything in this study is a way of writing that wobble down.

Because the wobble is a single number that varies with time, a microphone can be honest about it: one channel, one value of pressure per instant. Write the simplest possible case and every term in it has a name you will meet again.

p(t)=A sin(2πf t +φ) amplitude A in pascals · frequency f in hertz · phase φ in radians

Levels are quoted logarithmically because the usable range is enormous and the ear's response to it is closer to multiplicative than additive. Sound pressure level is referenced to that same 20 µPa:

That is a span of about 120 dB, a factor of a million in pressure, and it is the number every argument about bit depth eventually appeals to. It is also — as Chapter 02 shows — not a range you have available all at once.

Frequency has a geometric consequence worth carrying. A wave travels at about 343 metres per second in dry air at 20 °C, so its wavelength is

λ= cf = 17.16 m… 17.16 mm for 20 Hz and 20 kHz, with c = 343.2 m/s

A factor of a thousand across the audible band. That is why the bottom octave of a mix fills a room indiscriminately while the top octave behaves like light, and it is the reason the physical side of this story — instruments, rooms, microphones — is a different subject, handled elsewhere in this almanac. Here the signal has already arrived at the microphone, and the only question is how to write it down.

Zero on the scale

0 dB SPL is 20 micropascals. The corresponding eardrum displacement is usually quoted, after von Békésy, as around 10−11 m — smaller than a hydrogen atom. That figure is repeated everywhere and is worth treating as an order of magnitude rather than a measurement, but the order of magnitude is the point: the mechanism at the top of your neck resolves motions comparable with the size of an atom.

Fourier's promise

If you know what a system does to one sine, you know what it does to everything.

On 21 December 1807 Joseph Fourier presented a memoir on the conduction of heat to the Institut de France, containing the claim that an arbitrary function could be written as a sum of sines and cosines. Lagrange did not believe it and blocked publication. Fourier won the Academy's prize for the work in 1811 anyway, and the book — Théorie analytique de la chaleur — appeared in 1822, after Lagrange's death. The claim was not quite true as stated, and repairing it occupied a century of analysis. It is true enough for audio.

x(t)= a02 + ∑∞n=1 [an cos(2πn f0t) +bn sin(2πn f0t)] a periodic signal of period T = 1/f₀ is a sum of harmonics of f₀
X(f)= ∫x(t)e−j2πft dt , x(t)= ∫X(f)e+j2πft df and a signal that is not periodic is an integral of them, over all f

The reason this licenses everything downstream is narrower and more useful than "sines are a nice basis". A system that is linear and does not change with time cannot move energy from one frequency to another: feed it a sine and you get back the same sine, scaled and delayed. Sines are the eigenfunctions of every such system. A filter, a loudspeaker, a room, a length of cable — each is fully described by one complex number per frequency. Measure that and you have measured the whole device.

The catch, which Chapter 04 is largely about, is that this decomposition is exact only over infinite time. Music is not infinite and its spectrum will not hold still, so real coders chop it into blocks of a few tens of milliseconds and pay a well-defined price in frequency resolution for the privilege. The arithmetic of the fast Fourier transform that makes this affordable is taken apart, in C, in a separate write-up. For now, take the promise: every signal in this chapter is a sum of sinusoids, so arguing about one sinusoid is arguing about all of them.

The sampling theorem

The samples are not an approximation of the wave. Below half the rate, they are the wave.

Take the pressure at instants spaced T = 1/fs apart and keep nothing in between. The theorem says that if the signal has no energy at or above B hertz, and if

fs>2B strictly greater, not greater than or equal — the difference matters, see below

then those samples determine the original continuous signal completely. Not approximately, not to within some error term that shrinks as you add samples: exactly, with a closed-form reconstruction.

x(t)= ∑∞n=−∞ x[n]· sinc( t − nTT ), sinc(u)= sin(πu)πu Whittaker–Shannon interpolation: each sample contributes one sinc, and the sum is the unique band-limited function through all of them

The result has an unusually crowded set of parents. E. T. Whittaker gave the cardinal series in 1915; Nyquist derived the 2B figure for telegraph signalling at Bell Labs in 1928; Vladimir Kotelnikov stated and proved the theorem in the USSR in 1933, where it stayed unknown in the West for a decade; Herbert Raabe re-derived it independently in 1939; Shannon gave the modern statement with the interpolation formula in Communication in the Presence of Noise in 1949, the same year Someya published the equivalent in Japan. The literature calls it the WKS theorem when it wants to be fair and the Nyquist theorem when it wants to be brief.

Two vocabulary traps follow immediately. The Nyquist frequency is fs/2, a property of the sampler. The Nyquist rate is 2B, a property of the signal. They are different quantities that share a surname, and a great deal of confused writing comes from treating them as one. As for the strictness: sample sin(2πBt) at exactly 2B and every sample lands on a zero crossing. The reconstruction is silence. One line, and the case for the strict inequality is closed.

The staircase is a lie you have been shown a thousand times. It is a picture of a converter with its reconstruction filter deleted. On the standard illustration of digital audio

The figure is also the refutation of the most widespread image in the subject. Digital audio is not a staircase. The output of a reconstruction filter is the unique band-limited function passing through the samples, and it is smooth: there are no steps in it anywhere. The familiar blocky drawing is a picture of a zero-order-hold converter with its output filter removed — a real object, but a broken one, and not what any player does.

Folding

Energy above half the rate does not disappear. It comes back wearing a different frequency.

Sampling multiplies the signal by a train of impulses, and in the frequency domain that convolves the spectrum with a train of impulses spaced fs apart. The whole of aliasing is one line:

Xs(f)=fs ∑∞k=−∞ X(f − k fs) the spectrum, copied at every multiple of the sample rate

A real tone at f0 therefore appears at every |k fs ± f0|. The reconstruction filter keeps only the copy inside the baseband, and which copy that is falls out of simple arithmetic:

fa= |f − fs round(f/fs) | a triangle wave in f, period fs, reflecting off 0 and fs/2

The fold carries a detail that is usually left out. Write f = m·fs + r. If r ≤ fs/2 the samples are those of a tone at r with the phase intact. If r > fs/2 they are those of a tone at fs − r with the sign of the phase flipped. A folded component is not merely mistuned, it is mirror-imaged; the readout in Fig 1.1 says which of the two you are listening to. This is also why a rising sweep appears to descend once it crosses the Nyquist frequency, and why aliased harmonics of a bright instrument sound so wrong: they do not land on a musical interval and they move the wrong way when the note moves.

The cure is a filter before the sampler, and the cost of that filter is the reason sample rates are the numbers they are. Keep audio to 20 kHz and demand about 100 dB of rejection by the Nyquist frequency, and the transition band at each rate is:

Why 44,100

44,100 is a videotape number, and 20 kHz of audio bandwidth is an accident of television.

The usual story — twice twenty kilohertz, plus a little margin for the filter — is backwards. In the 1970s no affordable machine could store a megabit per second of anything, with one exception: the video recorder, built to lay down five megahertz of luminance on tape every second of the day. So the first digital audio recorders were PCM adaptors, boxes that turned samples into a fake television picture — black and white pulses ranged along the active lines — and handed it to an ordinary video machine.

A given adaptor packs a fixed number of samples into each active line. Three per channel per line was what the format could carry reliably, and then the sample rate is arithmetic on the television raster:

3×245 ×60= 44 100 NTSC: 3 samples per line, 245 active lines per field, 60 fields per second
3×294 ×50= 44 100 PAL: 294 active lines per field, 50 fields per second — the same answer

44,100 was the highest rate that both television systems could carry at three samples per line, which made a tape recorded in Tokyo playable in Hilversum. Colour NTSC runs its fields at 60000/1001 Hz rather than 60, so American machines locked to colour produced 3 × 245 × 59.94 = 44,055.94 — call it 44,056 — and sample-rate converters between 44,056 and 44,100 were a real product with a real market. The audio bandwidth of roughly 20 kHz is a consequence of that raster, not the cause of it: twenty kilohertz is an accident of the American and European television standards.

Everything else in the rate zoo has a similarly concrete parent: a telephone channel, a broadcast standard, a doubling of something older.

Quantisation

Time was free. Amplitude is where the loss actually starts.

A sample is a real number and a file holds integers, so every sample is rounded to the nearest of a finite set of levels. With N bits spanning a normalised range of −1 to +1, the step and the bound on the error are immediate:

q= 21−N , Q(x)=q round(x/q) , |e| ≤ q2 for 16 bits, q = 1/32 768 = 30.5 µV on a ±1 V converter

Assume for a moment that the error is equally likely to be anywhere in that band and uncorrelated with the signal — an assumption this section will then attack. Its mean power is the variance of a uniform distribution:

σe2= 1q ∫e2 de = q212 , erms= 0.2887q integrated over −q/2 … +q/2

A full-scale sine has amplitude 2N−1q and therefore power 22Nq2/8. Divide, take ten times the logarithm, and the most quoted equation in digital audio falls out in one line:

SNR= 10log10 (3/2) +20N log102 =6.02N +1.76 dB 6.0205999 dB is one bit; the mysterious 1.7609 dB is 10·log₁₀(3/2), the crest factor of a sine and nothing else

Both constants deserve to be demystified. The 6.02 is 20·log102: one more bit halves the step, which is 6.02 dB less error, and that is the entire content of "one bit is six decibels". The 1.76 is not a fudge — it is the 3/2 that appears when you divide the sine's mean power by the uniform error's, and it would be a different number for a different waveform. Which is exactly what the second panel of the next figure measures.

997, not 1000

Audio measurements are made at 997 Hz rather than 1 kHz because 997 is prime, so it refuses to line up with any common sample rate or FFT bin spacing. A 1 kHz tone at 48 kHz repeats every 48 samples and visits the same few quantiser levels forever, which makes the error look tidier and the distortion look smaller than they are. The figure above uses 997 Hz for exactly that reason.

The last two rows need a caveat that the arithmetic does not supply. No converter reaches 144 dB. A good 24-bit audio ADC delivers something like 20 to 21 effective bits — around 120 to 126 dB — and the limit is thermal noise in the analogue front end, not the number of bits in the register. About 130 dB is the practical ceiling for any analogue chain. Twenty-four bits is a production format: room to make a gain mistake and still have a master.

At the other end, 0 dBFS is not the loudest signal a file can imply. The reconstructed waveform passes through the samples but is free to overshoot between them, so a stream whose samples all sit at −0.2 dBFS can produce an analogue peak above full scale and clip a downstream converter or encoder. That is what ITU-R BS.1770's true-peak meter measures by oversampling the signal four times before looking for the maximum, and why delivery specifications quote a ceiling in dBTP — EBU R 128 uses −1 dBTP. Floating point sidesteps the issue differently: IEEE-754 binary32 carries 24 significand bits at every exponent, so its signal-to-noise ratio is 146 dB whether the sample is at full scale or 300 dB below it, over a range of roughly 1 529 dB. That is why mixing happens in float and delivery does not.

Dither

The cure for rounding is to add noise on purpose, before you round.

The uniform-error model has a condition attached that textbooks state once and then forget: the error behaves like noise only when the signal is busy and uncorrelated with the step grid. For a steady tone, a slow fade, a reverb tail or anything approaching silence, the error is a deterministic function of the signal. That is not noise, it is distortion — harmonics that move as the level moves, which is precisely the kind of artefact hearing evolved to notice. Fig 1.2's error trace at four bits is a repeating sawtooth locked to the waveform, not a random scatter.

The repair is counter-intuitive and completely standard: add a small random signal before rounding, so that whether a given sample rounds up or down becomes a coin toss whose bias carries the information. The rounding error is then decorrelated from the signal, and the average of many samples is exactly right even when every individual sample is wrong. What it costs is noise power.

RPDF: d=(U1−0.5)q →+3.01 dB one LSB peak-to-peak; kills the mean of the error, but the error's variance still tracks the signal — audible as "noise modulation"
TPDF: d=(U1−U2)q →+4.77 dB two LSB peak-to-peak; 10·log₁₀3. Makes both the mean and the variance of the error independent of the signal — the standard choice

Triangular dither is the industry default because of that second line: two independent rectangular sources subtracted give a triangular distribution two LSBs wide, and with it neither the first nor the second moment of the error depends on what the signal is doing. The price is 10·log103 = 4.77 dB of extra noise, taking a 16-bit stream's full-scale-sine signal-to-noise ratio from 98.1 dB down to 93.3 dB. In exchange the quantiser becomes, statistically, perfectly linear.

Then comes the move that prefigures the whole study. The noise has to go somewhere, but it does not have to be spread evenly across the band. Feed the rounding error back into the input with a sign and a delay and the noise acquires a shape:

v[n]=x[n] +e[n−1] , NTF(z)= 1−z−1 first-order error feedback: total noise power doubles (+3.01 dB) and tilts upward at 6 dB per octave

Every decibel the shaper removes from the bottom of the band it puts back, with interest, at the top. A first-order shaper makes twice the total noise power; the second-order version used in production, with NTF = (1 − z−1)2, makes six times the noise power — 7.78 dB more — and still sounds quieter, because it moves the noise above 15 kHz where the ear has largely stopped paying attention. How much quieter depends entirely on which weighting curve you believe; the commercial shapers (Sony's Super Bit Mapping, Apogee UV22, the POW-R family) all differ, and improvements of 12 to 20 dB in perceived floor are claimed for them.

Noise-shaped dither makes several times as much noise power and sounds quieter. That sentence, generalised, is the whole of the rest of this study. Why Chapter 02 is about the ear

That last demonstration disposes of another piece of folklore. A signal smaller than one quantisation step is not lost. With correct dither a −110 dBFS sine in a 16-bit stream is still there, statistically exact in the mean, under a total noise power some fifteen decibels louder — which is a modest amount of averaging away from being recovered, and audible outright once your own listening floor is low enough. This is the point at which quantisation stops being an accounting problem and becomes a perceptual one, and it is where Chapter 02 takes over.

The bill

Raw audio is not slightly too big. It is an order of magnitude too big, and always has been.

With the representation settled, the cost is a multiplication:

R=channels× fs×bits =2× 44 100×16 =1 411 200 bit/s 176 400 bytes every second; 10.584 MB a minute; 635.04 MB an hour

A 74-minute disc is therefore 783,216,000 bytes of audio — 746.93 MiB, not the "749.9 MB" that careless sources produce by mixing decimal and binary prefixes. The same disc, formatted for computer data, holds only 681,984,000 bytes, because a data sector spends 304 of its 2 352 bytes on error detection and correction that music can do without. A wrong bit in a program is fatal; a wrong bit in a cymbal is inaudible.

The inequality that started it

Six channels at 48 kHz and 24 bits is 6,912,000 bit/s. DVD-Video's linear-PCM ceiling is 6,144,000 bit/s. The specification could not carry its own reference format, which is why the disc in your hand shipped Dolby Digital and DTS instead — and why Chapter 08 opens where it does.

The first escape

G.711 halved the problem in 1972 by making the step size depend on the signal.

Long before anyone modelled masking, the telephone network had already found the first crack in uniform quantisation. A linear 8-bit quantiser has a fixed step, so its signal-to-noise ratio is 49.9 dB for a full-scale sine and falls one decibel for every decibel the talker backs away from the microphone: at −40 dBFS it is about 10 dB, which is unusable. But the ear does not judge error against full scale. It judges error against the signal that is present. A quiet passage needs small steps; a loud one does not.

Recommendation G.711, approved by the CCITT in 1972, is that observation turned into eight bits: 8 000 samples a second, a 300–3 400 Hz passband, 64 kbit/s, and a logarithmic transfer curve in one of two flavours.

F(x)= sgn(x) ln(1 + µ|x|)ln(1 + µ) ,µ=255 μ-law, used in North America and Japan
F(x)= sgn(x) 1 + ln(A|x|)1 + lnA ,A=87.6 A-law, used in Europe; linear below |x| = 1/A, logarithmic above it

No implementation evaluates a logarithm per sample. Both laws are approximated by a piecewise-linear curve — fifteen distinct slopes for μ-law, thirteen for A-law — and the code that comes out is not really an integer at all:

One last flourish: the byte is inverted before it goes on the line — all eight bits for μ-law, the even bits for A-law — so that a silent channel transmits a dense pattern of ones rather than a long run of zeros, and the receiver keeps clock recovery.

The gain is real but modest — the usual figures are about 24 dB of effective companding advantage for A-law and around 33 dB, five and a half bits, for μ-law — and it costs a 2 : 1 compression against linear 16-bit. What matters is not the ratio but the sentence underneath it: quantise in proportion to the signal, because the ear measures error relative to the signal. Every codec in the rest of this study is a more sophisticated version of that sentence. MP3 and AAC scale each band of each block by a scalefactor and then quantise with a three-quarter power law, which is the same idea applied per frequency band rather than per sample (Chapter 05). What G.711 lacks is any notion of which error the listener will actually hear — it treats all frequencies alike, and spends bits on a 3 kHz hiss that a 300 Hz vowel would have hidden completely.

What people get wrong

Six things that are said constantly and are not true.

Most of the confusion in this subject comes from a small number of claims that are repeated until they sound like background knowledge. Each one below is corrected by something already on this page.

"Digital audio is a staircase"
The reconstruction filter's output is the unique band-limited function through the samples, and it is smooth. Fig 1.1 computes it, sample by sample, from the sinc sum. The staircase is a picture of a zero-order-hold converter with its filter removed.
"44.1 kHz limits timing to ±11.3 µs"
Timing resolution is set by the signal-to-noise ratio, not by the sample spacing. A delay of a fraction of a sample is encoded in the amplitudes of neighbouring samples and survives exactly; the binaural threshold of around 10 µs is nowhere near a constraint on 44.1 kHz. More bits do not buy finer timing either — bit depth sets the noise floor and nothing else.
"Quantisation error is white noise"
Only when the signal is busy and uncorrelated with the step grid. For tones, fades and reverb tails it is a deterministic function of the signal, with harmonics that move as the level changes. Dither is what makes the textbook assumption true, and Fig 1.3 shows the harmonics appearing and vanishing as you switch it.
"Signals below one LSB are lost"
Not with dither. Set Fig 1.3 to 8 bits and −70 dBFS — thirty decibels below the least significant bit — and the tone is still measured at its correct level. Undithered, it is gone.
"16 bits gives 96 dB, so 24 are needed for the ear's 120 dB"
Three different quantities are being mixed. 96.33 dB is peak-to-LSB; the dithered signal-to-noise ratio is 93.3 dB; the ear's 120 dB is a lifetime range, not one available in a single instant in a room with a noise floor. Real 24-bit converters reach 120–126 dB anyway. In 2007 Meyer and Moran reported that inserting a 16-bit/44.1 kHz analogue-to-digital-to-analogue loop into high-resolution playback was undetectable to their listeners at normal levels; the loop's noise became audible only at grossly elevated gain.
"Higher sample rates are always better"
It is a trade, not a verdict. Faster sampling allows a gentler anti-alias filter and shorter pre-ringing, which are genuine advantages of 88.2 and 96 kHz. Against that, the same converter measures worse for distortion and noise at 192 kHz than at 96 kHz, and ultrasonic content can intermodulate down into the audible band in amplifiers and tweeters. Dan Lavry has argued the optimum sits nearer 60 kHz.

What survives all this is a representation with known, exact costs. Two channels, 44 100 samples a second, sixteen bits: 1 411 200 bits every second, of which the ear will notice a small and highly structured fraction. Nothing so far has removed a single bit on perceptual grounds — the only thing that has come close is G.711, which chose its step sizes to suit a listener rather than a meter, and got a factor of two for it.

To do better you have to answer a question this chapter never asks: given this particular instant of this particular music, how much error can be added at each frequency before anyone can tell? That question has a measurable answer, worked out over a century of psychoacoustics, and it is the subject of Chapter 02 — where the 120 dB range quoted above turns out to be nothing like available all at once, the ear turns out to be a filterbank with about two dozen usable channels, and the absolute threshold of hearing becomes the first curve a real encoder carries a copy of.
