Every failure of a codec has a name and a mechanism.

Ten chapters have argued about how encoders decide what to discard. This one is about what it sounds like when they decide wrongly — and the artefacts are not vague impressions of "digital harshness". Each has a specific cause in the machinery, a characteristic frequency range, a characteristic programme that provokes it, and a name that practitioners have used for thirty years. Once you can hear one on demand, you cannot stop hearing it.

Why they have names

A codec does not degrade gracefully. It fails in specific ways.

Analogue media degrade along one axis. Tape adds hiss, wow and flutter; vinyl adds surface noise and tracking distortion; both get worse in proportion to how badly you treat them. A perceptual codec is not like that. It is a machine that is right almost all the time and then, under a specific and identifiable circumstance, catastrophically wrong for a few milliseconds — because a model of your hearing was consulted and returned the wrong answer.

That is why artefacts are enumerable. Each corresponds to an assumption in chapter 03 or chapter 04 being violated: the masking model assumes a steady signal within the analysis window, so a transient breaks it; it assumes the spectrum is smooth enough to be described band by band, so an isolated tone in an otherwise empty band breaks it; it assumes the two channels can be described by one signal plus a balance, so a decorrelated stereo image breaks it.

Everything below is produced here, live, by the small perceptual coder described on the contents page: an MDCT, Bark-band masking, a rate loop and an entropy estimate. It is cruder than LAME or libopus, so it produces these artefacts at higher bitrates than a real codec would — which is exactly what makes it a good teaching instrument. The mechanisms are identical; only the threshold at which they become audible differs.

How to listen

Use headphones. Every one of these artefacts lives 20 to 60 dB below the music, and a laptop speaker in a normal room will mask them exactly as a codec would — you will hear nothing and conclude, wrongly, that there is nothing to hear. Play the clean version first and fix it in your memory; auditory memory for timbre is short, on the order of a few seconds, which is the entire reason the ABX design below exists.

Pre-echo: noise that arrives before the sound that caused it.

This is the defining artefact of transform coding and the one with the most elegant explanation. The transform sees a block of 2N samples at once. If that block contains a castanet strike, the quantisation noise the encoder adds is spread — by construction, because a transform coefficient describes the whole block — evenly across the entire window. The signal is not spread evenly: it is silence, then a crack. So for the tens of milliseconds before the attack, there is noise and nothing to hide it.

Chapter 03 gave the number that turns this into an audible fault. Backward masking — a sound hiding a noise that preceded it — extends only about 2 to 5 milliseconds, and even that much is disputed and trainable. Forward masking runs for 100 to 200 ms, which is why you never hear the noise after the crack. A 1024-line MDCT at 44.1 kHz covers about 46 ms. The mismatch is roughly a factor of ten.

Twindow= 2Nfs = 204844 100 ≈46 ms ≫ Tpre-mask≈2–5 ms the whole of block switching, temporal noise shaping and gain control exists to close this gap

Birdies: the sound of a spectral line that cannot make up its mind.

At low bitrates the rate loop has to zero whole bands. A band that sits close to the threshold of being worth coding will be kept in one block and dropped in the next, as the music moves by a decibel. The result is a component that switches on and off at the block rate — 43 times a second for a 1024-line transform at 44.1 kHz — and the ear, which is very good at detecting amplitude modulation in that range, hears a warbling, watery, chirping quality.

It is worst between roughly 8 and 14 kHz, for two reasons that compound. The bands are wide up there, so dropping one removes a lot at once; and it is the region where cymbals, sibilance and room noise put a thin, noise-like, low-level spectrum — exactly the material that hovers around the decision boundary. The name is old and exactly right: a flock of small birds.

The ceiling: what a lowpass costs, and why it is the cheap way out.

Every lossy encoder has a frequency above which it simply stops. Early MP3 encoders put that wall at about 16 kHz and were mocked for it; the mockery was misplaced, because a clean lowpass is one of the most benign things you can do to music. Nothing flickers, nothing warbles, the phase relationships below the cut are untouched — you have simply removed the top part of the top octave, where there is very little musical energy and where the absolute threshold is climbing steeply.

The alternative uses of those bits are worth far more. That is the whole economics of the decision: an encoder at 128 kbit/s that spends nothing above 16 kHz has bought itself perhaps 8 per cent more bits for everything below, where you can actually hear the difference.

Stereo collapse: the image falls into the middle of your head.

Coding two channels separately wastes bits, because most recordings are highly correlated between left and right. Mid/side exploits that losslessly — send (L+R) and (L−R) instead — and costs nothing. Intensity stereo goes much further: above some crossover frequency it sends one signal plus a per-band left/right balance, and discards the phase relationship between the channels entirely.

The saving is large and the price is specific. Interaural time differences — the dominant localisation cue below about 1.5 kHz, and a strong contributor to the sense of space above it — are carried in exactly the phase relationship intensity stereo throws away. What the ear hears is not a loss of loudness but a loss of width: reverberation that used to surround you shrinks to a line between the speakers, and on headphones the whole mix moves to a point in the middle of your skull.

You cannot trust yourself, so do not. Run the test.

Everything in the preceding sections was presented with labels on it, and a labelled comparison is worthless. Knowing which button you pressed is enough to make you hear a difference that is not there, reliably, in yourself, no matter how well you understand the effect. The only remedy is to take the label away.

ABX is the minimal honest design. You may hear A as often as you like, and B as often as you like, and then X, which is one of them chosen at random. You say which. Do that n times and count. Under the null hypothesis — that you are guessing — the number correct is binomial with p = ½, so the probability of doing at least as well as you did by luck alone is exact and easy:

p= ∑ni=c n!i!(n−i)! ·2−n 12 of 16 gives p = 0.038; 8 of 8 gives p = 0.0039; 11 of 16 gives p = 0.105, which is not evidence of anything
How the industry measures

Three methods, each for a different size of impairment.

ABX answers one question — can you tell? — and says nothing about how much you mind. Standards bodies need a number, and have three of them, chosen by how badly damaged the signal is expected to be.

ITU-R BS.1116
For small impairments — codecs expected to be near transparent. A double-blind triple-stimulus test with a hidden reference: the listener gets a labelled reference and two others, one of which is the reference again, and grades the impairment of each on a continuous five-point scale. The reported figure is the diffgrade: the grade given to the system minus the grade given to the hidden reference, so a listener who cannot tell scores zero by construction. It demands expert listeners and a controlled room, and it is slow.
ITU-R BS.1534 (MUSHRA)
For intermediate quality — the 32 to 96 kbit/s range where impairments are plainly audible and you want to rank systems. Several systems are presented at once on a 0–100 scale against a labelled reference, with a hidden reference and low-pass anchors, conventionally at 3.5 kHz and 7 kHz, mixed in among them. The anchors are the clever part: a listener who rates the hidden reference below 90, or the 3.5 kHz anchor above the systems, is not listening carefully, and their data can be excluded on a stated rule rather than on the experimenter's judgement.
ITU-R BS.1387 (PEAQ)
For when you cannot convene a panel. An objective model: run reference and test through an ear model, extract a set of output variables — modulation difference, noise-to-mask ratio, bandwidth, harmonic structure of the error — and map them, through a small trained neural network, to an Objective Difference Grade from 0 (imperceptible) to −4 (very annoying). Extremely useful as a regression test on an encoder you are developing. Not a substitute for listeners, and it can be gamed by any artefact its ear model does not represent.
What does not work, and why it keeps being tried

Subtracting the decoded file from the original and looking at the residual — the "null test" — is intuitive and almost entirely uninformative for a perceptual codec, because the codec's whole job is to put energy where you cannot hear it. A large residual that is correctly shaped is inaudible; a small residual in the wrong band is not. Signal-to-noise ratio fails for the same reason, and fails in a specific direction: switch this study's own encoder from a masking threshold to a flat one and its measured SNR improves by about eleven decibels while the result plainly sounds worse. That is not a quirk of a toy — it is why perceptual coding needed a new measurement discipline before it could have an engineering discipline.

What listening tests are guarding against

Mostly, they are guarding against the listener.

The reason all of this machinery exists — hidden references, anchors, randomised presentation, statistical exclusion rules — is that sighted listening produces confident, consistent, strongly held results that do not survive blinding. This is not a statement about other people's credulity. It is a measured property of human perception: expectation changes what is heard, not merely what is reported, and it does so in trained listeners with equipment they know well.

The best-known example in this field is the high-resolution audio question. Meyer and Moran's 2007 experiment inserted a 16-bit/44.1 kHz analogue-to-digital-to-analogue loop into the playback path of SACD and DVD-Audio systems, in real listening rooms, with listeners including audio professionals — and found that under blind conditions the loop could not be detected. Later meta-analyses have argued for a small effect under some conditions, and the debate is not closed; but the direction of the correction is always the same, and it is always downward from what sighted listening reported.

Every claim in this study that could be tested by ear has been tested by ear, by somebody, under blinding — and a good many claims that were once made confidently did not survive it. The reason chapter 11 exists at all

Which is the practical advice this chapter ends on. When you want to know whether a bitrate is good enough, do not reason about it and do not read about it. Run the test in figure 11.5 on your own ears, with your own headphones, on the hardest programme you own. You will get a number, it will be specific to you, and it will almost certainly be lower than you expect. Chapter 12 is about what to do with that number.
