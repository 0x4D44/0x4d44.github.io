First, a category error

A file extension tells you the box, not what is in it.

"Convert it to Ogg" is not a statement about quality, because Ogg is a container and can hold Vorbis, Opus, FLAC or Speex. "Send me an MP4" does not say whether the audio is AAC-LC at 96 kbit/s or ALAC lossless. The codec decides what the bits mean; the container decides how they are framed, timed, interleaved with video and labelled with metadata. Almost every compatibility problem in practice is a container problem wearing a codec's clothes.

The rule that resolves most of it

Name the codec and the bitrate, never the extension. "AAC-LC 256 kbit/s in MP4" is a specification. "An M4A" is a guess.

The codec you are actually listening through is one you never chose.

If the sound leaves your device over Bluetooth, everything upstream is re-encoded. A 256 kbit/s AAC stream from a music service is decoded to PCM and then re-encoded by the phone into whatever codec the headphones negotiated — which is the single largest quality decision in most listening chains, and it is made by a handshake nobody sees. It is also a transcode, with everything the next section says about that.

The peak you cannot see is the one that breaks the encode.

A PCM file's samples can all sit below full scale while the waveform they describe does not. Chapter 01 made the point that samples are not the signal — the signal is the band-limited curve that passes through them — and that curve can overshoot between samples. A file peaking at −0.1 dBFS by sample can reconstruct to well over 0 dBFS in the converter, and a lossy encoder, which does not reproduce the samples exactly, can push it further still.

x(t)= ∑n x[n]· sinc(fst−n) the true peak is the maximum of this, not of the samples — it is measured by oversampling, typically by four

Loudness normalisation is the other half of this. Since ITU-R BS.1770 gave the industry a measure of programme loudness that actually correlates with perception — a K-weighted, gated mean expressed in LUFS — and EBU R 128 turned it into a broadcast requirement, every major streaming service has adopted a target in the region of −14 LUFS and turns everything down to meet it. The practical consequence is that mastering a track louder no longer makes it louder to the listener; it only makes it more distorted once the service has turned it back down. The loudness war did not end by agreement. It ended because the scoreboard was replaced.

Every re-encode is a fresh set of decisions, made on damaged evidence.

Lossy coding is not idempotent. Decode a coded file and you get a waveform that is close to the original but carries shaped quantisation noise; encode that, and the new encoder's psychoacoustic model sees the noise as signal, spends bits on it, and adds its own noise on top. The two codecs' band edges and block boundaries do not line up either, so the second coder cannot even find the first one's damage to leave it alone.

The rule

Encode from the highest-quality source you have, once, at the moment of delivery. Never archive a lossy file as a working master; never "upgrade" a 128 kbit/s MP3 by re-encoding it at 320, which adds a generation of loss and a great deal of file size in exchange for precisely nothing. If you must transcode — and a Bluetooth link means you always are — give the intermediate as much headroom as the pipeline can afford.

What comes next

Two directions, only one of which is really new.

The first is the continuation of the story this study has told: more parametric description, less coding. xHE-AAC (USAC) switches between a transform path and an ACELP speech path inside one bitstream and stays useful well below where AAC-LC gives up; MPEG-H 3D Audio and the object-based systems code sound sources plus positions rather than channels, so that the renderer makes the final mix for whatever the listener is wearing. Both are the same move MP3 made — shift work from the bitstream to a model at the decoder — applied one level higher.

The second is genuinely different. Learned codecs — Google's Lyra, SoundStream, Meta's EnCodec, the Descript Audio Codec — replace the hand-designed analysis chain with an encoder network, a residual vector quantiser and a decoder network trained end to end, often with an adversarial loss so the output is judged on whether it sounds like speech rather than on whether it matches sample-for-sample. At a few kilobits per second, where every classical codec produces something unbearable, they produce speech that is clear and natural.

What they do not yet do is beat a well-tuned classical codec on music at the rates anybody actually uses. There are structural reasons to expect that to be hard: a learned decoder is free to synthesise something plausible rather than something faithful, which is exactly what you want at 3 kbit/s and exactly what you do not want at 128. And the honest accounting has to include the cost — a neural decoder is orders of magnitude more computation than a CELT decode, which is why the shipped applications so far are ones where bandwidth is scarcer than silicon.

The interesting middle

The likelier near future is not replacement but grafting: a classical codec doing the faithful work, with a learned post-filter repairing the artefacts it knows it left behind. That keeps the bitstream cheap, the decoder optional, and the failure mode graceful — and it is already happening at the low end of speech coding.

What to actually do

Six recommendations, and the reason for each.

Archive lossless
FLAC, at the original rate and depth. It is free, open, checksummed, decodes on everything and costs about half the space of PCM. Whatever delivery format is fashionable in ten years, you will be able to make it from this without a generation of loss.
Deliver Opus where you can
It is better than AAC at every rate it competes at, royalty-free, handles speech and music without being told which, and is in every current browser. The only real objection is old hardware.
Deliver AAC-LC where you cannot
At 128–192 kbit/s stereo it is comfortably good, and it decodes on essentially everything made since about 2005. This is the compatibility answer, not the quality answer.
Use MP3 only for compatibility, and then use LAME
If it must be MP3, -V2 or better from a current LAME is a different animal from the encoders that gave the format its reputation. Do not use a 1998 encoder to make a 2026 file.
Leave a decibel of true-peak headroom
Master to about −1 dBTP. It costs nothing audible, and it is the difference between a clean encode and one that clips in the decoder on somebody else's phone.
Stop worrying about the rest
Above about 160 kbit/s in a modern codec, the playback chain, the room and the listener dominate the codec by a wide margin. The bits you would spend going from 192 to 320 are better spent on a better master — or on nothing at all.

That is the study. It began with a number — 1 411 200 bits a second — and an observation that almost all of it is spent on detail you are not equipped to hear. Everything since has been the machinery for finding out which detail, and the discipline for throwing away exactly that much and no more.
