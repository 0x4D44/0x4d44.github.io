At a glance

The stinging nettle is the most familiar plant almost nobody looks at closely. It grows where we throw things away, it punishes the careless hand — and hidden inside that sting is a self-loading glass syringe, a deep-time family secret, and a thousand years of soup, cloth, medicine and myth.

Anatomy

The parts of a nettle

A hedgerow reference

The nettle carries its own identity kit: opposite toothed leaves, a grooved (not truly square) stem, drooping green flower-tassels, and — under a lens — the hollow glass hairs that give it its name. Its bright-yellow creeping roots are unmistakable once you've dug one up.

Tip: the classic lookalike, white dead-nettle (Lamium album), copies the leaves but has a genuinely square stem, big white flowers, and no sting at all — it isn't even in the nettle family.

The sting · the centrepiece

A glass hypodermic, self-loaded

The sting is not a chemical splash — it is a machine. Each stinging hair, or trichome, is a single hollow cell drawn out into a needle and stiffened with silica: literally glass, the same silicon dioxide as a window pane. It ends in a tiny round bead. Brush against it and the bead snaps off at a pre-scored line of weakness, leaving a bevelled, needle-sharp point — the same oblique tip a factory grinds onto a medical hypodermic. The point pierces your skin; the same motion squeezes the swollen base, and a cocktail of chemicals is injected up the hollow shaft. One gesture does both. Touch it below.

What gets injected

"Grasp the nettle"

The old proverb is real botany. The hairs are brittle and angled; a light brush lets them snap and inject, but a firm grasp, moving with the lie of the hairs, flattens them before they can break the skin. Playwright Aaron Hill fixed it in verse around 1750: "Tender-handed stroke a nettle, / And it stings you for your pains; / Grasp it like a man of mettle, / And it soft as silk remains." Try both modes above.

Myth

"It's the formic acid — like an ant."

The school-textbook answer, and largely wrong. Formic acid is present only in traces; in tests, even 10% formic acid barely stung. The immediate itch is histamine, acetylcholine and serotonin; the long, dull burn is now pinned on oxalic and tartaric acids (Fu et al., Annals of Botany, 2006) — the acid families of rhubarb leaves and wine.

Also a myth

"Rub a dock leaf on it."

Comforting, but it isn't chemistry. Dock sap is acidic too, so it neutralises nothing; in trials a dock leaf worked no better than a lettuce leaf. Any relief is cool sap evaporating, plus the placebo of doing something.

Why it stings

A weapon aimed at mammals, not bugs

The sting is an anti-grazing defence, and its target is you and the cattle and deer — not insects, whose small mouthparts slip between the hairs. The evidence is beautifully direct. On the Japanese nettle Urtica thunbergiana, deer avoid the hairiest plants and browse bald ones hardest. Clip a nettle and it grows back hairier: the defence is inducible, tuned to how much it's being eaten.

The most striking proof is in Nara Park, Japan, where sika deer have been protected as sacred for some 1,200 years. Under that unrelenting browsing pressure, the local nettles have evolved dramatically denser stings than deer-free populations nearby — evolution you can measure against a temple's calendar.

And here is the twist: the sting is also a nursery. Because grazers stay away, a nettle patch is one of the safest places a caterpillar can grow. Over 100 invertebrate species live on nettles, and five of Britain's showiest butterflies — the small tortoiseshell, peacock, red admiral, comma and painted lady — raise their young in silken tents on the leaves. The weapon that keeps the cow off keeps the caterpillar's enemies off too.

A garden starter-pistol. In early spring, nettle-only aphids explode on the fresh growth and become the first big meal for waking ladybirds — kick-starting the whole garden food chain weeks before anything else is going.

How it grows

A year in the life

Nettle plays both hands of reproduction: it seeds prolifically and creeps clonally on bright-yellow underground runners. Drag through the year to watch a patch wake, flower, seed and die back.

The plant that marks a lost farmstead

Nettle is a nitrogen-and-phosphate junkie — an Ellenberg nitrogen value of 8 out of 9. It craves the enriched, disturbed ground left by dung heaps, middens, byres and buried bone. So a dense stand of nettles in the middle of nowhere is often an archaeological marker: it grows over the phosphate ghost of a vanished building, farmyard or settlement. A single sunlit shoot can set 10,000–20,000 seeds; the clone also shoulders outward about half a metre a year. Belt and braces.

What it developed from

The nettle's surprising cousins

Molecular botany rearranged the family album. The old order "Urticales" was dissolved into an expanded Rosales — so the nettle now sits among the urticalean rosids, cheek by jowl with some very famous plants. Its closest kin are the figs and mulberries; a branch over are hops and cannabis; and further still, the elms. Hover the tree.

The sting was invented a dozen times over

Within the whole nettle family the stinging hair evolved just once, in the tribe Urticeae. But zoom out to all flowering plants and it's a textbook case of convergent evolution: the silica-needle-over-a-reservoir design arose independently at least a dozen times across roughly seven unrelated families. Some, in the Loasaceae, even tip their needles with hydroxyapatite — the mineral of your own tooth enamel and bone.

A genome doubling tracks the sting. The common stinging nettle is a tetraploid (2n = 52); its near-stingless, fen-dwelling relative U. galeopsifolia is a diploid (2n = 26). And despite the name dioica ("two houses" — separate male and female plants), true separate sexes are actually the exception across the genus, not the rule.

A thousand uses

Food, drink, cloth and compost

Cook, dry or crush a nettle and the brittle hairs shatter and the venom breaks down — the weapon becomes one of the most useful plants of the temperate world. It has fed, clothed, watered and fertilised people for millennia.

Food & drink

  • Nettle soup & porridge — a classic spring tonic; Samuel Pepys ate it in 1661 and called it "very good."
  • Nettle beer bittered ale before hops took over in the 1600s. Fresh nettle cordial turns bright pink and tastes of apricot.
  • Cornish Yarg — a cheese hand-wrapped in nettle leaves. "Yarg" is just "Gray" spelled backwards, after the couple who revived it.
  • Forage the young spring tops only — after flowering, gritty calcium cystoliths form that can irritate the kidneys.

Fibre & cloth

  • Nettle is a true bast fibre, kin to ramie — "the linen of the poor," once woven into Scots cloth, sheets and tablecloths.
  • A 2,800-year-old Danish burial cloth was found to be nettle, not flax — and likely imported ~1,000 km from the Austrian Alps: Bronze Age luxury fabric.
  • In WWI, blockaded of cotton, Germany harvested 1,300+ tonnes of nettles in a year for Nesseltuch uniform cloth.
  • Revived today: the Defra-backed STING project spun nettle into Oeko-Tex-certified upholstery fabric with Camira.

Garden & dye

  • Nettle liquid feed — rot nettles in water for a fortnight for a free, nitrogen-rich fertiliser (roughly 2-0.5-1.5 NPK). Legendarily smelly; dilute 1:10.
  • The leftover sludge is a fine compost activator.
  • A traditional natural dye: leaves give greens and yellows; roots with alum give yellow; iron darkens it toward black.
  • A biodiversity magnet — leave a patch for the butterflies.

Ounce for ounce, it out-mineralises spinach

Blanched, a nettle is a low-calorie powerhouse: about 481 mg of calcium per 100 g — three to four times cooked spinach — with less oxalate blocking its absorption. It carries huge amounts of vitamin K and A, useful iron, and the dried leaf runs to a quarter or more protein by weight. It was a genuine spring tonic and famine food; it helped keep people alive in the Irish Great Famine.

Medicine

Old remedy, modern evidence

Nettle is one of the oldest plants in the Western pharmacopoeia — Hippocrates, Dioscorides, Galen and Pliny all wrote it up two millennia ago. Most folk claims are just that, but a few have survived real clinical trials. The crucial rule: root and leaf are different medicines. Get the part wrong and you're taking the wrong drug.

Root

The prostate one

Nettle root is an approved herbal medicine in Germany (Commission E) for the urinary symptoms of an enlarged prostate (BPH). It doesn't shrink the gland like a drug; it eases the obstruction via hormone-modulating lignans and a lectin that blocks a growth-factor signal. In a 558-patient double-blind trial, symptom scores fell from 19.8 to 11.8, and 81% improved versus 16% on placebo.

Safarinejad, 2005. Its key compound is now, ironically, sold to bodybuilders as a "testosterone booster" — same molecule, opposite marketing.

Leaf

The anti-inflammatory one

Nettle leaf extract genuinely blocks NF-κB, the master switch of inflammation, and behaves as a multi-target antihistamine. Evidence is real but modest for hayfever (freeze-dried leaf) and joint pain. And urtication — deliberately stinging arthritic joints, a folk trick since Roman times — actually beat placebo in a small controlled trial for thumb-base arthritis.

The "placebo" in that trial was white dead-nettle: it looks almost identical but can't sting — an elegant blind.

Evidence, honestly. Strongest: nettle-root for BPH symptoms. Plausible: leaf for allergy and joints. Purely traditional (no good trials): "blood-building" for anaemia, and boosting breast-milk. This is a page about a plant, not medical advice.

History & folklore

Ten thousand years of nettles

The nettle has shadowed human settlement for as long as there have been dung heaps to grow on. It has been spun into prehistoric "silk," flogged onto Roman legs, charmed against serpents, woven into fairy tales and eaten competitively in a Dorset pub.

c. 940–750 BC · Bronze Age Denmark

Buried in nettle "silk"

A cremated Dane at Lusehøj, Funen, was wrapped in fine cloth long assumed to be local flax. In 2012, crystal and fibre analysis proved it was nettle — and strontium isotopes suggested it was imported ~1,000 km from the Austrian Alps. Bronze Age haute couture.

1st century AD · Rome

Urtication, and a tall tale

Pliny the Elder records flogging numb limbs with nettles to restore warmth and ease pain. The claim that soldiers introduced a special "Roman nettle" to Britain to whip themselves warm, though, is a myth — debunked as far back as 1586.

10th century · Anglo-Saxon England

The Nine Herbs Charm

Nettle ("stiðe") is one of nine sacred plants in a healing charm from the Lacnunga that names the god Woden — a rare survival of English pagan verse, invoked against poison and serpents.

1838 · Copenhagen

The Wild Swans

In Hans Christian Andersen's tale, Elisa must weave shirts from churchyard nettles — crushing them barefoot, in total silence, for years — to free her eleven swan-brothers. Pain and self-sacrifice made literal.

1939–45 · Wartime Britain

Picked by the ton

Schoolchildren were sent to gather nettles by the hundred-ton, so their chlorophyll could be turned into green dye for army camouflage netting.

1986 → today · Marshwood, Dorset

The World Nettle Eating Championship

Born of a farmers' argument over the longest nettle, competitors now strip and eat the leaves from 2-foot raw stalks for an hour, mouths blackening. The record: 104 feet of nettles (Phil Thorne, 2018). No mouthwash allowed.

The dangerous cousins

The sting-o-meter

The European nettle is the gentle one. The same silica-needle blueprint, scaled up and re-armed in warmer parts of the world, produces plants that have killed livestock, hospitalised botanists, and inspired some of the grimmest folklore in nature. Venom in a shrub. In 2020, University of Queensland scientists found the gympie-gympie's agony comes from gympietides — mini-proteins folded into the same "inhibitor cystine knot" as spider and cone-snail venoms, jamming the human pain channel NaV1.7 open. A plant independently evolved a neurotoxin. Its hairs can stay in your skin six months, and a 100-year-old museum specimen can still sting.

If you get stung

What actually helps

Don't rub it — you'll break more hairs and press the chemicals in. If you can, lift out the embedded hairs first: a strip of tape pressed on and peeled off, or a gentle wash, lifts the silica needles. Then cool it.

Cool water, a cold compress, and time do most of the work; an ordinary antihistamine cream or tablet targets the actual histamine. The wheal usually fades within a few hours — the common nettle's sting is short-lived, whatever the dock-leaf folklore promises.

And the counter-intuitive trick from the proverb: if you must handle nettles bare-handed, grip firmly and confidently in the direction the hairs lie, rather than brushing. Flattened hairs can't break the skin. Better still — gloves.

The bright side. That patch you're cursing is feeding a hundred insects and a nursery of butterflies. Cut the young tops in spring for soup and you'll have taken a free multivitamin off a plant most people only ever meet by accident.
