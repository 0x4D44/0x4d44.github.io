A Note from the Publishers

This volume makes no apology. For too long the upright card-player has been told that the outcome of a game rests upon such vulgar accidents as the deal, the odds, or the cards themselves. We reject this counsel of despair. The truly accomplished player understands that a hand of cards is not a sentence to be endured but a negotiation to be won — quietly, gracefully, and from a position of overwhelming and entirely undisclosed advantage.

What follows is the accumulated wisdom of a lifetime spent at the green baize: a body of knowledge passed down not in writing (writing being evidence) but by example, across countless family Christmases, to children who learned to count to thirteen and to four-times-thirteen with unusual speed.

Disclaimer, the Only One Nothing herein should be construed as an accusation. Our subject is, and has always been, a man of impeccable honour. We simply cannot account for the aces.

Chapter the First

On the Higher Ethics of Winning

In which a delicate matter of vocabulary is settled, once and for all.

Let us begin by dismissing an ugly little word. The reader will have noticed that, upon the title page, the word cheating has been struck through and replaced with winning. This is not an erratum. It is the single most important lesson in this book, and the rest is merely commentary.

To cheat is to break the rules. We do no such thing. We merely arrive at the table having prepared more thoroughly than our opponents — in matters of wardrobe, inventory, and conversation — and we decline to publish our preparations. There is no rule against being well dressed. There is no rule against owning several decks of cards. There is, regrettably, no rule against coughing.1

The accomplished player therefore operates within a private moral universe, governed by three immovable principles:

The Three Pillars

  • The Pillar of Foresight. An ace produced from the sleeve was not conjured; it was merely anticipated, earlier and more completely than the deck anticipated it.
  • The Pillar of Generosity. One must lose occasionally, and graciously, so that one's victories appear the gift of Providence rather than the fruit of labour.
  • The Pillar of Silence. The method is never explained, never confessed, and — above all — never written down.2
Maxim for the Beginner It is not cheating if you are family, and it is not cheating if nobody can prove it. On the rare occasion that both conditions fail, it is, at most, a Christmas tradition.

1. See Chapter IV, in which coughing is elevated from a mere bodily function to a fine art, and indeed to a second language.

2. The present volume is, the publishers concede, in some tension with this Pillar. We have agreed never to speak of it.

Chapter the Second

Of Wardrobe & Concealment

Wherein the humble knitted garment is revealed as the player's truest friend.

No subject is more shamefully neglected by the amateur than tailoring. He arrives at the card table dressed for the occasion rather than for the opportunity, and is duly punished for it. The seasoned player knows that a hand of cards is won not at the moment of play but weeks earlier, before the bedroom mirror, among the woollens.

The governing principle is that of volume without suspicion. One requires a garment loose enough to admit a quantity of cards yet respectable enough that no man would dream of asking you to remove it. The genius of the cardigan — and, in its highest form, the Aran sweater — is that it answers both demands at a single stroke. It is warm; it is grandfatherly; it is, structurally, a filing cabinet.

A Word on Climate

The reader will object that a heavy sweater is conspicuous in July. Just so. The committed player therefore ensures that the family always plays cards in winter, near a window, with the heating set unaccountably low and a draught helpfully identified. A man who insists on his cardigan in a cold room is not a suspect. He is a martyr.

    Chapter the Third

    The Doctrine of the Multiple Deck

    On the prudence of never owning fewer than four of anything.

    Here we arrive at the cornerstone of household strategy, a principle so elegant that its discoverer wept. It is this: a single deck of cards contains but one Ace of Spades. A man who owns four identical decks is, by the plainest arithmetic, never more than an arm's length from four Aces of Spades — and may distribute the surplus as conscience and cardigan allow.

    The method requires nothing more than patience at the point of purchase. One simply acquires the same brand, the same pattern, the same reassuringly ordinary blue-backed deck, year upon year, until the household holds a small but devoted standing army of cards, all indistinguishable, all loyal.

    Fig. 3. A household reserve, correctly maintained.
    A Caution on Wear Decks age. A pristine card dealt into a tired hand is as loud as a trumpet. The disciplined player therefore seasons his reserve, shuffling all decks together at intervals through the year so that every Ace acquires the same honest patina of thumb-grease and history.

    Chapter the Fourth

    The Fine Art of the Distraction

    In which the player learns that the eye, once turned, sees nothing.

    All concealment is wasted if performed before a watchful audience. The advanced player therefore cultivates the art of redirection: the brief, well-timed event that draws every eye to the window, the door, or the dog — affording a window of perhaps two seconds in which the universe may be quietly rearranged.

    The distraction must be plausible, brief, and above all deniable. Knocking over one's own drink is the classic of the genre — generous, self-deprecating, and instantly forgiven. The pointing of a finger at the garden, accompanied by the words "Was that a heron?", is a perennial favourite, requiring no props and surviving repeated use within the same evening through the sheer force of the family's reluctance to discuss it.

    A Taxonomy of Diversions

    • The Meteorological. "Is it snowing?" Best deployed in any season but snow.
    • The Domestic Crisis. "I think something's burning." Reserved for high-stakes hands; clears the room entirely.
    • The Sympathetic Ailment. A sudden, theatrical search for one's spectacles — which are, of course, upon one's head — buying time and goodwill in equal measure.
    • The Conversational Gambit. A pointed question about a relative's love life, guaranteed to occupy the table for no less than four minutes.

    Chapter the Fifth

    Manoeuvres for the Bridge Table

    Advanced technique for the four-handed game, where one's partner is an accomplice by law.

    Bridge is the connoisseur's arena, for it furnishes the player with that rarest of gifts: a partner, bound by the rules of the game itself to act in concert with him. Where the rules permit so much open collusion, the imaginative player finds the remaining latitude positively luxurious.

    The Meaningful Cough

    The laws of bridge sternly forbid signalling to one's partner by word, gesture, or expression. They are, however, silent on bronchitis. A robust system may be built entirely from the respiratory tract: one cough for hearts, two for spades, a thoughtful clearing of the throat for "I have absolutely nothing and intend to blame you." The beauty of the method is that any opponent who objects must first accuse a beloved elderly relative of faking a chest complaint at Christmas, a social impossibility.3

    On the Dummy

    When one's partner is dummy and lays down his hand, a brief and apparently absent-minded tidying of his cards — straightening them, admiring them, gently reordering a suit "for clarity" — communicates a wealth of intelligence while presenting only the picture of a helpful, tidy-minded man.

    The Long Deal

    The player who deals slowly, with much licking of the thumb and many small apologies for his clumsiness, is afforded ample opportunity to acquaint himself with the lower regions of the pack. Clumsiness, cultivated over decades, is the perfect disguise: nobody suspects the man who cannot find his own glasses of having memorised the bottom eight cards.

    Partnership Note The ideal partner is a spouse of forty years' standing, who requires no signals at all, having long ago learned to read everything from the angle of an eyebrow — and who will, crucially, never testify.

    Chapter the Sixth

    Plausible Deniability & the Innocent Countenance

    The final and most important art: that of being, at all times, beyond reproach.

    Technique is nothing without temperament. A man may have an ace in each sleeve and a heron at the ready, yet undo himself entirely with a single guilty glance. The crowning discipline of the advantage player is therefore the cultivation of an innocence so complete that suspicion itself comes to seem the accuser's failing rather than his own.

    This is achieved through a lifetime's rehearsal of three expressions: mild surprise at one's own good fortune; gentle hurt at any insinuation; and warm, forgiving amusement when — as occasionally happens — a grandchild flips back the cuff of a cardigan and discovers the better part of a second deck.

    Fig. 4. The Countenance of Total Innocence (with corroborating evidence).

    The Standard Replies

    Every household champion maintains a repertoire of unanswerable responses, delivered with a faint air of wounded patience:

    • "I've been dealt good cards all my life. It's called positivity."
    • "You'd never speak to your mother like this."
    • "Show me the rule. Go on. Fetch the box and show me the rule."
    • "That card was in my hand the whole time. You weren't watching."
    • "Why would I cheat to win twenty pence?" — a question whose unanswerability is its entire purpose.

    Master these, and the player attains the final state to which all of this tends: not merely to win, but to win while being comforted by his victims for the distress their suspicions have caused him.

    Appendix

    A Glossary & The Code of Honour

    A Brief Glossary of Terms

    Foresight
    The act of knowing which card is coming, on account of having put it there.
    The Reserve
    A household's standing stock of identical decks; a pension, paid in aces.
    The Heron
    Any object in the garden, real or imagined, sufficient to turn four heads for two seconds.
    Seasoning
    The disciplined ageing of new cards until they may be introduced without scandal.
    The Cough System
    A complete language of the chest, fluent only between players married thirty years or more.
    Twenty Pence
    The traditional and entirely sufficient motive for a lifetime of meticulous deception.

    The Player's Code of Honour

    1. I shall never cheat — only prepare, anticipate, and decline to explain.
    2. I shall lose with grace, that I may win without examination.
    3. I shall never deploy the heron twice before pudding.
    4. I shall season my decks, that no Ace betray me by its youth.
    5. I shall keep my cardigan, my counsel, and my conscience all firmly buttoned.
    6. I shall, if discovered, be hurt — deeply, visibly, and at considerable length.
    7. Above all, I shall never, ever write any of this down.

    And so the family hereby breaks the seventh rule, with love, in the hope that he will take it in the spirit intended — and deal us, just once, an honest hand.
