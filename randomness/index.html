How computers fake randomness — and how to catch them

The Shape of Chance

A digital computer is a machine for doing exactly the same thing every time. Ask it for a random number and it can only pretend — it runs a little recipe that churns out numbers looking scrambled while being utterly determined. The good news: the pretending leaves marks. Every fake randomness has a shape. This is an instrument for seeing those shapes — the hidden lattices, the ghostly planes — and the machines that tell an honest generator from a liar.

How it works

The bargain

Nothing here is random

A pseudo-random number generator — a PRNG — is a tiny machine with a hidden number inside it, its state. Each time you ask for a number it stirs the state with a fixed arithmetic recipe and hands you a piece of the result. Give it the same starting state — the seed — and it will produce the same "random" sequence forever after. That is not a bug; it is the whole point. It is what lets a game replay, a simulation reproduce, a shuffle be audited.

Because the state is a finite number, the machine must eventually return to a state it has visited before — and from there it can only repeat. Every PRNG runs on a loop. The length of that loop is its period. A good modern generator has a period so vast (2¹⁹⁹³⁷ − 1 for the Mersenne Twister) that you could never reach the seam. A careless one loops in seconds.

So we strike a bargain. We give up true randomness — impossible on a deterministic machine — and in exchange we ask only that the numbers be indistinguishable from random by any test we care about. The bitmap above is the crudest such test: does it look random? It is a weak test. The generator that sailed through it is about to fail a harder one badly — and the failure is beautiful.

A theorem, 1968

Random numbers fall mainly in the planes

The lines are not an accident of a bad multiplier — they are a law. In 1968 George Marsaglia proved that the output of every linear congruential generator, the workhorse recipe xₙ₊₁ = (a·xₙ + c) mod m, is doomed to lie on a lattice. Stack the numbers into pairs and they sit on a family of parallel lines; into triples and they sit on a family of parallel planes; into d-tuples and they lie on (d−1)-dimensional sheets. The only question a good design answers is: how many sheets, and how close together? Marsaglia titled his paper, with perfect economy, Random Numbers Fall Mainly in the Planes.

A little theory would have gone a long way… the generator RANDU is entirely unsuitable for high-dimensional Monte Carlo work. after Marsaglia & Knuth

Which brings us to the villain of the story. RANDU was IBM's linear congruential generator, shipped in the 1960s on the Scientific Subroutine Package and copied into countless Fortran programs across the mainframe world. Its recipe was xₙ₊₁ = 65539 · xₙ mod 2³¹. In one dimension it is uniform. In two dimensions it looks like fog — you just saw it pass the pairs plot cleanly. For years, nobody looked in three.

When you do, RANDU collapses. Its triples do not merely sit on a lattice — they sit on just fifteen planes, with vast empty gulfs between them. A decade of published science that leaned on RANDU for three-dimensional sampling was quietly built on a grid of fifteen sheets pretending to be a solid cube. Randomness, put to work

Throwing darts at π

Why does any of this matter beyond the pretty pictures? Because we use these numbers to compute. The purest example is Monte Carlo integration: to measure an area, throw random darts at it and count how many land inside. Fence a quarter-circle inside a unit square, rain darts uniformly across the square, and the fraction landing inside the arc must approach the ratio of the areas — π⁄4. Multiply by four and you have measured π with nothing but a coin and patience.

This is where a generator's shape becomes a scientific error rather than a curiosity. Darts that secretly lie on a lattice do not cover the square evenly; they over-sample some regions and starve others, and the estimate drifts to a wrong answer that no amount of extra darts will fix. Below, watch a good generator home in on 3.14159… while a lattice-bound one settles — confidently, stubbornly — beside it.

The judges

When the eye is not enough

You may have noticed that RANDU gives a perfectly respectable value of π. That is the whole trap: a two-dimensional test cannot see a three-dimensional flaw. The eye — and any single experiment — can be fooled. So the field grew a battery of statistical judges: formal tests that put a number on how far a stream departs from the ideal of independent, uniform chance. Modern suites like Diehard, its successor Dieharder, and the NIST and TestU01 batteries run dozens of them at once.

Below is a miniature bench. Each generator is fed a fresh stream and put through five tests. The verdicts tell a subtler story than "good" and "bad": watch RANDU pass the one- and two-dimensional tests and fail only in three; watch the toy lattice pass a one-dimensional uniformity check and fall apart the moment we look at pairs. Passing a test proves nothing — it only means that particular judge did not catch you.

Predict the next number

The oracle

Every test so far has asked whether a stream looks random. Here is a sharper question, the one that matters for a password or an encryption key: given the numbers you've already seen, can someone predict the next one? A pseudo-random generator is deterministic — its next output is a fixed function of its hidden state — so if an observer can recover that state from the output, the entire future is an open book.

For a linear generator this is embarrassingly easy. Watch the oracle below. It knows nothing about the machine it's facing — not the multiplier, not the modulus. It simply collects a few outputs, takes differences, and solves for the hidden constants by taking a greatest common divisor. Within a handful of numbers it has reconstructed the exact formula and calls every future "random" number in advance. Then point it at the cryptographic generator: it never gains an inch, and guessing a 32-bit number blind is one chance in 4,294,967,296.

Three kinds of random

Choosing a generator

Not every job needs the same randomness, and using the wrong grade is how disasters happen. There are three tiers.

True random number generators (TRNGs) harvest physical noise — thermal jitter in a resistor, the timing of radioactive decay, atmospheric static, the shot noise on a photodiode. They are genuinely unpredictable and genuinely slow, so a machine typically uses one only to seed the others. This is what your operating system's entropy pool is for.

Pseudo-random generators (PRNGs) are the fast arithmetic machines we have been watching. The Mersenne Twister (1997) ended the era of lattice disasters for ordinary work and remains the default in Python, R and MATLAB — enormous period, excellent statistics. Its modern successors are smaller and faster and pass even harder tests: PCG (2014) and the xoshiro / xorshift family, now the default in NumPy, Rust and much of the games industry — all of which you can put on the bench above, alongside the Twister and the respectable old MINSTD. The mulberry32 you have been watching is a tiny cousin of these. All of them are wonderful for simulation and shuffling — and none is safe for secrets. Observe enough output and you can solve for the state and predict every number to come.

Cryptographic generators (CSPRNGs) close that door. Built on the same primitives as ciphers and hash functions, they are designed so that no feasible computation can run the sequence backward or forward from its output. This is the crypto reference at the top — crypto.getRandomValues in the browser, /dev/urandom on Unix. Slower, but the only correct choice for keys, tokens, passwords and nonces. The rule is blunt: if an adversary would benefit from guessing your next number, a PRNG is a vulnerability, not a shortcut.

A last trap: modulo bias

Even perfect randomness is easy to ruin at the final step. To get a die roll from 1–6, the tempting one-liner is 1 + rand() % 6. But if rand() returns, say, 0–32767, those 32768 values do not divide evenly into six buckets — the low faces get one extra value each. The die is loaded, faintly, toward the small numbers. The fix is rejection: draw again whenever you land in the ragged remainder at the top of the range, so every face is backed by exactly the same number of draws. A flawless generator, undone by a careless %.

What this is

A single self-contained page. Every generator runs live in your browser — nothing is pre-rendered, nothing phones home — and every plot is drawn from numbers computed on the spot. The crypto reference is your platform's real cryptographic generator (crypto.getRandomValues); the Mersenne Twister, PCG32, xoshiro128**, mulberry32 and xorshift32 are genuine modern PRNGs implemented from their reference definitions; MINSTD is the 1988 Park–Miller "minimal standard"; and RANDU, the toy LCG (multiplier 5), middle-square and the low-bit LCG are the historical cautionary tales. Every one is implemented exactly as described, so the flaws — and the passes — are real, not staged.

The 15-plane collapse of RANDU follows from uₙ₊₂ = 6uₙ₊₁ − 9uₙ (mod 1), a relation you can derive in three lines from its multiplier. The test bench uses ordinary Pearson χ² with Wilson–Hilferty critical values; the verdicts are computed, not hand-set. Where a fact is folklore — the exact toll of RANDU on 1970s science — it is described as reported rather than certain.
