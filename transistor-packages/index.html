Why the shells changed

A package is a thermal argument.

The silicon die is only part of the story. The package has to make electrical contact, survive assembly, shed heat and sometimes bolt itself to several kilograms of aluminium.

01 · seal

Metal cans kept the world out.

TO-18 and TO-39 packages enclosed the die in a welded metal shell. They were expensive but robust, easy to identify and well suited to early low-volume electronics.

02 · mould

Plastic made transistors ordinary.

The TO-92 and SOT-23 use moulded epoxy. Once automatic assembly took over, the package became cheap enough to vanish inside almost everything.

03 · heat

Power puts the metal back.

TO-220, TO-247 and TO-3 expose a tab or case to a heatsink. Their apparent bulk is not wasted space: it is a low-resistance path from a hot die to the outside world.

Method and limits

What “to scale” means here

Inside each interactive SVG, one coordinate unit equals one millimetre. Coin diameters and package bodies therefore preserve their mathematical ratio at every zoom level. The physical size on your monitor is unknown until you calibrate it.

  • Coin faces are schematic original illustrations, not reproductions of Royal Mint artwork.
  • For non-round coins, “diameter” is the specified constant maximum diameter.
  • Package drawings use representative body dimensions; formed leads and manufacturer tolerances differ.
  • The TO-3 comparison includes its flange, because that flange is the package’s defining envelope.
