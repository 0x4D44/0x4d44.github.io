The power chain: diesel in, controllable pull out.

There is no gearbox in a Class 47. A single twin-bank Sulzer turns a big DC generator; the generator feeds six traction motors. Electricity is the transmission — and it is an infinitely variable one.

Why electric transmission

The generator is a gearbox with no gears.

A 2,750 bhp engine produces its power in a narrow band of speeds, but a train needs full pulling effort from a dead stand right up to 95 mph. No mechanical gearbox of the 1960s could shift that much power across that range and survive. The elegant answer: let the engine spin a generator, and use the electricity to drive traction motors geared to the axles.

Now the engine never has to "match" the wheels. It runs at whatever speed the driver's power handle selects; the generator's excitation is varied to soak up exactly the power the engine is making, and the motors turn that into torque at any road speed. At a standstill the motors draw huge current at low voltage — maximum torque. At speed they spin fast, generate back-EMF, and draw less current. The transmission re-gears itself, continuously, with no moving ratio at all.

The key idea

A diesel-electric isn't "a diesel that makes electricity." It is a constant-power machine: the engine works hardest at one steady output, and the electrical system trades volts for amps to deliver that same power as either lots of pull at low speed, or modest pull at high speed. Keep this in mind — it is the whole of Chapter 03.

Interactive · Fig. 2.1

The Sulzer 12LDA28 — two banks, two crankshafts.

Sulzer's twin-bank layout is the 47's signature oddity. Instead of a V, it stands two banks of six cylinders side by side, each bank on its own crankshaft; the two cranks are geared together and drive the generator from one end. It is compact, rigid, and — at 280 mm bore — a true medium-speed marine-style diesel pressed into a railway frame. Toggle the systems to see how it works.

Drive the whole transmission, end to end.

Open the power handle and watch energy move: the engine spins up, the generator excites, current floods down the bus to six motors, and the wheels turn. Then change road speed and watch the trade — the same power delivered as amps (pull) at low speed, or volts (revs) at high speed.

Load regulator

The clever automatic at the centre of it all. As road conditions change, the load regulator continuously adjusts the generator's field so the engine is always loaded to exactly the power the driver selected — never lugged down, never over-fuelled. It is what lets one fixed engine speed serve every road speed, and it is doing its work every second you move the sliders above.
