An engine worked too hard

Light installation, big demand, hot cylinder heads.

Chapter 01 ended on a warning: the 47 sat right on its axle limit, so every component was built as light as it could be — including the engine installation. Then it was asked for the full 2,750 bhp. The Sulzer 12LDA28 could make that figure, but doing so day after day ran the cylinders hot. Cylinder heads cracked. Exhaust temperatures climbed. Components downstream of the combustion — heads, valves, turbocharger — wore and failed faster than the railway could tolerate.

Early-fleet availability suffered. A locomotive in the works with a cracked head earns nothing, and with hundreds entering traffic the problem multiplied across the network. The engineers faced a classic choice: chase the reliability with ever-heavier, costlier components — weight the 47 could not spare — or simply ask the engine for less power and let it live.

The failure mode

At sustained full power the combustion side ran hotter than the structure liked. Thermal cycling — heat, cool, heat, cool — opened cracks in the cylinder heads and stressed the exhaust system. Each crack meant a failure, a removal, a works visit. Reliability, not raw output, became the number that mattered.

Fires & other troubles

It wasn't only the heads.

A hard-worked diesel in a tight engine room is a fire risk: hot exhaust, fuel and lubricating oil under pressure, and electrical gear all share a confined space. The 47 saw its share of engine-room fires over a long career — a hazard the class was not unique in, but one that maintenance and later modifications worked steadily to reduce.

The dead end

Class 48: five locomotives with the wrong engine.

If the twin-bank Sulzer was being pushed too hard, why not try a different engine? In 1965 five locomotives — D1702–D1706 — were completed not with the 12LDA28 twin-bank but with a Sulzer 12LVA24 V-form engine, a more modern, more compact design. Classified separately as Class 48, they were the experiment that would test whether a V-engine was the 47's future.

Case study · the 12LVA24
The V-engine was lighter and newer — and it was a failure. Within a few years all five were stripped of their Sulzer V-units, fitted with the standard twin-bank 12LDA28, and quietly absorbed back into the Class 47 fleet.
The locomotives lost their separate identity, absorbed into the 47 fleet. The same 12LVA24 V-engine had one other outlet, though — built at the very same time: ten near-identical "Clayton" locomotives for Cuba (Ch. 05). The twin-bank engine the engineers had been fighting turned out to be the better bet after all.

The lesson was pointed: the 47's problem was not fundamentally its engine type but how hard it was driven. Once the derating tamed the twin-bank, the case for a riskier, unproven V-engine evaporated. The Class 48 stands as the road not taken — proof that the conservative choice, run within its limits, beat the clever one run on the edge.

What it cost, what it bought

A reliable workhorse beats a brilliant invalid.

Derating cost the 47 nothing a passenger would notice and everything a fleet manager would celebrate. A locomotive that holds 2,580 bhp and stays in traffic for weeks earns far more than one that makes 2,750 bhp and spends a fortnight in the works with a cracked head. The number that mattered was never peak power — it was availability, and the 47 won it the hard way.

The through-line

Every chapter has circled the same compromise — power against weight, volts against amps, performance against reliability. The 47's greatness is not that it resolved these tensions, but that British Railways found the right point of balance on each, and then built five hundred and twelve locomotives around it. Chapter 05 is what that fleet did for the next sixty years.
