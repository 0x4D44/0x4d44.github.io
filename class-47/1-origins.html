The 1955 Modernisation Plan

Steam was to go. Something had to replace it fast.

The British Transport Commission's 1955 Modernisation & Re-Equipment Plan committed £1.2 billion to drag the railway into the diesel and electric age. Diesel locomotives were sorted into power bands — Type 1 (under 1,000 bhp) up to Type 4 (2,000–2,999 bhp) — and a "Pilot Scheme" ordered small batches from many builders so the best could be picked.

The early Type 4s were a disappointment in one specific way: they were heavy. The English Electric Class 40 and the Sulzer-engined "Peak" Class 44/45/46 all rode on a 1Co-Co1 wheel arrangement — six driven axles plus a carrying axle at each end purely to spread the weight. Those idle axles hauled themselves around the network for thirty years doing no useful work. BR wanted a Type 4 with the same power on six axles only: a true Co-Co.

Why the wheel arrangement mattered so much.

Axle load — the weight pressing through one wheelset onto the rail — decides which lines a locomotive may use. Civil engineers set a route ceiling; exceed it and bridges and track wear out. Spread the same mass over more axles and each one presses down less. Drag the weight slider and switch arrangements to see the engineers' dilemma.

The Type 4 contest

Three prototypes raced to define the lightweight Type 4.

Between 1961 and 1962 three privately-funded demonstrators tried to crack the same brief: full Type 4 power on a Co-Co. Each paired a different engine and electrical philosophy. Select one to read how it argued its case — and how the contest resolved.

The decision

BR liked the Lion formula — one Sulzer LDA28 engine, Co-Co, electric transmission — but not its AEI electrical gear. The production locomotive took the Lion's layout and married it to Brush electrical equipment, built largely at Brush's Falcon Works in Loughborough with a second line at BR Crewe. The last twenty of an existing "Peak" order were quietly turned into the new design, entering service as D1500–D1519 in 1962. They became the Class 47.

Body & bogies

An all-welded box that carries its own loads.

To hit the axle-load target the 47 abandoned the old idea of a separate underframe carrying a bolted-on body. Instead the whole structure is an integral, stressed-skin monocoque: the bodysides and roof are load-bearing members, welded into a rigid tube. It is light, stiff, and — crucially — cheap to build in volume.

Underneath sit two three-axle bogies of fabricated/cast steel, each carrying three nose-suspended DC traction motors. Power is fed from a single large diesel-generator set amidships. The two cabs are handed: a No. 1 end (by convention the boiler/closer-to-radiator end) and a No. 2 end, which matters when you read the electrical drawings later.

What went right

  • MassLight enough for a Co-Co at Type 4 power — the core win.
  • Route availabilityRA 6/7 — accepted over most of the network.
  • Build rateTwo production lines turned out 512 in six years.

The compromises baked in

  • No spare marginSitting on the axle limit left no weight budget for upgrades.
  • Engine stressed2,750 bhp from a light installation pushed the Sulzer hard (Ch. 04).
  • AdhesionSix axles, ~19 t each — wheelslip control mattered (Ch. 03).
