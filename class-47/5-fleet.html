A closer look

Inside the two biggest families: the 47/4 and the 47/7.

The headline sub-classes hide a lot of variation. Two groups in particular repay a closer look — the passenger workhorse 47/4, which was never quite one thing, and the 47/7, whose intensive duties exposed real operating and maintenance problems.

The many faces of the 47/4

The 47/4 was a programme, not a single build. Electric train heating arrived on the two pioneers D1960/D1961 in 1967, and over the following years some 263 locomotives were equipped — but no two batches were quite alike:

  • Dual-heatEarly conversions kept the steam boiler alongside the new ETH, so they could heat older steam-stock and modern electric stock through the changeover.
  • Electric-onlyLater locos dropped the boiler entirely — lighter, simpler, and free of the boiler's perennial unreliability.
  • ETH indexHeating capacity (the "ETH index") varied, governing how many coaches a loco could warm.
  • Number sprawl47401–47420 were the original generation; conversions later filled 47421–47547, 47549–47555 and 47556–47665.

The 47/7 — and what went wrong

Built for the fast, frequent Edinburgh–Glasgow push-pull shuttle from 1979, the 47/7 packed in TDM remote control, long-range tanks and 100 mph gearing. The intensive diagrams piled on mileage — and surfaced two hard lessons:

  • Push-pull safetyIn the 1984 Polmont accident, a 47/7 propelling its train from the rear struck a cow and the lightweight leading coach derailed, with heavy loss of life — forcing a national rethink of running a heavy loco at the back of light stock.
  • Fire47713 was withdrawn after fire damage; 47717 was added to the class in 1986 to replace it.
  • TDM upkeepThe time-division-multiplex control and its cabling were extra systems to maintain on already hard-worked machines.
  • A second waveIn the 1990s more 47/4s were converted for Rail Express parcels with long-range tanks and propelling-control-vehicle gear, numbered from 47721 — a different, simpler push-pull system to the Scottish TDM.
Polmont, 30 July 1984

A 47/7 was propelling the 17:30 Edinburgh–Glasgow from the rear when the train hit a cow that had strayed onto the line near Polmont. The lightweight leading driving-trailer rode up and derailed, and thirteen people died. The accident did not blame the locomotive's mechanics — but it raised lasting questions about the safety of push-pull operation with a heavy 47 shoving lighter coaching stock ahead of it, and fed directly into later thinking on vehicle crashworthiness and obstacle deflectors. It is the most sobering entry in the 47/7's service record.

Miles per casualty: the scale of a safe workhorse.

Over sixty years a fleet this size runs an almost unimaginable distance — and the rarity of serious casualties against that distance is the real measure of the machine. Exact lifetime mileage and a complete casualty census are not public to the mile, so this model is illustrative: set your own assumption for annual mileage and watch the order of magnitude emerge. Plotted on it are three of the best-documented incidents in the class's career.

The Summit Tunnel fire of December 1984 is famous precisely because no one died: a train of petrol tankers burned for days underground, yet the tunnel's Victorian brickwork held and the line reopened. It stands, with Polmont, as a reminder that the 47's long record was shaped as much by circumstance and infrastructure as by the locomotive itself.

In the cab

What it was like to drive.

The 47 cab is functional and unpretentious — a flat desk, a power handle to the driver's hand, a brake column, and the regulation BR safety gear. After the chaos of a steam footplate it was a revelation: warm, quiet-ish, one-person operation, and a locomotive that simply did what the handle asked.

From the cab
"She rides well at speed and you trust her. The boiler in the steam-heat days was the only drama — you'd be coaxing it alight at the platform end in January while the guard glared. Take that away and the 47 is the easiest big engine on the railway."
— composite driver recollection, illustrative

An export footnote

The ten that went to Cuba — wearing Clayton plates.

In 1965, while Brush was building Type 4s for British Railways — including the five experimental Class 48s — it quietly built ten near-identical machines for export to Cuba. And here is the twist that ties this chapter back to Chapter 04: the Cuban locomotives did not use the standard twin-bank engine. They used the Sulzer 12LVA24 V-engine — the very unit that failed in the home-market Class 48 — rated at 2,534 hp at 1,050 rpm, with Brush electrical gear modified to suit the V-engine's clockwise rotation (the twin-bank turned the other way).

They were recognisably 47s with tropical detailing: a large cab-roof headlight, corner number boxes, centre knuckle couplers instead of buffers, air brakes only, and a revised roof over an enlarged cooling group built for the Caribbean heat — all turned out in BR two-tone green. Curiously, because Brush's new parent company was entangled in contracts with US firms, the order was disguised: the locomotives were badged as the work of the Clayton Company of Hatton, Derbyshire, and even carried Clayton builder's plates. Numbered 2501–2510, they worked for decades until the climate took its toll — most were derelict by the 1980s–90s.

The V-engine's real home

It is a neat irony. The 12LVA24 V-engine was a failure in British service — five Class 48s were re-engined with the twin-bank and absorbed into the 47 fleet (Ch. 04). Yet the same engine, built at the same moment, crossed the Atlantic in ten "Clayton" 47-clones and ran on for years under the Cuban sun. The engine Britain rejected found its longest service 7,000 km away.

Second life

The Class 57: a new heart in an old body.

By the 1990s the airframes were sound but the Sulzer engines were tired and spares were scarce. Rather than scrap good bodyshells and bogies, Brush rebuilt them as the Class 57 from 1998: the worn Sulzer came out, and in went a reconditioned EMD 645 two-stroke diesel — American, plentiful, and very well understood — driving a refurbished alternator. The 47 lived on under a new number.

Why it worked

The economics were irresistible: the costliest parts of a locomotive — the body, the cab, the bogies, the traction motors — were already paid for and still good. Swapping in a known, supportable engine bought another generation of service for a fraction of a new-build price. The 47's stressed-skin body, designed in 1962 to save weight, was still earning its keep in the 2020s under a different badge.

That is the final measure of the design. A locomotive specified to a 1955 plan, derated to survive its own engine, and rebuilt around an American heart forty years later — and still on the network. Britain built 512 of them because they worked. They are still working because they were built right.
