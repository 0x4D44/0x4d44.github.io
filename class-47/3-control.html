Control & configuration: the power handle, the curve, and the great wiring question.

The driver has one lever and almost nothing to think about — the load regulator does the rest. But behind the simplicity sits a genuine fork in the fleet: how do you wire six traction motors? All in parallel, or in series-parallel? The choice changes the locomotive's whole character.

One lever to drive it

The driver selects power, not speed.

Unlike a steam locomotive — where the driver juggles regulator and reverser by feel — the Class 47 driver moves a single power handle through eight notches. Each notch commands an engine speed and a power level. From there the load regulator takes over, trimming the generator's excitation so the engine is always loaded to exactly that power, whatever the road throws at it.

This is why a 47 feels so unfussy to drive: you ask for power, and the electric transmission silently re-gears itself to deliver it as pull. The skill is no longer in matching engine to wheels — it is in managing adhesion, watching for wheelslip, and knowing the road. The machine handles the rest.

From the cab

"You wind the power handle round and just wait for her to take hold — there's a moment where nothing happens, then the ammeter swings and she leans into the train. On a greasy rail you learn to ease it, or she'll spin a wheel and the lights flicker as the slip-relay knocks the power back."

— composite driver recollection, illustrative

Drag the power handle and read the curve. At low speed effort is flat-topped — limited by adhesion and motor current, not by power. Past base speed the locomotive runs out of voltage and effort falls away as a hyperbola: constant power means effort × speed is fixed, so going faster must mean pulling less.

Six motors, and the wiring BR changed its mind about.

This is the detail enthusiasts argue about, and it is a genuine generational split in the fleet — not a marketing trim. The generator feeds six traction motors (Brush TM64-68, one per axle, from a TG160-60 generator), and there were two rival ways to group them.

The earliest Brush Type 4s were built series-parallel (SP): like a DC electric multiple unit, they stepped through three stages as they accelerated — Series → Transition → Parallel — regrouping the motors on the move to trade torque for speed. The bulk of the fleet was wired the simpler way: permanent all-parallel, six motors always across the line, no transition, with the load regulator and field-weakening doing all the work. Toggle between them below; the consequences for starting, slip and reliability are the rest of this chapter.

All-parallel — the standard

  • GroupingSix motors permanently across the line
  • Speed controlGenerator excitation + field weakening — no transition
  • SwitchgearMinimal — fewer contactors to fail
  • SlipEach motor independent — a slip stays local
  • VerdictBecame the BR standard; the bulk of the fleet

Series-parallel — the early scheme

  • GroupingSeries strings at start → parallel at speed
  • StagesSeries · Transition · Parallel
  • SwitchgearTransition contactors — more to maintain
  • SlipSeries-coupled motors drag each other down
  • VerdictNon-standard; flagged for early withdrawal
Why the early scheme was dropped

The series-parallel locomotives worked — but they were the odd ones out. The transition stages needed extra contactors and relays that could (and did) give trouble, the changeover itself put a momentary interruption into the tractive effort, and — as Fig. 3.3 shows — series-coupling behaves badly when one wheelset loses grip. With most of the fleet wired the simpler permanent-parallel way, the SP machines were a maintenance minority. When British Rail later drew up a list of locomotives for early withdrawal, the non-standard "series-parallel" locomotives were specifically targeted: the clever scheme became a liability simply by being different.

Interactive · Fig. 3.3 — why the grouping mattered

The loco "sits back" — and the wiring decides who slips.

Here is the deep reason all-parallel won. The moment a locomotive pulls, weight shifts rearward: the drawbar drags at coupler height while the rail pushes back at rail level, so the whole body pitches back onto its rear axles. Each bogie does the same internally — its leading axle lightens, its trailing axle digs in. The front axle of the front bogie ends up lightest of all, and is first to slip.

Now recall the awkward geometry: three axles per bogie, but series pairs take two motors each. Three pairs cannot all sit inside one bogie — at least one pair must straddle the gap, putting a light front-bogie axle in series with a well-gripping rear-bogie axle. Series-connected motors are forced to share one current, so they must make the same torque. When the light axle slips, it drags its partner's torque down with it — wasting grip the rear axle still had. Apply power below and watch it happen.

The honest caveat

That the early 47s were series-parallel with a Series–Transition–Parallel sequence, and that BR later singled the SP machines out for early withdrawal, are both documented. The exact internal motor topology — whether the series stage paired motors two-by-two (forcing a cross-bogie pair, as drawn here) or ran each bogie's three motors as a string — is not something this study can state with certainty, so Fig. 3.3 illustrates the principle of series-coupling under weight transfer rather than a verified wiring diagram. The physics it shows — that series-coupled motors share current and so propagate a slip, while parallel motors do not — is general to all DC traction.

Adhesion & wheelslip

All that effort is useless if the wheels spin.

Tractive effort can only be applied as far as the steel-on-steel grip allows — typically a fraction of the weight on the driven wheels. Demand more and the wheels break away into a slip: motor current surges, the wheelset races, and pulling effort collapses. A 47 carries a wheelslip relay that senses the runaway and momentarily cuts power to let the wheel re-grip.

This is the practical limit behind the flat top of the curve in Fig. 3.1. On a dry rail the 47 can lay down its full ~60,000 lbf; on a wet, leaf-strewn autumn rail the usable figure can halve, and the driver's art is keeping the train moving without ever asking for grip that isn't there. Six powered axles help — more wheels sharing the load means more total grip than a machine with idle carrying axles.
