The Brush Type 4 — Class 47 — was the workhorse the 1955 Modernisation Plan was looking for. Take it apart: the twin-bank Sulzer, the electric transmission, the faults that forced a famous derating, and the 512-strong fleet that ran everything from the Royal Train to coal.

Five hundred and twelve were built between 1962 and 1968 — more than any other mainline diesel class in British history. Sixty years on, examples are still earning revenue. This study explains why the design endured, and where it very nearly came undone.

How to read this study

One locomotive, taken apart.

The Class 47 is easy to love and easy to underrate. It looks ordinary — a box on two bogies — and it did ordinary work for sixty years. But underneath is a careful set of engineering compromises: a big medium-speed diesel, a DC electric transmission, and a body light enough to keep the axle-load legal.

Each chapter circles one question: how do you turn 2,750 horsepower of diesel into controllable pull at the rail, reliably, on a 19-tonne axle limit? We answer it with the engine, the generator, the motors, the faults, and the fleet.

Diagrams are interactive where you see the orange signal colour — drag, toggle, and drive the models. All figures are schematic and not to scale.
