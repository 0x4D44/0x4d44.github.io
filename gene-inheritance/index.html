Two Copies

how traits hide, and pass, and reappear

Two brown-eyed parents. A blue-eyed child. No mix-up at the hospital — just a 200-year-old rule quietly doing its work. Scroll, and let's watch it happen.

Chapter 1

A trait that skips a generation

Grandpa had blue eyes. His son — your father — has brown. And then you: blue again. The trait vanished for a generation and came back as if it had been waiting.

People noticed this centuries before anyone knew what a gene was. A feature disappears in the children and re-emerges in the grandchildren. Red hair. Attached earlobes. A whole list of conditions that a doctor will tell you "runs in families" — but skips people along the way.

It looks like magic, or bad record-keeping. It's neither. It falls cleanly out of a single idea, first written down by a monk counting pea plants in a monastery garden in 1865. Here it is in one sentence:

You carry two copies of (almost) every gene — one from each parent — and you pass on only one.

That's the whole engine. Everything else — dominant and recessive, carriers, the famous grid of squares, why cousins are cautioned about marrying — is just this rule, played out. Let's build it up piece by piece.

Chapter 2

One from each parent

Deep inside almost every cell in your body sits a near-complete copy of your genome, packed into chromosomes — long, coiled threads of DNA. You have 46 of them, and here is the crucial part: they come in 23 matching pairs.

One chromosome of each pair came from your mother, one from your father. They line up together because they carry the same genes in the same order — the gene for a given trait sits at the same address on both. So for that gene, you hold two copies. A version of a gene is called an allele, and your two alleles might agree — or they might not.

Chapter 3

Some alleles shout; others whisper

When your two alleles disagree, who wins? Often, one simply speaks louder. A dominant allele shows its trait even when there's only one copy. A recessive allele shows only when it's the only version present — two copies, no dominant to drown it out.

We write the dominant allele as a capital letter and the recessive as the same letter, lowercase. For Mendel's peas, purple flowers (B) are dominant over white (b). Three genotypes are possible — try them:

Notice something quietly profound. A Bb plant and a BB plant look identical — both purple. You cannot tell them apart by looking. What you see is the phenotype; what's written in the DNA is the genotype. The recessive allele is still there in the Bb plant, hidden, whispering — and ready to be passed on.

Chapter 4

The grid that predicts children

Now the rule earns its keep. Each parent passes on one of their two alleles, chosen at random — a coin flip inside every egg and sperm cell. The child gets one from each. To see every way the coins can land, we draw a little grid: the Punnett square.

Put one parent's two possible contributions across the top, the other's down the side, and fill each cell with the pair that meets there. Four equally-likely outcomes. Set the parents below and watch it build.

The classic case is two Bb parents — both purple, both secretly carrying white. Their grid gives the famous 3 : 1 ratio: three purple children to every white one. And exactly one child in four is bb — the hidden trait, made visible again.

But a prediction of "1 in 4" is not a promise. Probability is a long-run truth, not a rule about any one family. Flip four fair coins; you won't reliably get two heads. Let's actually run the experiment.

Chapter 5

The carriers among us

A person who is Bb — one dominant, one recessive — is called a carrier. They don't show the recessive trait, but they hold it, and they'll pass it on to about half their children. This is the quiet machinery behind most recessive genetic conditions.

Take cystic fibrosis. Roughly 1 in 25 people of European descent is a healthy carrier — no symptoms, often no idea. The condition only appears when a child inherits a recessive allele from both parents. Two carriers, no disease between them, have a 1-in-4 chance with each child. That's why it can appear in a family with no history of it: the allele was travelling silently, carrier to carrier, for generations.

This is also the real reason for the old caution about marrying close relatives. Everyone carries a handful of rare recessive alleles doing no harm — because their other copy is fine. But two relatives are far more likely to carry the same rare allele, inherited from a shared ancestor. Pair them up, and the odds of a child inheriting two broken copies climb sharply.

Chapter 6

When the rules bend

Neat dominant-and-recessive is the beginning of the story, not the end. Real inheritance is richer, and three twists come up constantly.

Blending: incomplete dominance

Sometimes neither allele wins outright and the heterozygote lands in between. Cross a red snapdragon with a white one and you don't get red — you get pink. Both alleles contribute; the result is a blend.

Sharing the stage: blood type

Your ABO blood type breaks two rules at once. There are three alleles in the population — A, B and O — not two. A and B are codominant: inherit one of each and you're type AB, showing both fully, no blending. O is recessive to both. Pick two parents and see which types their children can be:

Riding the X: sex-linked traits

Some genes live on the sex chromosomes. Females are XX, males XY — so a recessive allele on the X plays out unevenly. A woman with one bad copy is protected by her second X; she's a carrier. But a man has no second X to hide behind. One recessive copy and the trait shows.

A necessary honesty. The single-gene stories here are the clean textbook cases — and some of the famous ones are oversimplified. Human eye colour isn't one gene with brown beating blue; it's polygenic, the summed effect of many genes, which is why two blue-eyed parents can, rarely, have a brown-eyed child. Height, skin tone and most of what makes you you work this way: dozens or hundreds of genes, each a small nudge, plus the environment. The two-copies rule still runs underneath all of it — there are just a great many copies in play at once.
Chapter 7

You, drawn from an enormous deck

Step back and feel the scale of it. You have around 20,000 genes. For each, your mother and father each carried two copies and passed one — a coin flip apiece. Along the way, before those cells even formed, your chromosomes were shuffled, swapping segments in a process called recombination, so the copy you got was itself a fresh remix of your grandparents.

The number of genetically distinct children any two people could have isn't in the millions or the billions. It's a number with more digits than there are atoms in your body:

> 70,000,000,000,000ways to shuffle just the 23 chromosome pairs — before recombination

Every one of your ancestors won this lottery, unbroken, back billions of years. The blue eyes that skipped your father and found you weren't a glitch. They were a recessive allele riding quietly through a carrier, waiting for a second copy to meet it — the two-copies rule, keeping its patient books.

That's the whole engine. One from each parent; one passed on. Everything else is just watching it run.
