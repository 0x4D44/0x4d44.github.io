How the verdict is reached — and how much to trust it

Real production detectors (Pangram, the tool this is modelled on) run a neural network trained on roughly a million labelled human and machine documents. That needs a server and a GPU. Opus 4.8 Verdict is the honest client-side cousin: a transparent, explainable heuristics engine that measures the writing-style fingerprints large language models tend to leave, then adds them up. Everything happens in a few milliseconds of JavaScript on your own machine.

The signals it weighs

Each signal returns a 0–100 "how machine-like" score; the overall verdict is their weighted average.

Burstiness — humans mix very short sentences with long ones; models pace themselves evenly. Low variance in sentence length is the single strongest tell. Machine vocabulary — the density of the words and phrases LLMs over-reach for (delve, tapestry, testament to, navigate the complexities, it's important to note…). Transition density — over-signposting with moreover, furthermore, however. Em-dash habit — the tell-tale over-use of the em-dash. Formulaic openers — many sentences starting the same way (The…, This…, It is…). Rule-of-three — the model's love of tidy three-item lists. Hedging and lexical repetition round it out.

This is a writing-style signal, not proof. Polished human writing (a legal brief, a press release) can score high; deliberately messy AI output can score low. Use it to raise a question, never to settle one. No detector — this one included — should be the sole basis for an accusation.
