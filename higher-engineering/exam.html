The Admiralty Board · the Lieutenant's examination

The Lieutenant's examination: exam and assignment

Every officer on HMS Ratchet sat before the Admiralty Board once the rest of the training was done, and the Board did not ask what you knew. It asked what you could do, with the clock running and the Data Booklet on the table. This deck is that examination: how the 160 marks are won, what is (and is not) in the booklet, how to write answers that earn marks, how to survive the 50-mark assignment, a revision map, and a timed mock paper.

⚓ Ship's log: Captain Callum Reid

"I have seen sound engineers fail an examination and middling ones pass it. The difference was never the engineering. The ones who passed read the question, did the working in order, wrote the unit, and spent their minutes where the marks were. Master Shipwright Kerr will show you how."

1 · How the course is assessed COURSE ASSESSMENT

Higher Engineering Science is graded A to D from one total: your question-paper mark plus your assignment mark, out of 160. There is no separate pass for each part. That is good news, because a strong assignment can carry a weaker paper and the other way round. The awarding body is Qualifications Scotland, which took over from SQA on 1 February 2026; the course specification (version 2.0) and the Data Booklet (January 2026) are the documents in force.

160 marksquestion paper + assignment, one grade
110 marksquestion paper, 2 h 30 min (69%)
50 marksassignment, 8 hours (31%)

The question paper: 110 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes

  • Section 1 is 20 marks of short-answer questions. Section 2 is 90 marks of structured questions: a context, then parts (a), (b), (c)… that usually build on each other.
  • The questions mix calculations, short or limited responses and extended responses. The specification says a proportion of the marks are for more challenging questions: ones that need you to interpret a bigger engineering context, to write a more detailed or justified description, to rearrange a formula, or to carry the result of one formula into the next.
  • You are given the Data Booklet (formulae and data). The rounding instructions are on the front cover of the question paper: read them before you start.
  • For programmable control you do not have to write code. If you draw a flowchart it must carry "sufficient and appropriate detail, such as input and output pin numbers and a delay unit".

Where the question-paper marks come from

The specification gives, for each area, a range of marks the paper may carry. The ranges overlap and the exact split changes from year to year, so treat them as a guide to weight, not a promise.

Add the bottom of each range and you get 78 marks; add the top and you get 136. The paper has exactly 110, so every paper sits somewhere in between. Two things follow. Analogue electronics is the heaviest area (up to 35 marks), and the 2024 course report says candidates "found analogue electronics more demanding". And no area can be skipped: even the smallest range, Materials, is worth up to 14 marks, and the course reports repeat that candidates must be prepared in all areas of the specification.

The quartermaster's revision plan

Set how many hours you have, then rate your confidence in each area from 1 (very shaky) to 5 (very secure). The plan shares your hours in proportion to (midpoint of the mark range) × (6 − confidence): more marks and less confidence both pull time towards an area. It is a rule of thumb for planning, not an official weighting.

Timing: about 1.4 minutes per mark

The paper gives 150 minutes for 110 marks. That is $150 \div 110 = 1.3636\ldots$ minutes per mark, or about 82 seconds a mark. Everything you do counts against it: reading, thinking, writing, drawing, checking. So a 2-mark question deserves about 2 min 44 s, and a 6-mark question about 8 min 11 s. If you split the paper in proportion to its marks, Section 1 (20 marks) takes about 27 minutes and Section 2 (90 marks) about 123 minutes. That is a planning guide, not a rule: you might finish Section 1 faster.

The assignment: 50 marks, 8 hours

The assignment is 50 marks (31% of the total), set each year by Qualifications Scotland and marked externally. It is a problem-solving activity made of several tasks, done over 8 hours, alone, in a supervised classroom, with no teacher help. Chapter 4 walks through it in detail. For now, remember the five skills and their mark ranges: Analysis 4–8, Designing a solution 8–12, Building the solution 8–12, Testing 8–14 and Evaluation 8–14. Testing and evaluating together can be worth up to 28 of the 50 marks, and they are the skills the course reports say candidates find hardest.

Grade boundaries: what the marks have been

The grade boundary is the minimum total mark (out of 160) for each grade. It is set after the exams, from the evidence of how hard that year's paper and assignment turned out to be. Qualifications Scotland aims for a "notional" grade C at 50% of the marks (80 of 160) and a notional grade A at 70% (112 of 160), but adjusts the real boundaries up or down so that standards stay comparable. What the grade descriptions say

The course specification gives two descriptions. For grade C, candidates will typically have demonstrated "successful performance in relation to the skills, knowledge and understanding for the course". For grade A, they will typically have demonstrated "a consistently high level of performance" in relation to the same skills, knowledge and understanding. The word to notice is consistently: an A is not one brilliant question, it is a high standard held across all areas and across both components. That is why chapter 5 asks you to revise every area.

2 · The Data Booklet and the calculation layout EXAM SKILLS

Every candidate gets the Higher Engineering Science Data Booklet (Qualifications Scotland, January 2026) in the paper, and may use it in the assignment too. Its own preface makes three promises worth knowing. First, it holds the relationships and data for the concepts that may be assessed in written papers. Second, "all of the material contained in this booklet is likely to be examined at some time". Third, if you need anything else, "such information will be included in the question paper". So a number that is not in the booklet is either something you must know, or something the question will give.

What is in it, and what is not

In the booklet

  • Quantities, symbols and units: including $n$ in "revs min⁻¹ or revs sec⁻¹", $\sigma$ in N m⁻² (Pa) and $\varepsilon$ with no unit.
  • Decimal prefixes: peta to pico.
  • Energy and power: $E_p, E_k, E_h, E_e, E_w$, $P = E/t$, $P = VI = V^2/R = I^2R$, $P = Fv$, $P = 2\pi nT$, efficiency; $g = 9{\cdot}8$ m s⁻², $c_{water} = 4180$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Mechanisms: velocity ratio, the "speed × size" rule, $T = Fr$, $C = \pi d$, moments, equilibrium.
  • Pneumatic systems: $P = F/A$, $A = \pi r^2 = \pi d^2/4$.
  • Structures: stress, strain, $E_s = \frac12 Fx$, Young's modulus, factor of safety.
  • Properties of materials: a table of $E$, yield, ultimate tensile and compressive stress for fourteen entries.
  • Electrical and electronic: Ohm, series and parallel, Kirchhoff, voltage divider $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$.
  • Transistors: $h_{FE} = I_c/I_b$ and $g_m = \Delta I_d/\Delta V_{gs}$.
  • Typical op-amp circuits: inverting, non-inverting, comparator, difference, summing, voltage follower.

Not in the booklet: know it, or the question gives it

  • The base–emitter voltage of a transistor ($V_{BE}$, about 0·7 V). If a question needs it, it states it; say what value you used.
  • MOSFET data: the threshold voltage, and the transfer characteristic ($I_D$ against $V_{GS}$) come with the question.
  • Other specific heat capacities (the booklet gives water only), LDR and thermistor characteristics, data-sheet information.
  • Logic gate symbols, truth tables, Boolean laws and NAND equivalents.
  • Pneumatic and flowchart symbols, and the names of valves and drives.
  • Trigonometry (resolving forces, triangles of forces) and Pythagoras.
  • Definitions: brittleness, ductility, tension and compression, negative feedback, and the properties of materials.
  • Conversions you must do yourself: rev/min to rev/s, mm² to m², kN to N.

The materials table needs special care. Several entries are ranges (stainless steels 190–200), some are blanks ("–": for example cast iron has no yield stress listed because it is brittle), and $E$ is in kN mm⁻² while stresses are in N mm⁻². For mild steel, $E = 196$ kN mm⁻², yield stress 220 N mm⁻² and ultimate tensile stress 430 N mm⁻². A question will usually tell you which entry in a range to use, or give you the value outright, but check the question's wording.

How to use the booklet

  1. Name the unknown in the question, with its symbol.
  2. List what you are given, with units. Convert now.
  3. Find the relationship that has your unknown and your givens in it. Check the symbols match (is $n$ revs per second?).
  4. Rearrange the symbols first, then substitute. The booklet prints each relationship one way: $h_{FE} = I_c/I_b$ becomes $I_b = I_c/h_{FE}$; $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$ becomes $R_1 = R_2V_1/V_2$.
  5. Use the diagram. In the booklet's op-amp pages, $V_i$ and $V_{ref}$ are marked on the circuit, so the comparator rule only makes sense with the diagram beside it.

Find the formula: the speed game

Ten random situations; pick the booklet relationship you would use. It covers every relationship in the booklet, from energy to op-amps. Afterwards the game tells you which booklet section it lives in, so you build a map in your head. Switch to "the same section" for harder wrong options.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I can do the sum in my head, why do I have to write out the formula and the substitution?"

Check the published marking instructions for exactly how marks are split. If you slip on the arithmetic, the method may still earn credit, as long as the examiner can see it."

Prefix and unit drill

Ten conversions, typed answers. Write your answer as a plain number (4700), in e-notation (4.7e3) or as 4.7×10^3: all are accepted, but it must be exactly right.

Rounding rules

  • Never round in the middle. Keep the full calculator value (use the ANS or memory key) and round only the final answer. The 2025 and 2026 reports give this advice (the 2024 report records marks lost to rounding intermediate answers), and the 2026 report adds that rounding instructions are "on the front cover of the question paper". In 2024 "some candidates rounded intermediate calculated answers, which made their final answer incorrect".
  • Round to what the paper asks. If the front cover (or the question) says 3 significant figures, give 3. If you are practising and nothing is stated, use 3 significant figures.
  • Counting significant figures. Start at the first non-zero digit and count to the right: 0.00470 has three (4, 7, 0); 1870 has three or four, so write 1.87 kN if you mean three. Zeros at the front never count.
  • Use the π key. The 2024 report noted that "many candidates consistently used the π button on their calculators, which led them to more accurate final answers". Typing 3.14 is a rounded intermediate value.
  • Every final answer has a unit, and a sensible prefix: 1870 N or 1.87 kN, but not 1.87 N.

The rounding trap

A 1100 kg car at 18 m s⁻¹ brakes to rest; 80% of its kinetic energy goes into the brake discs (total mass 8.0 kg, specific heat capacity 490 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, as the question would state). Slide the number of figures kept in each intermediate answer and watch the final temperature rise.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I round to 3 significant figures as I go, my answer will still be accurate to 3 figures, won't it?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "No. Each rounding can nudge the last figure, and the nudges add up through a chain of steps. That is exactly what the widget shows. Rounding in the middle is a habit the 2025 and 2026 reports ask candidates to avoid, and the 2024 report records marks lost to it."

Worked example 1 · calculation · torque, speed and power

A winch-drum motor delivers a torque of 24 N m at 1200 rev/min. Calculate its output power. [3]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $P = 2\pi nT$ Relationship from the booklet; $n$ is revolutions per second.
  2. $n = 1200 \div 60 = 20\ \text{rev/s}$, so $P = 2\pi \times 20 \times 24$ Convert first, then substitute. Use the π key.
  3. $P = 3015.9\ldots = 3020\ \mathrm{W}$ (3 s.f.) Give the unit. $3.02\ \mathrm{kW}$ is equally good.

Common slip: leaving $n = 1200$ gives $2\pi \times 1200 \times 24 = 180\,956\ \mathrm{W}$, sixty times too big. A one-line sense check (a hand-held winch motor is not 180 kW) would have caught it.

Worked example 2 · calculation · a chain with rounding

A car of mass 1100 kg brakes from 18 m s⁻¹ to rest. 80% of its kinetic energy becomes heat in the brake discs, which have a total mass of 8.0 kg and a specific heat capacity of 490 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate the temperature rise of the discs. [4]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1100 \times 18^2 = 178\,200\ \mathrm{J}$ Kinetic energy: keep every digit.
  2. $E_h = 0.80 \times 178\,200 = 142\,560\ \mathrm{J}$ 80% goes to the discs.
  3. $E_h = cm\Delta T$, so $\Delta T = \dfrac{E_h}{cm} = \dfrac{142\,560}{490 \times 8.0}$ Rearrange the symbols, then substitute.
  4. $\Delta T = 36.4\ \mathrm{K}$ (3 s.f.) Round once, at the very end. A 36.4 K rise is 36.4 °C.

Common slip: rounding $E_k$ to $178\,000$ and then $E_h$ to $142\,000$ gives $\Delta T = 36.2\ \mathrm{K}$: wrong in the third figure, which is the one that is marked.

Worked example 3 · calculation · stress, strain and a units trap

A mild steel rod 2.5 m long and 12 mm in diameter carries a tensile load of 15 kN. Use the Data Booklet to calculate its extension. [5]

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. $A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi \times 12^2}{4} = 113.1\ \mathrm{mm^2}$ Area of the circular section; work in mm² because stress will be in N mm⁻².
  2. $\sigma = \dfrac{F}{A} = \dfrac{15\,000}{113.1\ldots} = 132.6\ \mathrm{N\,mm^{-2}}$ Convert 15 kN to 15 000 N. This is below the 220 N mm⁻² yield stress, so Young's modulus applies.
  3. $E = 196\ \mathrm{kN\,mm^{-2}} = 196\,000\ \mathrm{N\,mm^{-2}}$ From the materials table; convert kN to N so the units match the stress.
  4. $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\sigma}{E} = \dfrac{132.6\ldots}{196\,000} = 6.77 \times 10^{-4}$ Strain has no unit.
  5. $\Delta l = \varepsilon \times l = 6.7668 \times 10^{-4} \times 2500 = 1.69\ \mathrm{mm}$ Strain times original length (2.5 m = 2500 mm). Answer with its unit.

Common slip: using $E = 196$ without converting kN to N gives a strain 1000 times too big, and an extension of 1.7 m, which is obviously absurd for a 2.5 m rod carrying 15 kN.

⚠ From the course reports

The 2024 report records that for a torque calculation on a drum, some candidates "did not use the radius of the drum", and in a strain-energy question "a few" lost the mark by not using "the correct units". The 2026 report names "not round intermediate values" as advice for teachers and candidates alike. These are not engineering mistakes. They are layout and habit mistakes, and the three-line layout prevents them.

3 · Answering well: command words, descriptions and calculations EXAM SKILLS

You can know the engineering and still lose marks by answering a different question from the one asked. The 2024 course report puts it plainly: "Questions requiring a detailed written response proved to be challenging, and often candidates provided a non-descriptive answer which lacked the detail required to achieve marks at this level." This chapter is about closing that gap.

Command words

The first word of a question tells you what shape of answer is wanted. These are working definitions for this site; read each question's own wording as well, because many say how many points to give (two roles, two adaptations, two examples).

Descriptive answers: point + reason

The commonest weak answer is a statement of what happens with no because. "The transistor switches on" is true, and worth almost nothing. A good descriptive answer is a chain in which every link is a cause and an effect:

1 Changewhat changes first (light falls, button pressed, voltage rises)
2 Becausewhat that does to the next part (resistance rises, valve actuates)
3 Sothe effect on the circuit's signal (voltage across LDR rises above the threshold)
4 Resultwhat the output does (MOSFET conducts, lamp lights)

Count the marks available and the links you are writing: a 3-mark description usually needs three separate, correct points. Don't repeat yourself, don't write the question back, and name each component by its proper name (LDR, comparator, solenoid valve, MOSFET).

Sort these. Which answers would gain the mark, and which would lose it?

"The transistor turns on."
"In the dark the LDR's resistance rises, so the voltage across it rises. When the gate voltage passes the threshold, the MOSFET conducts and current flows through the lamp."
"This design is better because it is more efficient."
"The cylinder moves out when you press the button."
"The push-button 3/2 valve actuates, so the 5/2 valve changes state, supply air reaches the cap end and the piston outstrokes."
"A MOSFET is voltage-operated: its gate draws almost no current, so a high-resistance divider can drive it directly. A BJT needs base current."
"A civil engineer designs things and makes sure they work."

Describing pneumatic circuits: the exact words

Both the 2025 and 2026 reports single out pneumatic descriptions. The 2026 report says: "Candidates must use correct terminology, with a statement referring to 3/2 valves actuating, 5/2 valves changing state, and a piston instroking or outstroking." In 2026 "few candidates correctly described the full operation of the pneumatic circuit"; in 2025 "a few" used "appropriate terminology". So learn this vocabulary like a drill:

  • a 3/2 valve (three ports, two positions) actuates (is pressed, or its roller is struck);
  • a 5/2 valve (five ports, two positions) changes state when its pilot signal arrives, and changes back (spring return) when the pilot signal goes;
  • air is supplied to one end of the cylinder while the other end exhausts;
  • the piston outstrokes (rod extends) or instrokes (rod retracts).

Watch the air: a 3/2 valve, a 5/2 valve and a cylinder

A push-button 3/2 valve sends a pilot signal to a spring-return 5/2 valve that drives a double-acting cylinder. Hold the button, release it, or run the auto demo. The sentence under the picture says what is true now, in the words a marker wants. Then write your own description and test it for the key terms.

Worked example 4 · description · the pneumatic circuit above

Describe the operation of the circuit when the push button is pressed and then released. [4]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. When the push button is pressed, the 3/2 valve actuates and supply air flows to the pilot port of the 5/2 valve. Name the valve, say it actuates, say where the air goes.
  2. The 5/2 valve changes state: supply air goes to the cap end of the cylinder (the closed end, opposite the piston rod) and the rod end (the end the rod comes out of) exhausts. Both halves: supply to one end, exhaust from the other.
  3. The piston outstrokes. The movement, in the right word.
  4. When the button is released, the 3/2 valve returns (spring) and the pilot air exhausts, so the 5/2 valve changes state back, air goes to the rod end and the piston instrokes. The return half of the sequence is often forgotten.

Calculation technique

  1. Underline the unknown and circle the numbers and units.
  2. Convert units before substituting (chapter 2).
  3. Write the relationship from the booklet, rearranged for the unknown.
  4. For multi-step questions, find the missing intermediate quantity first: the voltage drop across a resistor, the area of a tube, a reaction force.
  5. Substitute, calculate with full calculator values, and round only at the end.
  6. Write the unit and ask: is the size believable?

The 2024 report records that in a base-current question some candidates did not "calculate the voltage drop over the base resistor", and in a MOSFET question some did not "calculate the drop over the solenoid", so could not find the drain current and then read $V_{GS}$ from the graph. Step 4 is the cure: find the voltage across the resistor before you use Ohm's law on it.

Worked example 5 · calculation · rearranging the voltage divider

A voltage divider is built from a 12 V supply. The lower resistor $R_2$ is 4.7 kΩ and the output across it must be 3.0 V. Calculate the value of the upper resistor $R_1$. [3]

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $V_1 = 12 - 3.0 = 9.0\ \mathrm{V}$ $V_1$ in the booklet formula is the voltage across $R_1$, not the supply.
  2. $\dfrac{V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{R_1}{R_2}$, so $R_1 = \dfrac{R_2 V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{4700 \times 9.0}{3.0}$ Rearrange the symbols first, then substitute.
  3. $R_1 = 14\,100\ \Omega = 14.1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ With unit.

Common slip: using $V_1 = 12\ \mathrm{V}$ gives 18.8 kΩ. The 2024 report says that "many" found it "challenging to manipulate the voltage divider formula V1/V2=R1/R2 to find the value of R1".

Worked example 6 · calculation · a transistor switching a load

An npn transistor with $h_{FE} = 100$ must pass a collector current of 40 mA through a 120 Ω load on a 9.0 V supply. The base is fed from a 5.0 V logic output through a base resistor $R_b$. Take $V_{BE} = 0.7\ \mathrm{V}$ (the question would give this). Calculate (a) the base current needed, [2] (b) the value of $R_b$, [3] (c) the power dissipated between the collector and emitter. [2]

Show solution [7 marks]
  1. $h_{FE} = \dfrac{I_c}{I_b}$, so $I_b = \dfrac{I_c}{h_{FE}} = \dfrac{0.040}{100}$ (a) Relationship and substitution, in amperes.
  2. $I_b = 0.00040\ \mathrm{A} = 0.40\ \mathrm{mA}$ (a) Answer with unit.
  3. $V_{R_b} = 5.0 - 0.7 = 4.3\ \mathrm{V}$ (b) The voltage across the base resistor: the supply minus the base–emitter drop.
  4. $R_b = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{4.3}{0.00040}$ (b) Ohm's law on the base resistor alone.
  5. $R_b = 10\,750\ \Omega \approx 10.8\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$ (b) With unit and sensible rounding.
  6. $V_{load} = IR = 0.040 \times 120 = 4.8\ \mathrm{V}$, so $V_{CE} = 9.0 - 4.8 = 4.2\ \mathrm{V}$ (c) The transistor takes whatever the load does not.
  7. $P = V_{CE}I_C = 4.2 \times 0.040 = 0.168\ \mathrm{W}$ (c) Power between collector and emitter. The 2026 report says few candidates calculated this.

Check: with the transistor passing 40 mA, the load needs only 4.8 V, so the transistor is in its active region and $I_c = h_{FE}I_b$ is allowed. If the load tried to take more current than the supply could give, the transistor would saturate and $I_b$ would be "more than enough".

Worked example 7 · calculation · moments and reactions

A 5.0 m beam rests on a hinge at A (left) and a roller at B (right). Downward loads of 8 kN and 6 kN act 1.5 m and 4.0 m from A. Calculate the reactions at A and B. [4]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Moments about A: $R_B \times 5.0 = (8 \times 1.5) + (6 \times 4.0)$ Take moments about the hinge so $R_A$ drops out. Every force is multiplied by its perpendicular distance (the 2024 report notes a few candidates forgot the distance).
  2. $R_B = \dfrac{12 + 24}{5.0} = 7.2\ \mathrm{kN}$ upward Answer with unit and direction.
  3. $\Sigma F_v = 0$: $R_A + R_B = 8 + 6 = 14\ \mathrm{kN}$ Vertical equilibrium for the second unknown.
  4. $R_A = 14 - 7.2 = 6.8\ \mathrm{kN}$ upward Check by taking moments about B: $6.8 \times 5.0 = 34 = 8 \times 3.5 + 6 \times 1.0$. ✔

4 · The assignment: analyse, design, build, test, evaluate ASSIGNMENT · 50 MARKS

The assignment is 31% of your grade. It is the part of the course where you behave like an engineer instead of answering questions about one: you take a problem, analyse it, design a solution, build or simulate it, test it and judge it. And because the course reports describe it so consistently, you can plan for it very precisely.

The conditions

ConditionWhat the specification says
Time8 hours, started once all the course content has been delivered.
WhoYou, alone. No group work.
WhereA supervised classroom, so the work is demonstrably yours, without interruption by periods of teaching.
Help"Without any teacher or lecturer intervention or guidance" at any stage.
ResourcesClosed book: no notes, internet, exemplars or wall displays. The Data Booklet may be used.
EvidenceLikely to include completed solutions, prints from simulation software, photographs of built models, and records of testing and evaluation. Everything is handed in on paper, even if you made it on a computer.
Word countNone.
MarkingSet by SQA / Qualifications Scotland each year and marked externally. Once handed in, it is not returned to you for more work.

The five skills and where the marks are

SkillMarksWhat it looks likeWhat the reports say
Analysis4–8Read the problem, pull out the specification, complete a control or system diagram.2026: "most" achieved full marks analysing a specification and completing a control diagram.
Designing a solution8–12Calculate component values, draw circuits, logic diagrams, flowcharts.2024–25: "most" achieved full marks designing and simulating an electronic circuit. 2025: "many" did not use the least number of logic gates by converting to NAND equivalents.

The pattern is clear. Building and simulating are where candidates score well. Testing and evaluating are the skills where the marks are won and lost, and between them they can be worth up to 28 of the 50 marks. If you only practise building, you are practising the part you will probably already do well.

Evidence the marker can read

  • Clear photographs and screenshots. The 2026 report asks that they are "clear, so that components are easily identified"; the 2025 report warns that unclear ones risk "text being unreadable or cut off, or components and connections not being clear". Zoom in, fill the frame, check the printout before you hand in.
  • Label substituted components and show orientation. If the simulator has no LDR and you used a variable resistor set to 20 kΩ, write that on the diagram. Mark the legs or terminals of polarised parts (diode, transistor, LED, electrolytic capacitor) so the marker can see which way round they are. In 2025 "many" lost marks here, and some building with real parts did not make pipes "easily identifiable and followed".
  • Use the software to match the task. Read each task carefully: the 2025 report warns that a task may look like a past one but have "significant differences".

Test plans: the test is not the expected result

A test plan is a table with at least these columns: the test (what you do), the expected result (what you predict will happen, with a value and unit or a clear state), and, as you carry the tests out, the actual result and the amendment. In 2024 candidates "did not understand the difference between an expected resulted and the test to be carried out". Here is the difference:

The test (an action)

"Set the LDR to 20 kΩ and measure the gate voltage."

The expected result (a prediction)

"The gate voltage is about 2.05 V, above the 2.0 V threshold, so the lamp only just begins to glow."

The expected result is worked out before you test, from your design calculation. If the actual result does not match it, you have found a fault in the circuit or in your calculation, and that is the whole point. And then: make the amendment before the next test. The 2026 report says some candidates "carried out the planned tests and recorded the actual results" but "did not make appropriate amendments before moving onto the next test, as instructed".

Evaluating: one specification point at a time

The 2025 report says candidates "did not refer to the performance, testing and amendments made to meet each specification point". The 2026 report says some "stated that all specification points were met, when the performance of their amended flowchart did not provide evidence to support this". The cure is a fixed sentence pattern for each point:

1 The pointname the specification point (S2: lamp switches on at 20 kΩ)
2 The evidencequote your actual result, with value and unit, and say where it is (test 2, screenshot 3)
3 The verdictmet, partly met or not met, by comparing evidence with the specification
4 The amendmentif not fully met: what you changed, or what you would change, and why

Never write "all the specification points were met" unless each one has been shown separately. Evidence means actual performance (what the circuit, program or structure really did), not what it should have done.

Counting integrated circuits, not gates

In 2026 "many candidates" "did not refer to factors such as manufacture and/or cost in relation to the number of Integrated Circuits used" and focused on "the number of gates used for each design, instead of the Integrated Circuits found in the data sheets provided". In 2025, "many" did not use the least number of logic gates possible by converting to NAND equivalents. A chip contains several gates of one type, so what costs money is the number of chips.

Worked example 8 · design · fewest gates versus fewest chips

A circuit has $X = A \cdot B + C$. Compare an AND/OR design with a NAND-only design, using the data sheet fact that a 7400-series quad 2-input NAND has four gates, a quad 2-input AND has four gates and a quad 2-input OR has four gates. Which design needs fewer chips? [4]

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. AND/OR design: one AND gate ($A\cdot B$) and one OR gate (with $C$): 2 gates. The fewest gates.
  2. But an AND and an OR are different gate types, so they need 2 chips (a quad AND and a quad OR), each mostly unused. Count chips from the data sheet.
  3. NAND-only: $X = \overline{\overline{A\cdot B}\cdot\overline{C}}$, built from NAND($A$, $B$), NAND($C$, $C$) as a NOT, and a final NAND of the two: 3 gates. Double inversion removed; a NAND with tied inputs is a NOT.
  4. All three are NAND gates, so they fit in 1 chip (a quad 2-input NAND has four gates, one left spare). The NAND design uses more gates but fewer chips, and chips are what cost money and board space. Conclusion: the NAND-only design is cheaper to make.

Structures in the assignment

The 2026 report notes that "most candidates correctly identified the vertical reactions" in a structures task but "many did not correctly identify whether each support could withstand the reaction force". The task has a final step: compare each reaction with the capacity of its support, and state a clear conclusion for each support.

'It worked' is a claim; '2.05 V at the gate with the LDR at 20 kΩ, lamp lit, screenshot 3' is evidence. And if a test failed and you wrote 'all met', the marker can see the contradiction. A practice mini-assignment: the chart-table night lantern

Practice material, invented for this site

The task below is a small invented practice assignment. It is not a past assignment and it is not marked by Qualifications Scotland. Its marks and stages show the shape of the real thing; a real assignment has several tasks and is much longer. Real past and specimen assignments are on the Higher Engineering Science subject page of the Qualifications Scotland website.

The problem. HMS Ratchet's navigator wants a lamp over the chart table that comes on by itself at dusk and stays off in daylight.

Given. Supply 9 V. LDR: about 2 kΩ in bright daylight, 20 kΩ at dusk, 100 kΩ in the dark. Lamp: 60 mA at 9 V. n-channel enhancement MOSFET: threshold gate–source voltage 2.0 V; the lamp is fully on (60 mA) when $V_{GS}$ is 4.0 V or more. (A real question gives data like this in a data sheet or graph.)

Specification.

  1. S1 The lamp is off in bright daylight (LDR about 2 kΩ).
  2. S2 The lamp switches on when the LDR reaches about 20 kΩ (dusk).
  3. S3 In the dark (LDR 100 kΩ) the lamp is fully bright, drawing 60 mA.
  4. S4 The control circuit works from the single 9 V battery and uses no more than three components besides the lamp and battery.
Stage 1 · Analysis (4–8 marks in a real assignment)

Turn each specification point into a number or a state you can test. Draw the control diagram: input (LDR in a divider: light level in, voltage out) → process (MOSFET as a voltage-operated switch with a threshold of 2.0 V) → output (lamp: on or off). Note what the specification does not say, such as a gate current, so you can see why a MOSFET fits: its gate draws almost no current, so it does not load the divider.

Stage 2 · Designing (8–12 marks)

At dusk the LDR is 20 kΩ and the gate must reach the 2.0 V threshold. The LDR therefore has 2.0 V across it, so the fixed resistor has $9.0 - 2.0 = 7.0\ \mathrm{V}$. From $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$: $R = \dfrac{20\,\mathrm{k\Omega} \times 7.0}{2.0} = 70\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$. Choose the nearest standard value you have, 68 kΩ. Predict each case: bright daylight gives $9 \times 2/(68+2) = 0.26\ \mathrm{V}$ (off); dusk gives $9 \times 20/(68+20) = 2.05\ \mathrm{V}$ (just on); dark gives $9 \times 100/(68+100) = 5.36\ \mathrm{V}$, above 4.0 V so the lamp is fully on. These predictions become the expected results in your test plan.

Stage 3 · Building or simulating (8–12 marks)

Build it in the simulator with a variable resistor standing in for the LDR. Your evidence: a clear screenshot of the whole circuit with every value shown, a note that the variable resistor substitutes for the LDR, the MOSFET's gate, drain and source clearly identifiable, and meters placed where you will read them. Print it large enough to read.

Stage 4 · Testing (8–14 marks)

Use the builder below. For every row: an action in the first box, a prediction (with value and unit, or a state) in the second, the measured number in the third, pass or fail, and an amendment before the next test if it failed.

Test-plan builder

The three rows are the night lantern's tests, with the expected results from the design above. Write the actual results as you "test", mark pass or fail, and add an amendment to any failed test. Stage 5 · Evaluation (8–14 marks)

Write one evaluation for each of S1 to S4 using the four-part pattern above (the point, the evidence, the verdict, the amendment). Use the checker below on your sentences.

The weak example fails exactly where the course reports say candidates fail: "the circuit worked well" gives no evidence, "so this was met" gives no value, and "all the specification points were met" is a blanket claim. The stronger example quotes a measured value, says where the evidence is, gives a verdict, and, for the point that was only partly met, records what was changed and retested.

Question A · analogue electronics · 10 marks

The lamp on the Admiralty Board's chart table is to come on when a sensor voltage $V_2$ rises above a reference voltage $V_1$. A difference amplifier compares them: when $V_2$ is above $V_1$ the op-amp output rises, which switches the transistor on. The output drives the base of an npn transistor through a resistor $R_b$, and the transistor switches a 6.0 V, 60 mA lamp from a 9 V supply. The transistor is not saturated: it conducts as an amplifier, so you use $I_c = h_{FE}I_b$ with the minimum $h_{FE}$ given.

(a) The amplifier gain for the difference is $R_f / R_i$. Calculate the output voltage $V_o$. [2]
(b) The lamp must carry its full 60 mA. Calculate the minimum base current needed. [2]
(c) Calculate the value of $R_b$ that gives this base current when $V_o$ is as in (a). [3]
(d) With the lamp carrying 60 mA and dropping 6.0 V, calculate the power dissipated in the transistor. [1]
(e) Explain why $R_b$ is needed between the amplifier and the transistor base. [2]

Question B · structures · 10 marks

A 6.0 m beam is pinned (hinged) at A and rests on a roller at B. It carries a 12 kN vertical load 2.0 m from A, a 20 kN load acting 30° below the horizontal at 4.0 m from A, pushing towards B, and a uniformly distributed load of 4 kN m⁻¹ along the whole length.

(a) Resolve the 20 kN force into vertical and horizontal components. [2]
(b) Calculate the total of the uniformly distributed load, and state where it acts. [1]
(c) Take moments about A to calculate the reaction at B. [3]
(d) Calculate the vertical reaction at A. [2]
(e) State the horizontal reaction at A, and calculate the magnitude of the resultant reaction at A. [2]

Question C · drives and pneumatics · 10 marks

A winch motor delivers 5.0 N m at 1440 rev/min to gear A. The compound gear train has gear A (18 teeth) meshing with gear B (72 teeth); gear C (20 teeth) is fixed to the same shaft as B and meshes with gear D (60 teeth) on the output shaft. The gear train is 85% efficient. A double-acting cylinder (bore 63 mm, piston rod 20 mm) then lifts a guard at an air pressure of 6.0 × 10⁵ Pa.

(a) Calculate the output speed and the velocity ratio of the gear train. [3]
(b) Calculate the output torque of the gear train. [3]
(c) Calculate the force the cylinder exerts on the outstroke and on the instroke. [4]
