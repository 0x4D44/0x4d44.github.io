Place on the ship · the Engine Deck, below the waterline

Drive systems & pneumatics

A ship of the line does not move, hoist, steer or fire by wishing. Somewhere below the gun-decks a motor turns a shaft, a train of gears or a belt turns the shaft into the speed and force you actually need, and a piston pushed by compressed air does the last bit of work. This deck follows that path from the capstan to the gun-port piston. It is worth 10 to 20 marks in the question paper, and it is full of calculations and of descriptions that candidates throw away by being vague.

What this deck covers. Torque, power and efficiency of rotating machines; simple, idler and compound gear trains; belt, chain, rack and pinion, worm and wheel, and worm and nut drives; couplings, bearings, brakes and clutches; pneumatic cylinders, valves and symbols; sequential circuits of up to three cylinders; and electro-pneumatic circuits with relays and limit switches.

1 · Torque, power and rotation DRIVE SYSTEMS

A force pushing in a straight line makes things speed up. A force acting round an axis makes things spin, and the quantity that measures how strongly it does so is torque. Push a spanner at the end of its handle and the nut turns easily. Push the same force close to the nut and nothing happens. The force is identical, so what changed is the distance from the axis.

📘 Torque

Torque is the turning effect of a force about an axis. For a force $F$ acting at right angles to a radius $r$:

$$T = Fr$$

$T$ is in newton metres (N m), $F$ in newtons (N) and $r$ in metres (m). The $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of the force. For a rope wound on a drum, $r = d \div 2$: the drum radius, measured to the rope, which is half the diameter.

You have met this formula before as a moment, $M = Fx$ in the Data Booklet, and it really is the same thing: a force multiplied by a perpendicular distance. We tend to say "moment" when something is held still (a beam balancing) and "torque" when something is being turned (a shaft delivering power).

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2024 course report

The 2024 course report says some candidates "did not use the radius of the drum" in a torque calculation. If the question gives a diameter, halve it first. Write "$r = d \div 2 = \dots$ m" as its own line so that the marker can see you did.

Rotational speed, and the unit trap

Machines are described by how many times they turn in a given time. Motors are usually quoted in revolutions per minute (rev/min, or "rpm"), but the Data Booklet formula for power wants revolutions per second. The symbol is $n$ in both cases and the booklet lists the two units, so you must read which one a formula needs.

$$n_{\text{rev/s}} = \frac{n_{\text{rev/min}}}{60}$$

So 1200 rev/min is 20 rev/s. This conversion is the commonest slip in the whole deck. Make it the first line of any power calculation.

Power in a rotating shaft

Power is work done per second. Take a shaft turning at $n$ revolutions per second against a torque $T$. In one revolution, a force $F$ at radius $r$ moves through a distance equal to the circumference, $2\pi r$, so the work done is $F \times 2\pi r = 2\pi T$. In one second there are $n$ revolutions, so

$$P = 2\pi n T$$

with $P$ in watts, $n$ in rev/s and $T$ in N m. The straight-line version, also in the booklet, is $P = Fv$. The two agree because the rim speed is $v = \pi d n = 2\pi r n$ (the circumference $C = \pi d$ times $n$ turns per second).

Torque

$$T = Fr$$

$r$ is the radius. Units N m.

Shaft power

$$P = 2\pi n T$$

$n$ in rev/s: divide rev/min by 60.

Linear power

$$P = Fv$$

$v$ in m s⁻¹. Rim speed $v = \pi d n$.

Efficiency

$$\eta = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}$$

Between 0 and 1, or as a percentage. Never above 100%.

Efficiency was met in the first deck as useful energy out over energy in. Because power is energy per second, the same ratio works with powers. Everything you take out of a drive is less than what you put in: the rest is lost as heat in gear teeth, bearings and belts.

Worked example 1 · Calculation · torque on a capstan drum

A rope on the HMS Ratchet capstan drum pulls with a force of 1500 N. The drum has a diameter of 400 mm. Calculate the torque the capstan shaft must supply. [2]

Show solution [2 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $r = d \div 2 = 0.400 \div 2 = 0.200$ m. The radius. Diameter in mm must also become metres.
  2. $T = Fr = 1500 \times 0.200 = 300$ N m. Substitute and give the unit. Using $d$ would give 600 N m and score nothing for the final answer.

Worked example 2 · Calculation · speed of a motor

A motor delivers a power of 2.4 kW at a torque of 38 N m. Calculate its rotational speed in rev/min. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $P = 2\pi n T$, so $n = \dfrac{P}{2\pi T} = \dfrac{2400}{2\pi \times 38}$. Rearrange first, then substitute. 2.4 kW is 2400 W.
  2. $n = 10.05\ldots$ rev/s. Keep the full calculator value; do not round yet.
  3. $n = 10.05\ldots \times 60 = 603$ rev/min. Convert to what the question asked for. Round only here.

Worked example 3 · Calculation · efficiency of a hoist

A motor turns at 1200 rev/min with a torque of 40 N m. It drives a hoist that lifts a load of 24 kN at a steady speed of 0.15 m s⁻¹. Calculate the efficiency of the hoist. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $n = 1200 \div 60 = 20$ rev/s. Convert before using $P = 2\pi nT$.
  2. $P_{in} = 2\pi n T = 2\pi \times 20 \times 40 = 5026.5$ W (keep the full value). Power into the hoist.
  3. $P_{out} = Fv = 24000 \times 0.15 = 3600$ W. Useful power: lifting force times speed, with 24 kN written as 24 000 N.
  4. $\eta = P_{out} \div P_{in} = 3600 \div 5026.5\ldots = 0.716$, or 71.6%. Output over input. A value above 1 would tell you that you have swapped them.

⚓ The capstan: torque, speed and power

Set the pull on the rope (drag the rope end, or use the slider), the drum diameter and the drum speed, and watch the radius, torque, power and rim speed change. Check that the power from $2\pi nT$ matches the power from $Fv$.

Master Shipwright Kerr: "You can, and the answer will be sixty times too big. The booklet's $n$ in that formula is revolutions per second. Divide by sixty first, every time. If your motor comes out at forty megawatts, you forgot."

2 · Gear trains DRIVE SYSTEMS

A motor is happiest turning fast with modest torque. A capstan needs to turn slowly with enormous torque. Something has to sit between them and trade speed for torque, and the oldest and most reliable thing for the job is a gear train: wheels with interlocking teeth, so that one cannot turn without the other, and with no slipping at all. A drive like this, where interlocking teeth rather than friction carry the load, is called a positive drive.

The wheel that receives power from the motor is the driver (or input gear). The wheel it turns is the driven gear (the output). Two gears can only mesh if their teeth are the same size, so the number of teeth is proportional to the diameter. That is why the Data Booklet can say "size" and leave you to use teeth or diameter, whichever the question gives. Real teeth also need a tiny clearance to mesh freely, so the driven gear can move a small amount before the driver pushes it when the direction reverses. That free play is called backlash; it is small, and does not change the velocity ratio.

The velocity ratio

📘 Velocity ratio (VR)

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{speed of input}}{\text{speed of output}}$$ and, from the booklet, input speed × input size = output speed × output size. For a single pair of gears this makes

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{teeth on driven gear}}{\text{teeth on driver gear}}$$

A VR greater than 1 is a reduction (the output turns slower than the input); a VR below 1 is a speed-up.

Why does a big driven gear turn slowly? Because for every tooth that passes the meeting point on the small driver, one tooth passes on the driven wheel. A 20-tooth driver must make three whole turns for a 60-tooth driven gear to make one.

Torque and the trade-off

In an ideal gearbox nothing is lost, so the power in equals the power out. Since $P = 2\pi nT$, if the speed falls by a factor of three, the torque must rise by a factor of three. Real gear trains lose some power as heat, so the torque rises by a little less. For a drive with efficiency $\eta$:

$$T_{out} = T_{in} \times \text{VR} \times \eta$$

This is a consequence of $P = 2\pi nT$ and $\eta = P_{out} \div P_{in}$, not a separate booklet formula, so be ready to show it from those two if you are asked. You never get something for nothing: a reduction gear multiplies torque only by giving up speed.

Idlers, and which way it turns

Two meshing gears turn in opposite directions. If a middle gear is placed between two gears, the middle gear reverses the direction, then the third gear reverses it again, so the first and third gears turn the same way. This middle gear is an idler. Its job is to change the direction, or to bridge a gap between shafts that are too far apart. It does not change the velocity ratio: the idler's teeth are counted on both sides, so they cancel.

Compound trains

A single pair of gears can give a reduction of 5:1 or so before the big gear becomes absurdly large. A bigger reduction in a small space uses a compound train: two gears are fixed to the same shaft (B and C in the figure) so they turn at the same speed. The first pair reduces the speed, then the second pair reduces it again. The velocity ratios multiply:

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{teeth}_B}{\text{teeth}_A} \times \frac{\text{teeth}_D}{\text{teeth}_C}$$

With two meshes, a compound train of this kind turns its output the same way as its input. Count the meshes to find the direction: an even number gives the same direction, an odd number the opposite.

Worked example 4 · Calculation · a simple pair

A motor turning at 1500 rev/min drives a 20-tooth gear that meshes with a 60-tooth gear. The input torque is 12 N m and the efficiency of the gears is 90%. Calculate the output speed and the output torque. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\text{VR} = 60 \div 20 = 3$. Driven over driver. A reduction, so the output is slower.
  2. Output speed $= 1500 \div 3 = 500$ rev/min. Output speed is input speed divided by VR. No need to convert to rev/s here.
  3. $T_{out} = T_{in} \times \text{VR} \times \eta = 12 \times 3 \times 0.90$. The torque is multiplied by the VR but cut by the efficiency.
  4. $T_{out} = 32.4$ N m. An ideal gear train would give 36 N m; the 10% loss takes off 3.6 N m.

Worked example 5 · Calculation · a train with an idler

A 24-tooth driver gear meshes with a 36-tooth idler, which meshes with a 48-tooth output gear. The driver turns at 600 rev/min clockwise. Calculate the output speed and state its direction. Explain what the idler does. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\text{VR} = 48 \div 24 = 2$. Only the first and last gears count. The idler's 36 teeth cancel.
  2. Output speed $= 600 \div 2 = 300$ rev/min. Divide by the velocity ratio.
  3. Direction: clockwise, the same as the driver. Two meshes, so two reversals.
  4. The idler only changes the direction of rotation (and bridges the distance); it does not change the speed or the VR. The explanation mark. Say what it does and what it does not do.

Worked example 6 · Calculation · a compound train

A compound train has a 15-tooth gear A on the input shaft meshing with a 45-tooth gear B. A 20-tooth gear C is fixed to the same shaft as B and meshes with a 50-tooth gear D on the output shaft. The input shaft turns at 1800 rev/min with a torque of 5 N m. The efficiency of the whole train is 85%. Calculate the output speed and torque, and say which way the output turns relative to the input. [5]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\text{VR} = \dfrac{45}{15} \times \dfrac{50}{20} = 3 \times 2.5 = 7.5$. Multiply the VR of each stage. Do not add them.
  2. Output speed $= 1800 \div 7.5 = 240$ rev/min. Input speed over VR.
  3. $T_{out} = 5 \times 7.5 \times 0.85$. Torque is multiplied by the VR and by the efficiency.
  4. $T_{out} = 31.875 \approx 31.9$ N m. Keep 31.875 in the calculator; round at the end.
  5. Same direction as the input. Two meshes (A with B, C with D), so the direction reverses twice.

⚠ Exam lookout · describing the benefit

If a question asks why a reduction gearbox is used, a bare "to slow it down" earns little. Say that the gears reduce the speed and increase the torque, and say why that suits the job: the motor runs at its efficient speed while the load gets the high torque it needs.

3 · Belts, chains, racks and worms DRIVE SYSTEMS

Gears are not always the right answer. The shafts may be far apart, the machine may need to turn a corner, or the output may need to move in a straight line rather than spin. The Higher course names five other drives you must be able to select and calculate: belt, chain, rack and pinion, worm and wheel, and worm and nut. Master Shipwright Kerr's rule is to ask three questions of any job: how far apart are the shafts, does the speed ratio have to be exact, and what should happen if the load jams?

Belt drives

A belt joins two pulleys and carries the drive by friction between the belt and the pulley rim. It is cheap, quiet, needs no lubricating oil and copes with shafts a long way apart. It also has a built-in safety feature: if the load jams, the belt slips and the motor is not wrecked. The same slipping is its weakness, because the output can lag behind the input by an unpredictable amount.

The velocity ratio comes from the pulley diameters, using the booklet relationship input speed × input size = output speed × output size, where "size" is the diameter. The speed of the belt itself is the rim speed of either pulley, $v = \pi d n$ with $n$ in rev/s.

  • Flat belt. A simple band of leather or rubberised fabric on smooth pulleys. It is simple and gentle but needs high tension to grip, and it slips most readily.
  • V-belt. The belt has a trapezoid section and sits in a V-shaped groove. The sides wedge in, so it grips far harder at a given tension and carries more power. It can still slip.
  • Toothed (timing) belt. The inside of the belt has teeth that fit grooves in the pulley. It cannot slip, so, like gears, it is a positive drive and the speed ratio is exact, and it needs less tension. That precision is why it is used to keep two parts of a machine in step. It gives up the "safety slip".

An open belt makes both pulleys turn the same way. A crossed belt is twisted into a figure of eight and makes them turn in opposite directions, but the two sides rub against one another where they cross, so the belt wears faster.

Chain drives

A roller chain runs over toothed wheels called sprockets. Like a toothed belt, it is a positive drive (the teeth, not friction, carry the load): the rollers sit in the teeth, so there is no slip and the speed ratio is exact. The ratio uses the teeth on the sprockets, with the same rule as for gears:

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{teeth on driven sprocket}}{\text{teeth on driver sprocket}}$$

Chains carry far more torque than a belt of the same width and survive oil, heat and dirt, but they are noisier, they need lubrication, and they stretch as the pins wear. Both sprockets turn the same way. A bicycle is the obvious chain drive, and so is the chain-pump on the lower deck.

Turning rotation into a straight line

Two drives change rotation into linear motion. In a rack and pinion, a toothed wheel (the pinion) meshes with a straight toothed bar (the rack). One turn of the pinion moves the rack through one circumference of the pinion, so the rack's speed is $v = \pi d n$, where $d$ is the diameter of the pinion. It is quick, direct and reversible, which is exactly what a ship's steering gear needs.

In a worm and nut (or lead screw), a threaded rod, the worm, turns inside a nut that is stopped from rotating, so the nut slides along the rod. The lead is how far the nut advances in one turn of the screw. The nut speed is therefore $v = \text{lead} \times n$. It moves slowly, with great force and fine control, which suits a vice, a jack or the stern gate of a dock.

Worm and wheel

A worm is a screw-shaped gear that meshes with a toothed worm wheel at right angles to it. For each turn of a one-start worm, the wheel advances by one tooth. So

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{teeth on the wheel}}{\text{number of starts on the worm}}$$

A single worm and wheel can give a reduction of 30:1 or more in a small space, which would take a compound gear train several stages. With a shallow thread the load usually cannot turn the worm backwards, so a worm drive can hold a load without a brake, a handy property for a hoist. The price is efficiency: the teeth slide on each other instead of rolling, so more energy is lost as heat. The torque formula is the same as for any gear train, $T_{out} = T_{in} \times \text{VR} \times \eta$, but $\eta$ may be much lower than for gears.

Worked example 7 · Calculation · a belt drive

A motor pulley of diameter 80 mm turns at 1440 rev/min and drives a belt on a pulley of diameter 240 mm. Calculate the speed of the driven pulley in rev/min and the speed of the belt in m s⁻¹. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $1440 \times 80 = n_{out} \times 240$. The booklet relationship with sizes as diameters. The units of diameter cancel, so mm is fine.
  2. $n_{out} = 480$ rev/min. VR is 3, a reduction.
  3. Belt speed: $n = 1440 \div 60 = 24$ rev/s, $v = \pi d n = \pi \times 0.080 \times 24$. Rim speed of the driver; the diameter must be in metres here.
  4. $v = 6.03$ m s⁻¹. Both pulleys have the same rim speed if the belt does not slip. If it did slip, the driven pulley would turn slower than 480 rev/min.

Worked example 8 · Calculation · rack and pinion

The steering rack of a ship's boat is driven by a pinion of diameter 40 mm turning at 90 rev/min. Calculate the speed of the rack, and the time taken for a stroke of 450 mm. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $n = 90 \div 60 = 1.5$ rev/s. Convert, for use in a formula with SI units.
  2. $v = \pi d n = \pi \times 0.040 \times 1.5 = 0.1885$ m s⁻¹. The rack moves one circumference per turn.
  3. $t = \dfrac{\text{distance}}{v} = \dfrac{0.450}{0.1885\ldots}$. Use the unrounded speed from the calculator.
  4. $t = 2.39$ s. Rounded only at the end.

Worked example 9 · Calculation · worm and wheel

A one-start worm turning at 1200 rev/min drives a worm wheel with 30 teeth. (a) Calculate the speed of the wheel. (b) The worm is replaced by a two-start worm. Calculate the new wheel speed. (c) With the one-start worm, the input torque is 2 N m and the efficiency is 40%. Calculate the output torque. [5]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) $\text{VR} = 30 \div 1 = 30$, so wheel speed $= 1200 \div 30 = 40$ rev/min. Wheel teeth over starts. One mark for the VR, one for the speed.
  2. (b) $\text{VR} = 30 \div 2 = 15$, so wheel speed $= 1200 \div 15 = 80$ rev/min. A two-start worm moves the wheel two teeth per turn, so the wheel goes twice as fast.
  3. (c) $T_{out} = T_{in} \times \text{VR} \times \eta = 2 \times 30 \times 0.40$. Use the VR of the one-start drive.
  4. $T_{out} = 24$ N m. The low efficiency costs a lot of torque: an ideal worm drive would give 60 N m.

Worked example 10 · Calculation · lead screw

A worm and nut has a lead of 5 mm and the screw turns at 300 rev/min. Calculate the speed of the nut, and the time to move it 400 mm. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Nut speed $= \text{lead} \times n = 5 \times 300 = 1500$ mm/min $= 25$ mm s⁻¹. One lead per turn. Convert the minutes to seconds with care.
  2. $t = 400 \div 25$. Distance over speed.
  3. $t = 16$ s. With the unit.

Drawing and reading a drive-system diagram

A drive-system diagram is a simple sketch that follows the power from the source (usually a motor) to the load, one stage at a time. Drawing it makes the speed and torque on each shaft easy to track. Draw it like this:

  1. Left to right. Start with the motor on the left and finish with the load on the right, so that power flows the way you read.
  2. Simple symbols. A box for the motor and the load. Two small flanges for a coupling. A circle with teeth round it for a gear or sprocket, labelled with its number of teeth. A plain circle for a pulley, labelled with its diameter. A straight line for each shaft, and lines round two pulleys (or a chain round two sprockets) for the belt or chain.
  3. Name every shaft. Between each pair of stages, call the shaft A, B, C and write the speed and the torque on it.

To read one, work stage by stage. Find the VR of each stage: here the gears give $60 \div 20 = 3$ and the pulleys give $200 \div 100 = 2$. Divide the speed by each VR in turn. The torque is multiplied by each VR and by the efficiency of that stage. The overall VR is the product of the stage VRs (here 6). A coupling has a VR of 1: it joins two shafts and changes neither speed nor torque, apart from any loss you are told to include. As a check, power is $P = 2\pi n T$ with $n$ in rev/s, and power out equals power in times the overall efficiency, so the speed falls exactly as much as the torque rises, less the losses.

Worked example 11 · Calculation · reading a drive-system diagram

For the drive system in the diagram above, the motor turns at 1440 rev/min and supplies a torque of 20 N m. The coupling and the belt have negligible losses and the gearbox has an efficiency of 90%. Calculate (a) the speed of shaft C, and (b) the torque on shaft C. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Gearbox $\text{VR} = 60 \div 20 = 3$ and belt $\text{VR} = 200 \div 100 = 2$, so overall $\text{VR} = 3 \times 2 = 6$. Each stage divides the speed. The coupling has a VR of 1 and drops out.
  2. Speed of shaft C $= 1440 \div 6 = 240$ rev/min. Shaft B is 480 rev/min on the way. Part (a) is worth 2 marks, one for each VR used.
  3. $T_{out} = T_{in} \times \text{VR} \times \eta = 20 \times 6 \times 0.90$. The torque is multiplied by the VR and cut by the efficiency. Only the gearbox loses anything here.
  4. $T_{out} = 108$ N m. Shaft B carries 54 N m. Check: power in $= 2\pi \times 24 \times 20$ and power out $= 2\pi \times 4 \times 108$, a ratio of 0.90.

4 · Couplings, bearings, brakes and clutches DRIVE SYSTEMS

A drive is more than the gears. Shafts must be joined, held in place so they can turn freely, slowed down when needed, and connected and disconnected from the motor. The course asks for the purpose of each of these parts, so for each one you need a clear, specific sentence: what it does and why the machine needs it.

Couplings: joining shafts

A coupling joins the end of one shaft to the end of another so that torque passes from the first to the second. There are two families.

  • Rigid coupling. The two shafts are locked together as if they were one long shaft, for example by two bolted flanges. It transmits torque without any give or backlash, but it only works if the shafts are lined up almost perfectly. Any misalignment bends the shafts at every turn, overloading the bearings and causing vibration and fatigue cracks.
  • Flexible coupling. An elastic element (rubber, a spring, a toothed spider) sits between the two halves. It tolerates small misalignment, absorbs shock loads and damps vibration, so it protects the motor and the gearbox. It is slightly less stiff in twisting, and the elastic part wears out.

Bearings: holding a shaft while it turns

A shaft cannot just hang in the air, and it cannot rub directly on its frame without wearing out and wasting power. A bearing supports the shaft, keeps it in the right position, and reduces friction. Bearings are named by the direction of the load they carry.

  • A radial bearing carries loads across the shaft, at right angles to its axis. The weight of the shaft, the pull of a belt and the force on a gear tooth all load a shaft radially.
  • A thrust bearing carries loads along the shaft, parallel to its axis. A propeller pushes its shaft along; a worm drive pushes the worm sideways along its own shaft; the vertical spindle of a capstan carries the weight of everything above it.

The course lists four kinds you should know:

  • Plain (or journal) bearing. The shaft (the journal) turns inside a sleeve of soft metal or plastic, ideally on a thin film of oil. It is simple, cheap, quiet and copes well with shocks. It has more friction when starting, and it needs oil or a self-lubricating material.
  • Ball bearing. Hardened balls roll between an inner and an outer ring. Rolling friction is much lower than sliding friction, so it suits high speeds. The balls touch the rings at points, so it is best for light and medium loads.
  • Roller bearing. Cylinders replace the balls. They touch the rings along a line instead of at a point, so they spread the load and carry heavier radial loads than a ball bearing of the same size.
  • Thrust bearing. Balls or rollers between two flat rings to carry axial load. A radial bearing is a poor choice for this job.

Friction on purpose: brakes and clutches

Friction is usually the enemy: it wastes power in bearings and gear teeth, and turns it to heat. In brakes and clutches it is the whole point. Both work by pressing two surfaces together so that friction forces act between them.

Brakes

A brake slows a moving machine by pressing a friction pad on a rotating disc or drum. The friction force turns the kinetic energy of the machine into heat. Because that is all the energy the machine had, the numbers can be large: the brake discs of a heavy vehicle may glow. The temperature rise of the brake discs follows the booklet's heating formula $E_h = cm\Delta T$, and the energy comes from $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2$:

$$\tfrac12 m_{vehicle} v^2 = c\, m_{disc}\, \Delta T$$

If the discs are too small the temperature rises too far and the brake fades, losing its grip. That is why heavy vehicles have big, ventilated discs, and why engineers choose disc materials that have a high specific heat capacity $c$ and survive high temperature. The Data Booklet lists $c$ only for water, so an exam question that needs the value for steel has to give it to you.

Clutches

A clutch connects and disconnects a driving shaft from a driven shaft. A spring presses a lined friction plate on the driven shaft against a spinning plate on the driving shaft. At first the plates are moving at different speeds, so they slip: the friction drags the driven shaft up to speed gently, and the slipping makes heat. When both shafts reach the same speed they lock together, and the drive passes through with no more slip, as long as the load torque is smaller than the most torque the friction can carry. If the load is too large, the clutch slips all the time and overheats. Engineers model the torque capacity of a plate with two friction faces as $2\mu F r$, where $\mu$ is the friction coefficient of the lining, $F$ the clamping force and $r$ the mean radius of the faces. That is a model for understanding, not a Data Booklet formula.

Because it slips before it locks, a clutch lets a heavy machine start gently instead of jerking, and lets the motor be disconnected while it keeps running.

Worked example 12 · Calculation · heating of brake discs

A vehicle of mass 1200 kg is travelling at 15 m s⁻¹ and brakes to a stop. All the kinetic energy is converted into heat in four steel brake discs, each of mass 3 kg. The specific heat capacity of steel is 490 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate the temperature rise of the discs. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1200 \times 15^2 = 135\,000$ J. The energy that has to go somewhere.
  2. Mass of discs $= 4 \times 3 = 12$ kg. All four share the heat. Using one disc's mass is the common slip.
  3. $E_h = cm\Delta T$, so $\Delta T = \dfrac{135000}{490 \times 12}$. Rearrange and substitute, using the same energy as the answer to the first step.
  4. $\Delta T = 23.0$ K (22.96 to four figures). A temperature rise is in K or °C; the same size either way.

Worked example 13 · Description · couplings and bearings

A conveyor on the lower deck is driven by an electric motor through a worm and wheel gearbox. (a) State why a flexible coupling is used between the motor and the gearbox. (b) State why a thrust bearing is fitted on the worm shaft. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) A flexible coupling allows for small misalignment between the motor shaft and the gearbox shaft. The function.
  2. It also absorbs shock loads and vibration, so that the motor and the gearbox are not damaged when the conveyor starts or jams. The reason, in this situation.
  3. (b) The teeth of a worm push the worm sideways, along its own shaft, so there is an axial load. Say where the load comes from.
  4. A thrust bearing is designed to carry load along the shaft, so the worm stays in position and does not wear the motor's bearings. What the thrust bearing does and why a radial bearing would not be enough.

🔥 Brake heating

Set the mass and speed of the load, the mass of each brake disc and how many discs share the work. Press the button to brake: the discs glow and the thermometer shows the temperature rise from $E_h = cm\Delta T$. Make a single small disc and see how fast the temperature climbs. The value of $c$ for steel is stated; in the exam it would be given.

🔗 The clutch: slip, then lock

Engage the clutch with different clamping forces and load torques. While the plates slip, the output shaft accelerates and heat is made. When the speeds match, they lock. If the friction cannot carry the load, the clutch slips for ever.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

Master Shipwright Kerr: "Friction is a tool. In a bearing it wastes power, so you fight it with oil and balls. In a brake you want it to do work against the motion, and in a clutch you want just enough to carry the torque. The skill is putting it where you want it, and leaving it out where you don't."

5 · Pneumatics I: air, cylinders and valves PNEUMATICS

Behind every gun-port on HMS Ratchet there is a piston that slams the port lid open and shut, and in the redstone world of Blockhaven those pistons are really pneumatic cylinders: tubes in which compressed air pushes a piston. Pneumatics is the use of compressed air to do work and to carry signals. It is clean, quick, safe from sparks and easy to organise into sequences, which is why you find it in packing machines, clamps, doors and production lines.

The compressed-air system

A compressor squeezes air into an air receiver, a tank that stores it and smooths out pulses. The air then passes through an air preparation unit (often called FRL): a filter to remove dirt and water, a regulator that sets a steady working pressure, and sometimes a lubricator that adds a mist of oil to protect the valves and seals. Valves then direct the air to the actuators, the parts that do the work, usually cylinders. Used air is simply let out through an exhaust (with a silencer) into the atmosphere.

Cylinders

A single-acting cylinder has one air port. Air pushes the piston out, and a spring pushes it back when the air is released. A double-acting cylinder has two ports, one at each end, and air drives the piston both ways. It needs a valve that can feed one end while exhausting the other. When the piston rod moves out, the cylinder outstrokes. When it moves back in, it instrokes. These two words are the correct terminology, and the examiners ask for them. The end of the cylinder away from the rod is the cap end: air fed there pushes the piston out. The end the rod comes through is the rod end: air fed there pulls the piston back in.

Valves: ports and positions

A directional control valve is named by two numbers: the number of ports (connections) and the number of positions (states). On the symbol, each position is a box, and arrows inside the box show which ports are connected.

  • A 3/2 valve has three ports (1 supply, 2 outlet, 3 exhaust) and two positions. In one position the supply is blocked and the outlet vents to exhaust; in the other the supply is connected to the outlet. It is used to feed a single-acting cylinder, or as a signal valve (a button or a limit switch). When something pushes it into its other position, we say the 3/2 valve actuates.
  • A 5/2 valve has five ports (1 supply, 2 and 4 outlets, 3 and 5 exhausts) and two positions. In one position, supply goes to outlet 4 and outlet 2 exhausts; in the other position, supply goes to outlet 2 and outlet 4 exhausts. It runs a double-acting cylinder: one outlet feeds one end while the other vents. When it flips, we say the 5/2 valve changes state.

Each end of a valve symbol shows what moves it. A push-button, a lever or a roller (a small wheel on a roller lever, pushed by a cam or by a piston rod) acts by hand or mechanical contact. A pilot is a small air signal that pushes the valve. A spring returns it. Inside the valve, a sliding part called the spool joins different ports together; when the valve changes state, the spool has moved. Port numbers 12 and 14 label the pilot lines: in this deck, air at pilot 14 joins supply 1 to outlet 4 (cylinder outstrokes), and air at pilot 12 joins 1 to 2 (cylinder instrokes). A valve with a pilot at both ends and no spring has memory: it stays in whichever state it was last pushed to, even after the signal goes away.

⚠ Exam lookout · terminology

The 2026 course report advises that "candidates must use correct terminology, with a statement referring to 3/2 valves actuating, 5/2 valves changing state, and a piston instroking or outstroking." Never write "the valve opens" or "the cylinder goes forward". Write actuates, changes state, outstrokes and instrokes.

Flow control

Air is compressible, so a loaded cylinder can lurch. A flow control valve (a one-way restrictor) is a narrow gap in the air line, with a one-way valve beside it so that air can bypass the restriction in one direction. Fit it so that it restricts the exhaust air from the cylinder and the piston moves more smoothly, at a speed you can set. Without it, a piston will slam into its end stop.

Force from a cylinder

Pressure is force per unit area. The Data Booklet gives $P = F/A$ and $A = \pi r^2 = \pi d^2 / 4$. Rearranged for force: $F = PA$. Pressure must be in pascals (N m⁻²) and area in m². Air pressures are quoted in bar, and 1 bar is $10^5$ Pa. Gauge pressures are the pressures above the atmosphere, and those are the ones used for force calculations.

On the outstroke, air pushes on the full face of the piston, so $A = \pi D^2 / 4$ where $D$ is the bore (the inside diameter). On the instroke, the piston rod takes up part of the face, so air acts only on a ring (the annulus) of area $\pi (D^2 - d^2)/4$ where $d$ is the rod diameter. The same pressure therefore gives a smaller force on the instroke. Real cylinders also lose some force to friction in the seals, so choose a cylinder with a margin.

Worked example 14 · Calculation · force from a cylinder

A double-acting cylinder has a bore of 50 mm and a piston rod of diameter 20 mm. It is supplied with air at a gauge pressure of 6 bar. Calculate the force on (a) the outstroke and (b) the instroke. [5]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $P = 6 \times 10^5$ Pa. Bar converted to pascals.
  2. (a) $A = \pi D^2/4 = \pi \times 0.050^2 / 4 = 1.9635 \times 10^{-3}$ m². Diameter converted to metres before squaring.
  3. $F = PA = 6 \times 10^5 \times 1.9635\ldots \times 10^{-3} = 1178$ N. Outstroke force.
  4. (b) $A = \pi (D^2 - d^2)/4 = \pi (0.050^2 - 0.020^2)/4 = 1.6493 \times 10^{-3}$ m². The annulus: bore squared minus rod squared.
  5. $F = 6 \times 10^5 \times 1.6493\ldots \times 10^{-3} = 990$ N (989.6). Smaller than on the outstroke.

Worked example 15 · Calculation · choosing a bore

A cylinder must push with a force of 900 N on its outstroke using air at 5 bar gauge. Calculate the minimum bore, and choose a cylinder from the standard bores 32 mm, 40 mm, 50 mm and 63 mm. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $A = F/P = 900 \div (5 \times 10^5) = 1.8 \times 10^{-3}$ m². Rearranged, with the pressure in pascals.
  2. $D = \sqrt{4A/\pi}$, so $D = \sqrt{4 \times 1.8 \times 10^{-3} \div \pi}$. Rearranged from $A = \pi D^2/4$.
  3. $D = 0.0479$ m $= 47.9$ mm. The smallest bore that would give exactly 900 N, with no allowance for friction.
  4. Choose the 50 mm cylinder. The next size up that is available, which also gives a margin for friction. 40 mm is too small, and 63 mm is bigger than needed.

🔀 Valve explorer

Choose a 3/2 or 5/2 valve and operate it. The valve box slides like a spool, the live air path is drawn, and the cylinder instrokes or outstrokes. Watch how the exhaust and supply swap round.

🧰 Cylinder force and speed

Change the bore, rod, pressure and load. The gauge, the force from $F = PA$ and the stroke all update. Push the load beyond what the cylinder can supply and it stalls. Compare the outstroke with the instroke.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"The gauge reads 6 bar on both strokes. Why is the cylinder weaker when it comes in?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "Same pressure, smaller area. The rod is in the way on the rod side, so the air has less to push on. $F = PA$ every time, but $A$ is smaller for the instroke. Put the larger force on the job that needs it."

6 · Pneumatics II: sequential circuits PNEUMATICS

A machine rarely needs one piston doing one job. A stamping press needs a clamp to close before the stamp comes down and to open after it goes back. That kind of ordered behaviour is a sequence, and the course asks you to analyse and draw circuits for sequences of up to three cylinders.

Writing a sequence

The sequence is written with the cylinder letters and a sign: A+ means cylinder A outstrokes and A− means it instrokes. So A+ B+ A− B− reads: A outstrokes, then B outstrokes, then A instrokes, then B instrokes. Each of these four events is a step. Only one step happens at a time, and the next step does not begin until the previous one has finished.

How does the circuit know a step has finished? Each cylinder has a limit valve at each end of its stroke: a 3/2 valve with a roller that the piston rod trips as it arrives. They are named after their cylinder and position: a0 is pressed when cylinder A is fully in and a1 when it is fully out, and so on for b0, b1, c0 and c1.

The rule that builds the circuit

📘 The chain rule
  • Give each cylinder a double-pilot 5/2 valve (it has memory). Pilot 14 makes it outstroke and pilot 12 makes it instroke.
  • Each step is started by the limit valve at the end of the step before it. That limit valve supplies the pilot that starts the next step.
  • The first step also needs the START button, in series with the limit valve that says the machine is in its rest position, so that the sequence only begins from a safe start.

For A+ B+ A− B− this gives four pilot signals, one for each step:

Pilot signals for A+ B+ A− B−. Each step is triggered by the end of the step before it.
StepPilot that firesSignal comes fromBecause the previous step was
1 · A+A, pilot 14START and b0B− (B fully in), at rest
2 · B+B, pilot 14a1A+ (A fully out)
3 · A−A, pilot 12b1B+ (B fully out)
4 · B−B, pilot 12a0A− (A fully in)

A displacement–step diagram shows the same thing as a graph: the position of each piston plotted against the step number. A rising line is an outstroke, a falling line an instroke.

🔧 The circuit simulator: pneumatic sequences

Pick a sequence and press START. Air lines turn blue when pressurised, signal lines turn orange when a limit valve is feeding a pilot, the valve boxes slide as they change state, and the pistons move. Use Step to go one stage at a time. Try the second sequence without the one-way rollers: it jams at the very start, before anything moves. Then fit them.

When signals collide

Now try the sequence A+ B+ B− A−. It looks harmless, but with plain rollers it jams, and it jams at the start. At rest, piston B is instroked and holding limit valve b0 pressed, so b0 is feeding pilot 12 of valve A. When START sends air to pilot 14 of valve A, pilot 12 is already live. Valve A has air on both of its pilot lines and cannot change state, so nothing moves. That is the first conflict.

There is a second one waiting. Suppose the start were cured: A outstrokes and presses a1, which fires pilot 14 of B. B outstrokes and presses b1, and B now needs to return with pilot 12. But a1 is still pressed because A has not moved, so pilot 14 of B is still pressurised too, and valve B is again jammed by opposing signals.

The cure is to remove the old signal before the new one arrives. A one-way trip (idle-return) roller on a limit valve operates only when the piston passes it in one direction, so the signal is a short pulse rather than a signal that is held for as long as the piston sits there. Fitting one-way rollers on both a1 and b0 turns the two held signals into pulses, and the circuit runs. One roller alone is not enough: with a one-way roller on a1 only, the circuit still jams at the start. Other cures exist (reversing valves, cascade circuits, timers), and a question will tell you which one it wants. The principle is always the same: two opposing pilot signals must never be on together.

Three cylinders

The method does not change when you add a third cylinder. For A+ B+ C+ A− B− C−, six steps need six pilot signals in a chain: START·c0 fires A+, a1 fires B+, b1 fires C+, c1 fires A−, a0 fires B−, and b0 fires C−. Notice that every limit valve is used once, in order, and that each signal fires the step that follows the one it ends.

Worked example 16 · Description · the operation of a circuit

Describe the operation of the pneumatic circuit shown above for the sequence A+ B+ A− B−, starting from rest with both pistons instroked. [6]

Show solution [6 marks, typical allocation]
  1. When the START push-button (3/2 valve) is pressed, it actuates; with b0 already pressed, air is supplied to pilot 14 of 5/2 valve A. Name the valve, the verb actuates, and the pilot port.
  2. 5/2 valve A changes state and air is supplied to the cap end of cylinder A, so piston A outstrokes. The valve changes state, the piston outstrokes.
  3. At the end of its stroke piston A actuates roller valve a1, which supplies air to pilot 14 of valve B. The end of one step starts the next. Say which limit valve.
  4. 5/2 valve B changes state and piston B outstrokes. Same pattern for step 2.
  5. Piston B actuates b1, which supplies pilot 12 of valve A; valve A changes state and piston A instrokes. Step 3: the other pilot, the other direction.
  6. Piston A actuates a0, which supplies pilot 12 of valve B; valve B changes state and piston B instrokes, and b0 is pressed again. The cycle stops until START is pressed again. Step 4, then the end condition.

Worked example 17 · Description · a signal conflict

A circuit is built for A+ B+ B− A− using plain roller limit valves and double-pilot 5/2 valves. When START is pressed, nothing moves. Explain why, say where a second conflict would arise, and state the change that fixes both. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. At rest, piston B is instroked, so roller valve b0 is actuated and supplies pilot 12 of valve A. Identify the signal that is being held at the start.
  2. START supplies pilot 14 of valve A at the same time, so valve A has opposing signals, cannot change state, and piston A cannot outstroke. The first conflict, with its consequence in the right words.
  3. Later, a1 stays actuated (piston A is still outstroked) and keeps pilot 14 of valve B live when b1 sends air to pilot 12, so valve B cannot change state for B to instroke. The second conflict.
  4. Fit one-way trip (idle-return) rollers to both b0 and a1, so each gives only a short pulse and the old signal has gone before the new one arrives. A change that removes both held signals. A roller on a1 alone would still jam at the start.

7 · Electro-pneumatics PNEUMATICS

In a pure pneumatic circuit, air does the work and carries the signals. That is neat for small machines, but long air lines are slow to respond, and changing a sequence means re-plumbing. Electro-pneumatic (EP) control keeps compressed air for the heavy work and moves the control into electricity: switches and relays make the decisions, and the electricity only talks to the air through a solenoid valve.

The new parts

  • Solenoid valve. A 5/2 valve whose pilot is replaced by a solenoid, a coil that becomes an electromagnet when current flows and pushes the valve spool. A single-solenoid valve springs back when the coil is switched off. A double-solenoid valve has a coil at each end (labelled Y1 and Y2, for example) and has memory, like the double-pilot valve of the last chapter.
  • Relay. An electromagnetic switch. A small current through the relay coil (for example K1) closes or opens one or more contacts in other circuits. It lets a small switch control a heavier load, gives several contacts from one signal, and can latch itself on.
  • Limit switch and reed switch. These replace the roller limit valves. A limit switch is a mechanical switch pressed by the piston rod. A reed switch is a sealed pair of metal reeds in a glass tube, fixed to the cylinder barrel; a magnet in the piston pulls the reeds together as it passes, closing the contact with no mechanical contact.
  • Push-buttons. START (S1) is normally open; STOP (S2) is normally closed so that pressing it breaks the circuit.

The ladder diagram

The electrical control circuit is drawn as a ladder diagram: two vertical rails, positive on the left and 0 V on the right, with a horizontal rung for each output. Each rung has switch contacts in series, which behave as AND (all must be closed), and sometimes switches in parallel, which behave as OR, followed by a coil on the right. When the contacts are closed, current flows through the coil, and it operates.

The step-by-step logic is identical to the pneumatic chain rule. Replace "limit valve supplies pilot" with "limit switch energises coil". For A+ B+ A− B− with a double-solenoid valve on each cylinder:

  • Y1 (A+): START S1 in series with b0 (and a0 as an interlock).
  • Y3 (B+): a1, with b0 as an interlock.
  • Y2 (A−): b1, with a1 as an interlock.
  • Y4 (B−): a0, with b1 as an interlock.

An interlock is an extra contact in series that only lets a coil be energised if the cylinder is in a safe position for it. Here it uses the cylinder's own opposite limit switch, so that the two coils of one valve are never energised together, which would put opposing signals on the spool, so the valve could not change state reliably.

Latching: a relay that holds itself on

Often the machine should keep cycling after one press of START. A relay K1 does this with a latch: one of its own contacts is wired in parallel with START, so once K1 is energised, its contact keeps the rung live even after START is released. A normally closed STOP button in series with the rung breaks the latch. This "hold-on" circuit appears in almost every machine controller, and it is exactly what the simulator's "latch a continuous cycle" option uses.

⚡ The electro-pneumatic simulator

Press START (S1) and watch the ladder diagram: when a rung's contacts are all closed the coil lights, the solenoid moves the valve, the piston moves, and the reed switch at the end of its stroke closes the next rung. Switch on the latch to run continuously and press STOP (S2) to end it.

Choosing between pneumatic, electro-pneumatic and electronic

How the three kinds of control compare.
ControlAdvantagesDisadvantages
Pure pneumaticNo electricity at the machine, so no sparks and no electric shock; safe in explosive, wet or dirty places; simple, robust parts; clean exhaust (only air)Signals slow over long air lines; changing the sequence means re-plumbing; compressed air is expensive to make and leaks waste energy
Electro-pneumaticFast signals over long distances; easy to change, add interlocks, timers and latches; compact control panels; fewer long air linesNeeds both an electrical and an air supply; wiring, relays and switches can fail; sparks and electrical hazard in some environments
Electronic or programmable (PLC, microcontroller)Very flexible: reprogram instead of rewiring; many inputs and outputs; can read analogue sensors and log dataNeeds a programmer and a protected supply; electronics can be damaged by dirt, water, heat and electrical noise

Compared with an electric motor or solenoid actuator, a pneumatic cylinder gives quick, powerful linear motion from a light, cheap actuator, and its exhaust is just air with no oil or fumes at the machine. The honest drawbacks are that compressing air wastes much of the energy as heat, leaks are costly, and the force available is limited by the air pressure. A paint-spraying booth full of solvent vapour is a good example of where pneumatic control may be preferred: a spark from a switch or motor could start a fire, and an air cylinder cannot make one.

Worked example 18 · Description · an electro-pneumatic circuit

The circuit shown above uses double-solenoid 5/2 valves, with solenoids Y1 and Y2 on valve A and Y3 and Y4 on valve B, and reed switches a0, a1, b0 and b1. Describe the operation of the circuit for the sequence A+ B+ A− B−. [5]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. When START S1 is pressed, with b0 closed, solenoid Y1 is energised; the 5/2 valve A changes state and piston A outstrokes. The first rung's contacts, the coil, the valve changing state and the outstroke.
  2. At the end of the stroke reed switch a1 closes; Y3 is energised, valve B changes state and piston B outstrokes. Each switch at the end of a step energises the next coil.
  3. Reed switch b1 closes and Y2 is energised: valve A changes state and piston A instrokes. The other coil of the same valve returns A.
  4. Reed switch a0 closes, Y4 is energised, valve B changes state and piston B instrokes. Step four, and the sequence is complete.
  5. b0 closes again, and the circuit waits for START to be pressed again. Note the end state and the restart condition.

Worked example 19 · Discussion · why pneumatics may be preferred

A cutting machine in a flour mill is currently moved by electric solenoid actuators. Give two reasons why pneumatic cylinders may be preferred, and one disadvantage of changing. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Pneumatic cylinders do not make sparks, so there is no risk of igniting the flour dust in the air. A property linked to the situation, with the reason.
  2. Air cylinders are simple, rugged and can be washed down; the exhaust is only clean air, so there is no oil or fumes to contaminate the product. A second, different reason.
  3. Disadvantage: compressed air is energy-intensive to produce, and leaks waste it, so the running cost is higher. An honest drawback, which is what the third mark needs.
