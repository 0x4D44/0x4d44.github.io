The Redstone Room · analogue electronic control

Analogue electronics

This is the biggest block of marks on the question paper: 20 to 35 of the 110. It is also the area the 2024 course report said candidates found more demanding than digital electronics or structures. This deck takes it slowly, from the sensor on the front of a system to the op-amp at its heart.

Captain Reid has sent 1805 Squadron below decks on HMS Ratchet to the Redstone Room, the ship's wiring loft. Master Shipwright Morag Kerr keeps it. Every signal on the ship passes through here. A redstone dust line carries a strength from 0 to 15. Redstone strength is like an analogue level, but in 16 steps; a real analogue signal has no steps and can take any value in a range. It is an analogy only. Repeaters and torches, by contrast, give only "on" or "off". Those are digital, and they have their own deck.

What you must be able to do (from the course specification): use variable resistors and light and temperature sensors in voltage dividers; use sensor characteristics to design a divider to a specification; explain the function of a bipolar transistor and design a current-amplifier circuit, including calculating $h_{FE}$; explain the function of an n-channel enhancement-mode MOSFET and design it as a voltage-operated switch; compare the two in a given application; and calculate input and output voltages for the six op-amp circuits. Diodes for protection are part of the supporting notes, so they are here too.

1 · The electronic system and your N5 toolkit ANALOGUE · foundations

Input, process, output

Every electronic control system does three jobs. An input transducer turns a physical quantity (light, temperature, force, sound) into a voltage the circuit can use. The process stage decides what to do: it might share a voltage, compare it with a reference, or amplify it. An output transducer turns the result back into something physical: light, heat, sound or movement. A transducer is simply anything that converts energy from one form to another.

On HMS Ratchet the bilge alarm is a neat example. The input transducer is a pair of probes that notice water. The process stage decides whether the water is high enough to matter. The output transducer is a buzzer in the wardroom.

Kirchhoff's laws look grand but they are only two statements of common sense. The first is "charge is conserved". The second is "energy is conserved": each coulomb gains energy in the supply and gives it all up in the components on its way round.

⚓ The redstone analogy, and where it breaks

Redstone dust starts at strength 15 and loses one level per block of wire. That is a picture of voltage being "used up" along a chain of components. Two cautions. A real wire loses almost nothing, so in a circuit diagram the voltage only drops across components. And a redstone repeater restores the signal to full strength for free. In a real circuit only the supply adds energy; an amplifier is just a clever valve that draws power from the supply.

Watch the current flow: series and parallel

Worked example 1 · Calculation · series–parallel and Kirchhoff

In the circuit below, $R_1 = 4.0\ \Omega$ is in series with $R_2 = 6.0\ \Omega$ and $R_3 = 12\ \Omega$, which are in parallel. The supply is 12 V.

(a) Calculate the total resistance. (b) Calculate the current $I$ from the supply. (c) Calculate $I_2$ and $I_3$, and show that Kirchhoff's first law holds. (d) Calculate the power dissipated in $R_1$.

Show solution [6 marks]
  1. $R_{23} = \dfrac{R_2R_3}{R_2+R_3} = \dfrac{6.0 \times 12}{6.0 + 12} = 4.0\ \Omega$ Combine the parallel pair first.
  2. $R_t = R_1 + R_{23} = 4.0 + 4.0 = 8.0\ \Omega$ Then add it in series with $R_1$.
  3. $I = \dfrac{V}{R_t} = \dfrac{12}{8.0} = 1.5\ \text{A}$ Ohm's law for the whole circuit.
  4. Voltage across $R_1$: $V_1 = IR_1 = 1.5 \times 4.0 = 6.0$ V, so the voltage across the parallel pair is $12 - 6.0 = 6.0$ V (Kirchhoff 2). The same 6.0 V is across both parallel branches.
  5. $I_2 = \dfrac{6.0}{6.0} = 1.0$ A and $I_3 = \dfrac{6.0}{12} = 0.5$ A. Then $I_2 + I_3 = 1.5$ A $= I$ ✔ Kirchhoff 1 at the node.
  6. $P = I^2R = 1.5^2 \times 4.0 = 9.0$ W Or $P = VI = 6.0 \times 1.5$.

Typical mark allocation: •1–2 for the resistance, •3 for the current, •4 for the voltage over the parallel pair, •5 for the branch currents and check, •6 for the power with its unit. Slip to avoid: using 12 V across $R_2$. The full supply is not across it, only the share left after $R_1$.

Worked example 2 · Calculation · an LED and its resistor

An LED needs 20 mA and drops 2.0 V when it conducts. It is to be powered from a 9.0 V supply through a series resistor. Calculate the resistance needed.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. Voltage across the resistor: $9.0 - 2.0 = 7.0$ V Series: the voltages add to the supply, so the resistor takes whatever the LED does not.
  2. $R = \dfrac{V}{I} = \dfrac{7.0}{0.020}$ The same 20 mA flows in the resistor. Convert mA to A.
  3. $R = 350\ \Omega$ Real resistors come in preferred values. The next one up, 390 Ω, would be chosen so the LED current stays below its limit.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Ma'am, if the current is the same everywhere in a series circuit, why does the voltage get shared out? Where does the energy go?"

Voltage is energy per coulomb. The same coulombs pass every component, so the same current flows everywhere. But each component takes a different amount of energy from each coulomb. A big resistor takes a big share; a small one takes a small share. All the energy the supply gave each coulomb is spent by the time it gets back. This idea, that the biggest resistor takes the biggest share of the voltage, is the whole of the next chapter.

  • State and apply both Kirchhoff laws as printed in the Data Booklet.
  • Use $P = VI$, $V^2/R$ and $I^2R$ and pick the one that fits the data you have.

2 · Voltage dividers and sensors ANALOGUE · input transducers

A light sensor changes its resistance. A transistor or an op-amp does not care about resistance; it responds to voltage. The voltage divider is the bridge between the two. It turns "the resistance of the sensor changed" into "the voltage at this point changed". That is why it appears in nearly every analogue question.

The idea: the bigger resistor takes the bigger share

Put two resistors in series across a supply. The same current $I$ flows through both. Ohm's law gives the voltage across each as $V_1 = IR_1$ and $V_2 = IR_2$. Divide one by the other and the current cancels:

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

This is the form printed in the Data Booklet. In words: the voltages are in the same ratio as the resistances. $V_1$ means "the voltage across $R_1$", and $V_2$ is the voltage across $R_2$. Because the resistors are in series, $V_1 + V_2 = V_s$, the supply.

Finding the output voltage

The Booklet gives you the ratio, not the output formula. You can build the output from it. Since $V_1 + V_2 = V_s$, the whole supply is split into $R_1 + R_2$ "shares" of resistance, and $V_2$ gets $R_2$ of them:

$$V_{out} = V_2 = V_s \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

Check it makes sense. If $R_2$ is huge, the fraction tends to 1 and $V_{out}$ tends to $V_s$. If $R_2$ is tiny, the fraction tends to 0 and $V_{out}$ tends to 0 V. If the two are equal, $V_{out}$ is exactly half the supply.

Worked example 3 · Calculation · output of a divider

A divider has $R_1 = 4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$ (top) and $R_2 = 3.3\ \text{k}\Omega$ (bottom) across a 9.0 V supply. Calculate $V_{out}$, the voltage across $R_2$.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $V_{out} = V_s \dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ Take the output across $R_2$, the lower resistor.
  2. $V_{out} = 9.0 \times \dfrac{3.3}{4.7 + 3.3} = 9.0 \times 0.4125$ The units of resistance cancel in a ratio, so kΩ can stay as kΩ.
  3. $V_{out} = 3.7125 \ldots = 3.71$ V (3 s.f.) Sense check: $R_2$ is a bit under half the total, so the output is a bit under half of 9 V.

Rearranging for $R_1$: the step the 2024 report flagged

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2024 course report

Many candidates "found it challenging to manipulate the voltage divider formula $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$ to find the value of $R_1$." Designing a divider means finding a resistor value, so this is exactly the skill you need. Learn the method below until it is automatic.

You are usually told the supply, the voltage you want somewhere, and one of the two resistors. You must find the other. Use this method every time.

  1. Draw it and label it. Mark $V_s$, the two resistors, and which voltage is across which. Write "V across R1" next to R1. Most slips happen because the voltage is attached to the wrong resistor.
  2. Find both voltages. You are given one (say $V_2$). The other is the rest of the supply: $V_1 = V_s - V_2$. This is the step candidates skip.
  3. Write the Booklet formula with numbers in the right places: $\dfrac{V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{R_1}{R_2}$.
  4. Isolate the unknown. Multiply both sides by the resistor you know. To find $R_1$: $R_1 = R_2 \times \dfrac{V_1}{V_2}$. To find $R_2$: $R_2 = R_1 \times \dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$.
  5. Substitute, calculate, add the unit, then check by putting your answer back in: do the voltages come out as specified?

A handy memory: the unknown resistor equals the known resistor times (its voltage ÷ the known resistor's voltage). The resistor with the larger voltage is the larger resistor.

Worked example 4 · Calculation · find $R_1$

A divider is built across a 12 V supply. $R_2 = 2.2\ \text{k}\Omega$ and the output voltage across $R_2$ is to be 3.0 V. Calculate the resistance of $R_1$.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $V_1 = V_s - V_2 = 12 - 3.0 = 9.0$ V The voltage across $R_1$ is what $R_2$ does not take. (Step 2 of the method: do not skip it.)
  2. $\dfrac{V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{R_1}{R_2} \ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{9.0}{3.0} = \dfrac{R_1}{2200}$ Formula from the Booklet, with $R_2$ in ohms.
  3. $R_1 = 2200 \times \dfrac{9.0}{3.0} = 6600\ \Omega = 6.6\ \text{k}\Omega$ Check: $R_1$ is three times $R_2$, and it has three times the voltage. ✔

Common slip: using the output (3.0 V) for both voltages, or putting 12 V in as $V_1$. The ratio is always between the two resistor voltages, which add up to 12 V.

Variable resistors and potentiometers

A variable resistor has a resistance you can set by turning a knob or sliding a contact. Used on its own, in series with a component, it lets you adjust the current. A potentiometer is a resistor with a third terminal, the wiper, that slides along it. Connect the two ends across the supply and the wiper splits the resistor into a top part and a bottom part: a divider whose ratio you can change by hand. If the wiper is 30% of the way up, the output is 30% of the supply. That is how a volume dial, a throttle or a "set the temperature" knob produces a reference voltage.

Sensors: resistors that listen

The two sensors in the course are resistors whose resistance depends on the world around them.

  • A light-dependent resistor (LDR): resistance falls as the light gets brighter. In our data it is about 100 kΩ at 10 lux (dim) and under 1 kΩ in bright light.
  • A thermistor: resistance changes with temperature. The type in this course is the negative temperature coefficient kind (NTC): resistance falls as temperature rises. The symbol carries "$-\theta$" as a reminder. (Other types exist; you will be told what you have.)

Their properties come from the question as a characteristic: a graph or table of resistance against light level (in lux, the unit of light level: roughly 10 lux is a dim room and 10 000 lux is bright daylight) or temperature (in °C). In an exam you read a value off it, so practise reading graphs carefully. Neither is a straight line, and the readings below are the ones used throughout this deck.

A third sensor you will meet in the materials deck is the strain gauge, whose resistance changes by a tiny amount when it is stretched. It too is read by putting it in a divider (or a bridge of dividers).

Why the sensor's position matters

Put the sensor on top of the divider and the fixed resistor underneath, and the output is across the fixed resistor. Now think what happens as the temperature rises. The thermistor's resistance falls, so it takes a smaller share of the supply. The fixed resistor takes a bigger share, so $V_{out}$ rises. Swap them over, with the output across the thermistor, and $V_{out}$ falls as it gets hotter. Same parts, opposite behaviour.

For an LDR the story is the same with "brighter" in place of "hotter". An LDR across the output gives a voltage that is high in the dark. That is just what you want to switch on a lamp at dusk.

The sensor divider and its characteristic

Pick a sensor and where it sits. Slide the light or temperature and see the operating point move along the characteristic while $V_{out}$ changes. Then press Design challenge: you are given a specification and must choose the fixed resistor. A step-by-step solution is there if you get stuck.

Designing a divider to a specification

This is the exam skill in one sentence: at the stated light level or temperature, the sensor has a known resistance (read it off the characteristic); decide what voltage you want across which resistor; then use $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$ to find the unknown fixed resistor. The sensor is simply "the resistor you know".

Worked example 5 · Design · a thermistor divider

A thermistor and a fixed resistor $R$ are connected in series across a 9.0 V supply, with the thermistor on top and $R$ at the bottom. The output voltage, taken across $R$, must be 4.0 V when the temperature is 40 °C. Use the characteristic (5.3 kΩ at 40 °C) to calculate the value of $R$.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Thermistor resistance at 40 °C: $R_{th} = 5.3\ \text{k}\Omega$ Read from the characteristic.
  2. $V_{th} = 9.0 - 4.0 = 5.0$ V The thermistor's voltage is the rest of the supply.
  3. $\dfrac{V_{th}}{V_R} = \dfrac{R_{th}}{R} \Rightarrow R = R_{th} \times \dfrac{V_R}{V_{th}} = 5.3 \times \dfrac{4.0}{5.0}$ The unknown is $R$, so it equals the known resistor times (its voltage over the known one's voltage).
  4. $R = 4.24\ \text{k}\Omega\ (= 4240\ \Omega)$ The nearest standard values are 3.9 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ; a question that asks for a calculated value wants 4.24 kΩ.

Check: $9.0 \times \dfrac{4.24}{5.3 + 4.24} = 4.0$ V ✔. Because $R$ is a little smaller than the thermistor, it gets a little less than half the supply, which agrees with 4.0 V.

Worked example 6 · Design · an LDR and a transistor threshold

A transistor is to switch on a night light. An LDR is the lower resistor of a divider with fixed resistor $R$ above it, across a 9.0 V supply. The transistor base is connected to the junction and starts to conduct when the voltage across the LDR reaches 0.7 V. This should happen when the light level falls to 100 lux (LDR resistance 20 kΩ). Assume the base takes negligible current. Calculate $R$.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. LDR: $V_{LDR} = 0.7$ V and $R_{LDR} = 20\ \text{k}\Omega$. Resistance read at 100 lux.
  2. $V_R = 9.0 - 0.7 = 8.3$ V The fixed resistor takes the rest.
  3. $R = R_{LDR} \times \dfrac{V_R}{V_{LDR}} = 20 \times \dfrac{8.3}{0.7}$
  4. $R = 237\ \text{k}\Omega$ (3 s.f.) A big resistor: the LDR must take only 0.7 V of the 9 V even at 20 kΩ, so the rest of the circuit has to be much bigger than the LDR.

In the dark the LDR's resistance rises well above 20 kΩ, so the voltage across it tries to rise above 0.7 V and the transistor switches on harder. In the light it drops below 0.7 V and the transistor is off. The 0.7 V figure is the base–emitter voltage, which is covered properly in chapter 4.

⚓ Why "negligible current" matters

The divider formula assumes the same current flows in both resistors. If the next stage draws current from the junction, the output sags. Engineers keep the divider's own current much bigger than the current it feeds. Higher questions tell you to ignore it, or give you numbers where it does not matter. In the Redstone Room: if a comparator reads a redstone line, it should take almost no signal strength from it. Reading a dial must not drain the dial.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why do I need the fixed resistor at all? Couldn't the circuit just use the thermistor on its own?"

On its own, a thermistor gives you a resistance, not a voltage, and transistors and op-amps respond to voltages. With only the thermistor connected across the supply, the voltage across it is always the whole supply, whatever its resistance. Add the fixed resistor and the supply is shared. Now when the thermistor's resistance changes, so does its share. Two resistors turn "resistance changes" into "voltage changes".

3 · Diodes, relays and protection ANALOGUE · protection

Before the transistors arrive, you need two small devices that every output stage leans on. One is the diode, which guards a transistor against a hidden danger. The other is the relay, which lets a tiny signal switch a big load.

The diode: a one-way valve

A diode lets current flow through it in one direction only. The triangle in the symbol points the way conventional current can go, from the anode to the cathode (the bar). Connected that way it is forward biased and conducts, dropping roughly 0.7 V if it is a silicon diode. Connected the other way it is reverse biased and blocks current, like a closed non-return valve. On a ship's bilge pump, that valve is the thing stopping the sea running back in.

The relay: a small current switching a big one

A relay is an electrically operated switch. Current in a coil makes an electromagnet, which pulls an iron armature and closes (or opens) a set of contacts. The contacts are a completely separate circuit. That brings three advantages:

  • the contacts can switch a much larger current than a transistor can (a motor, a heater);
  • they can switch a different voltage, even mains, while the control side stays at a safe low voltage;
  • the two circuits are electrically isolated from each other.

The coil is a fixed resistance. A 9 V relay whose coil is 180 Ω draws $I = V/R = 9/180 = 0.05$ A = 50 mA when it is on. That is more than most sensor circuits can supply directly, which is why a transistor sits between them.

The hidden danger: flyback

The relay coil, a motor winding and a solenoid (a coil that pulls a metal plunger in when current flows) are all inductive loads: coils of wire. A coil carrying current stores energy in a magnetic field. If you suddenly switch the current off, the field collapses. The collapsing field drives the current to keep flowing, and it does so by producing a large voltage of the opposite polarity across the coil. For a moment the end of the coil that was connected to the transistor goes far above the supply voltage. That voltage spike can be many times the supply, easily enough to destroy a transistor in a single switch-off.

The cure is a flyback diode (also called a protection or freewheel diode) connected across the coil, in reverse: its cathode (bar) goes to the positive side. While the load is on, the diode is reverse biased and does nothing. At switch-off the coil voltage reverses, the diode becomes forward biased, and it gives the coil's current a safe loop to circulate round and die away. The spike is limited to about 0.7 V beyond the supply.

⚠ Exam lookout · protection diodes

When a circuit drives a relay, motor or solenoid and the question asks what the diode is for, say all three parts: the load is a coil; switching off makes the magnetic field collapse and produces a large reverse voltage spike; the diode conducts that current so the transistor is protected from damage. When you draw one, get the polarity right: cathode (bar) to the positive rail, so it is reverse biased in normal running. A bare "it stops current flowing backwards" does not explain why the circuit needs it, so it is not enough.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If the diode is across the coil, won't it just short out the coil and the relay will never switch on?"

Only if it were connected the other way round. With the bar towards the positive rail, the diode is reverse biased while the coil is energised, so the diode acts as though it is not there. It only conducts during the brief moment at switch-off. That is why the polarity matters so much.

4 · The bipolar junction transistor ANALOGUE · BJT

A sensor circuit makes a signal that is tiny: a few microamps, perhaps. A lamp, a relay or a motor wants tens or hundreds of milliamps. Something has to bridge the gap. That something is the transistor, the most important component in the whole course. Picture a lock gate in a ship's dock: a small effort controls a huge flow of water (the picture is explained below).

Terminals and symbol

The bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three terminals: the base (B), the collector (C) and the emitter (E). This course uses the npn type, whose emitter arrow points out. The base is the control. The load connects to the collector, and the emitter goes to 0 V.

Three currents, then, and one law. The two currents going in must equal the one coming out, so $I_E = I_C + I_B$ (Kirchhoff 1 again). Because $I_B$ is so small, $I_E$ is almost the same as $I_C$.

A current amplifier: $h_{FE}$

A small base current controls a much larger collector current. The ratio is the current gain, written $h_{FE}$ (no unit, since it is a ratio of two currents):

$$h_{FE} = \frac{I_C}{I_B}\qquad\text{so}\qquad I_C = h_{FE} \times I_B$$

If $h_{FE} = 100$, a base current of 0.5 mA lets a collector current of 50 mA flow. Gains of 100 or more are common, and a transistor's gain differs from one device to the next, so a question always gives the value.

Three regions of behaviour

  1. Cut-off (off). If the base–emitter voltage $V_{BE}$ is below about 0.7 V, no base current flows, and so no collector current flows. The transistor is an open switch.
  2. Active region (amplifier). Once $V_{BE}$ reaches about 0.7 V, base current flows, and $I_C = h_{FE} \times I_B$. The collector current follows the base current, so it is proportional to it.
  3. Saturation (fully on). The load and supply set a limit to how much collector current can ever flow: at most $V_s/R_L$ (a little less, because the transistor itself drops a little). If you push more base current than $I_C/h_{FE}$ requires, the collector current cannot rise any further. The transistor is "saturated", and the extra base current is wasted. Now the voltage across the transistor, $V_{CE}$, is only a few tenths of a volt or less, and it behaves like a closed switch.
📘 The $V_{BE}$ value

The Data Booklet does not give $V_{BE}$. The question will state it; 0.7 V is the usual value for a silicon transistor. And when a question says a saturated transistor has a stated $V_{CE}$, use that value. Never invent one the question did not give you. If a question tells you to treat a saturated transistor as an ideal switch, use $V_{CE} = 0$.

One picture: the lock gate

Use a single picture for every transistor. The main path is a big water pipe with a lock gate in it. A thin control pipe pushes the gate open. A trickle in the thin pipe lets a torrent through the big one, $h_{FE}$ times as much. But the torrent can never be more than the main pipe can supply. Once the gate is wide open, a bigger trickle makes no difference. That is saturation. The table maps the picture onto the three terminals of both kinds of transistor.

Designing a BJT driver circuit

To switch a load on and off, you want the transistor saturated when "on". The recipe:

  1. Find the load current $I_C$. For a lamp or motor, from its ratings. For a relay, from coil voltage ÷ coil resistance.
  2. Find the base current needed: $I_B = I_C / h_{FE}$.
  3. Find the voltage across the base resistor. The base resistor is in series with the base–emitter junction, which always has about 0.7 V across it. So $V_{RB} = V_{in} - V_{BE}$. This is the step the 2024 report says some candidates missed (Q8(c)).
  4. Calculate the base resistor: $R_B = \dfrac{V_{in} - V_{BE}}{I_B}$.
  5. Choose a standard value smaller than the answer, so the base current is a little more than the minimum and the transistor is firmly saturated.
  6. If the load is a coil (relay, motor, solenoid), add the flyback diode.

Worked example 7 · Calculation · $h_{FE}$

(a) A transistor passes a collector current of 30 mA when the base current is 0.25 mA. Calculate its current gain. (b) A different transistor has $h_{FE} = 200$. What base current gives a collector current of 30 mA?

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. (a) $h_{FE} = \dfrac{I_C}{I_B} = \dfrac{30 \times 10^{-3}}{0.25 \times 10^{-3}}$ Both in the same unit: the mA cancel, so it is simply 30 ÷ 0.25.
  2. $h_{FE} = 120$ No unit.
  3. (b) $I_B = \dfrac{I_C}{h_{FE}} = \dfrac{30}{200}$ Rearrange the Booklet formula.
  4. $I_B = 0.15\ \text{mA}$ 150 µA. A tiny current controlling a large one.

Worked example 8 · Design · a base resistor

A lamp draws 120 mA when lit. It is switched by an npn transistor with $h_{FE} = 150$ and $V_{BE} = 0.7$ V. The base resistor is fed from a 5.0 V control signal. Calculate the largest base resistor that will allow the lamp to light fully.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. $I_B = \dfrac{I_C}{h_{FE}} = \dfrac{0.120}{150} = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}$ A $= 0.80$ mA The least base current that allows 120 mA.
  2. $V_{RB} = V_{in} - V_{BE} = 5.0 - 0.7 = 4.3$ V The 0.7 V is across the base–emitter junction, not the resistor.
  3. $R_B = \dfrac{V_{RB}}{I_B} = \dfrac{4.3}{8.0 \times 10^{-4}}$
  4. $R_B = 5375\ \Omega \approx 5380\ \Omega\ (5.38\ \text{k}\Omega)$ Any larger value gives too little base current. So a standard 4.7 kΩ would be a sensible choice, as it is smaller.

Common slip: using 5.0 ÷ 0.0008 = 6250 Ω. That forgets the 0.7 V, and since it is bigger than the right answer, the lamp would never reach full brightness.

Worked example 9 · Calculation · active region and the power in the transistor

A lamp of resistance 100 Ω (when lit) is in the collector circuit of an npn transistor with $h_{FE} = 200$ and $V_{BE} = 0.7$ V. The supply is 9.0 V. The base resistor of 47 kΩ is connected directly to the 9.0 V supply. (a) Calculate the base current. (b) Calculate the collector current. (c) Calculate $V_{CE}$. (d) Calculate the power dissipated between the collector and emitter. (e) The base resistor is changed to 4.7 kΩ. Explain what happens to $I_C$ and the power.

Show solution [7 marks]
  1. (a) $V_{RB} = 9.0 - 0.7 = 8.3$ V, so $I_B = \dfrac{8.3}{47000} = 1.766 \times 10^{-4}$ A $\approx 177\ \mu$A Voltage across the base resistor first. Keep the full calculator value for the next step.
  2. (b) $I_C = h_{FE} I_B = 200 \times 1.766 \times 10^{-4} = 0.0353$ A $= 35.3$ mA First check this is possible: the lamp can take at most 9.0/100 = 90 mA, so 35.3 mA is allowed and the transistor is in the active region.
  3. (c) Voltage across lamp: $I_C R_L = 0.03532 \times 100 = 3.53$ V, so $V_{CE} = 9.0 - 3.53 = 5.47$ V Kirchhoff 2: the supply is shared between lamp and transistor.
  4. (d) $P = V_{CE} \times I_C = 5.468 \times 0.03532 = 0.193$ W The power in that component is the voltage across it times the current through it. Not the supply voltage.
  5. (e) With 4.7 kΩ, $I_B = 8.3/4700 = 1.77$ mA, so $h_{FE} I_B = 353$ mA. The lamp cannot take this (the limit is about 90 mA), so the transistor saturates: $I_C \approx 90$ mA, the lamp is fully lit, $V_{CE}$ falls to nearly 0 V and so the power in the transistor falls to almost nothing.

Common slips: using 9.0 V instead of 8.3 V in (a); using the supply voltage in (d); and rounding 177 µA before multiplying (the report warns that rounding intermediate values can make the final answer wrong). Notice the surprise in (e): the transistor runs hottest somewhere in the middle of the active region, because power is $V_{CE} \times I_C$: at one extreme $I_C$ is zero, and at the other $V_{CE}$ is nearly zero, so the product is small at both ends. Switching circuits are designed to be either firmly off or firmly saturated so that little heat is made.

Worked example 10 · Design · a relay driver

Show solution [5 marks]

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If I double the base current, does the collector current always double?"

Only while the transistor is in the active region. Once the load has used up the supply, the current cannot rise further. You can check by working out the potential collector current $h_{FE} I_B$ and the most the load can pass $V_s / R_L$. Whichever is smaller is the real $I_C$. If the load's limit is smaller, the transistor is saturated, and the base current was more than enough.

5 · The MOSFET ANALOGUE · MOSFET

The BJT is current-operated: it needs a steady trickle of base current to stay on. The MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) is voltage-operated. Its control terminal needs a voltage but, once that is in place, practically no current at all. In the lock-gate picture from the BJT section (table there), the gate terminal G takes the place of B, but needs only a voltage, with no steady trickle.

Terminals and the n-channel enhancement type

The three terminals are the gate (G), the drain (D) and the source (S). The load goes between the supply and the drain, the source goes to 0 V, and the control voltage is applied between the gate and the source: the gate–source voltage, $V_{GS}$. The current through the load is the drain current, $I_D$.

  • n-channel: the conducting channel is made of n-type material, and it conducts when the gate is made positive relative to the source.
  • enhancement mode: with no gate voltage there is no channel, so the MOSFET is off. Applying a positive $V_{GS}$ enhances (builds) a channel between drain and source.
  • The gate is separated from the channel by a thin insulating layer of oxide, like one plate of a capacitor. That is why it draws practically no current: nothing can flow through an insulator. The gate has a very high input resistance.

Threshold voltage: what it really means

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2025 course report

In 2025 only "some candidates described the meaning of threshold voltage for a MOSFET". Learn a one-sentence definition and be ready to use it.

📘 Threshold voltage, $V_{th}$

The threshold voltage is the minimum gate–source voltage at which the MOSFET just begins to conduct. Below it the channel does not exist and the drain current is (effectively) zero. Above it, the channel forms and the drain current grows as $V_{GS}$ rises.

The common mistake is to think the MOSFET is "fully on" at the threshold. It is not: it is only starting to conduct. For a decent current you need $V_{GS}$ well above $V_{th}$, and the graph below shows how far. Compare this with the BJT, which has a roughly fixed turn-on voltage ($V_{BE} \approx 0.7$ V) that you do not choose: with a MOSFET, $V_{GS}$ is how you set the current.

The transfer characteristic and transconductance

A transfer characteristic is a graph of $I_D$ against $V_{GS}$. It is the MOSFET's version of $I_C = h_{FE} I_B$, but it is a curve, not a straight line, so you read it off the graph rather than multiplying by a number. This deck uses the graph below for every example.

How strongly the gate voltage controls the current is the transconductance:

$$g_m = \frac{\Delta I_D}{\Delta V_{GS}}$$

It is the gradient of the transfer characteristic, so it is measured in amps per volt, which is the siemens (S). A steep graph means a small change in $V_{GS}$ gives a large change in $I_D$. This is the MOSFET's amplifying property, parallel to $h_{FE}$ for the BJT. The curve gets steeper as $V_{GS}$ rises, so $g_m$ is bigger at higher $V_{GS}$.

Set $V_{GS}$ and read the current

Drag the dot along the curve (or use the slider). The load is a solenoid in the drain circuit. The panel does the 2024-report method for you: current, drop over the solenoid, $V_{DS}$ and power, and shows $g_m$ as the slope. If the curve asks for more current than the load can pass, the MOSFET is fully on and the load sets the current.

Designing a MOSFET switching circuit

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2024 course report

In the MOSFET question "many [candidates] did not calculate the drop over the solenoid", so they could not find $I_{DS}$ and then read $V_{GS}$ from the graph. The report adds that many "did not tackle this question in a methodical way". The method below is that method. Say it out loud until you can do it without the notes.

  1. Voltage across the load. Kirchhoff 2: $V_{load} = V_s - V_{DS}$. (If the question gives the voltage the load needs, use that.) This is the step that is skipped.
  2. Current. The load and the MOSFET are in series, so the same current flows: $I_D = V_{load} / R_{load}$.
  3. Read the graph. Find that $I_D$ on the vertical axis, go across to the curve, then down to $V_{GS}$. That is the gate voltage you must supply.
  4. Protect it. A solenoid or relay is a coil, so add a flyback diode, cathode to the positive rail.

Worked example 11 · Design · a solenoid on a MOSFET

A 6.0 Ω solenoid is switched by an n-channel enhancement MOSFET from a 12 V supply. When the solenoid is operating, the voltage across the MOSFET ($V_{DS}$) is 2.4 V. Use the transfer characteristic to find the gate–source voltage needed, and calculate the power dissipated in the MOSFET.

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. $V_{solenoid} = V_s - V_{DS} = 12 - 2.4 = 9.6$ V The drop over the solenoid: the missing step in 2024.
  2. $I_D = \dfrac{V}{R} = \dfrac{9.6}{6.0} = 1.6$ A Series circuit, so the solenoid's current is the drain current.
  3. From the graph, $I_D = 1.6$ A corresponds to $V_{GS} = 4.0$ V Read across at 1.6 A, then down.
  4. $P = V_{DS} \times I_D = 2.4 \times 1.6 = 3.84$ W $\approx 3.8$ W Again, voltage across the device times current through it.

Common slips: dividing 12 V by 6.0 Ω to get 2.0 A (that ignores $V_{DS}$) and reading the graph at the wrong axis. Check: the answer should be above the 2.0 V threshold. A gate voltage below it would mean no current at all.

Worked example 12 · Calculation · transconductance

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If no current goes into the gate, how can it control anything? Where does the energy to open it come from?"

The gate controls the channel with an electric field, not a flow of current, like the electric field of a charged comb that can pull a scrap of paper without touching it. A tiny burst of current is needed to charge the gate when the voltage changes, but once it is charged, no further current flows. The energy that drives the load comes entirely from the supply, as it does for the BJT. The gate only decides how much to let through.

6 · Operational amplifiers ANALOGUE · op-amps

The operational amplifier (op-amp) is an integrated circuit (a whole circuit of many tiny components built on one small chip) that amplifies the difference between two voltages. With a few resistors it can be made to add, subtract, amplify, invert or compare. Almost every analogue control question that is not about a transistor is about an op-amp. The Data Booklet prints six circuits and their formulae. This chapter explains where they come from, so that you can trust them and use them quickly.

The symbol and the three rules

An op-amp has an inverting input (marked −), a non-inverting input (marked +), one output $V_o$, and a supply of $+V_{CC}$ and $-V_{CC}$. We treat it as ideal, with three rules:

  1. No current flows into either input. The input resistance is so high that the inputs only sense voltage and take no current from whatever is connected to them.
  2. The gain without feedback is enormous. A difference of a few millivolts between the inputs is enough to swing the output all the way to a limit.
  3. The output can never go beyond the supply rails. It saturates at about $+V_{CC}$ or $-V_{CC}$. Real op-amps stop slightly inside the rails, but the course treats them as reaching $\pm V_{CC}$ unless you are told otherwise. (This "saturation" is not the same as a transistor being saturated: here it just means the output has hit its supply limit.) State this assumption if it matters.

The Redstone Room has a good model of rule 2. A redstone comparator block looks at a signal strength entering the back and one entering the side, and compares them. In "compare" mode it passes the back signal only if it is at least as strong as the side signal; otherwise it gives nothing. In "subtract" mode it outputs the back strength minus the side strength. The first is a two-state decision, like the op-amp comparator. The second is a proportional subtraction, like the difference amplifier.

The redstone comparator block

Set the strength entering the back and the side (0 to 15; redstone comes in 16 steps, so it is only an analogy for a smooth analogue signal). Switch between the block's two modes and compare. The block is the Minecraft cousin of two of the circuits below: a comparator and a difference amplifier. (The real block's rules have a few extra details; this is the idea.)

Feedback decides what an op-amp does

With no feedback, any difference between the inputs, however small, pushes the output to a rail. That is a comparator: its output is a two-state yes/no. With negative feedback (a resistor from the output back to the inverting input), the op-amp quietly adjusts its output to keep the two inputs at (almost) the same voltage. That makes a linear, proportional amplifier whose gain is set by two resistors. Both ideas are used in control systems, and the systems deck covers closed-loop feedback in detail.

Here is the key reasoning for the inverting amplifier. The non-inverting input is at 0 V. With negative feedback, the op-amp makes the inverting input equal to it: 0 V, a "virtual earth". So the current through $R_i$ is $V_i / R_i$. No current can enter the op-amp (rule 1), so all of it goes through $R_f$. The voltage across $R_f$ is $(V_i/R_i) R_f$, and since one end of it is at 0 V, the output is $V_o = -(R_f/R_i) V_i$. The minus sign is there because the current flows towards the output, lowering it below 0 V. You will not be asked to derive this, but understanding it tells you which formula goes with which circuit.

All six circuits

📘 The comparator, exactly as the Data Booklet has it

"If $V_i \lt V_{ref}$, then $V_o$ saturates positively. If $V_i \gt V_{ref}$, then $V_o$ saturates negatively." Note which input is which: $V_i$ is on the inverting (−) input and $V_{ref}$ on the non-inverting (+). So the output is positive when the signal is below the reference. This catches many people out: it is the reverse of "high input, high output". Check it by reasoning: the + input is higher than the − input, so the output goes positive.

The Booklet draws the op-amp with supplies of $+V_{CC}$ and $-V_{CC}$. Some circuits you will meet are powered from $+V_{CC}$ and 0 V instead. Then the output can only swing between 0 V and $+V_{CC}$ (it cannot go negative), so "saturates negatively" becomes "falls to 0 V". Read each question's circuit.

Saturation: the output is limited

Every formula gives the value the output would reach. The real output is limited to $\pm V_{CC}$. So after you calculate $V_o$, always compare it with the supply. If $V_o$ from the formula is beyond a rail, the answer is the rail. For example an inverting amplifier of gain $-4.7$ on $\pm 9$ V rails and an input of 2.5 V does not give $-11.75$ V. It gives $-9$ V. The input can only be as large as $V_{CC} / |A_v|$ before clipping: here $9 / 4.7 = 1.91$ V.

Choose a circuit, then set the resistors, the supply and the input signals. The scope shows the inputs and the output as they run. When the output reaches a rail it flattens: that is clipping. The formula and the numbers under the scope update with every change. For the difference and summing circuits, the second input is a steady DC level. For the comparator, $V_{ref}$ is a steady DC level and $V_i$ is the signal. Press Design challenge for a question to solve.

Choosing resistors for a gain

The formulae run in reverse as design tools. To get a gain of $-8$ from an inverting amplifier with $R_i = 4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$, you need $R_f / R_i = 8$ so $R_f = 8 \times 4.7 = 37.6\ \text{k}\Omega$. For a non-inverting amplifier with gain 6 and $R_i = 2.2\ \text{k}\Omega$, $1 + R_f/R_i = 6$ so $R_f = 5 R_i = 11\ \text{k}\Omega$. Note the "$1 +$" in the second case: it is $A_v - 1$ times $R_i$.

Link the answer back to the situation described, as the 2024 report asks for.

Proportional or two-state? Relating the circuit to the situation

The comparator gives two-state control: the output is fully on or fully off, depending on which side of the reference the input is. A thermostat works this way: the heater is either on or off. The difference amplifier, used as the error detector in a closed loop, gives proportional control: its output is proportional to the error, so a small error gives a small correction and a big error gives a big one. A heater controlled this way would run harder the colder the room gets.

The 2024 report says that on a two-state control question many candidates were "unable to relate to the situation described". The fix is to use the words of the question: "when the water level reaches the probes, the comparator output goes positive and the pump runs". Do not just repeat "it compares the voltages".

⚠ Exam lookout · positive outputs and the 2026 report

The 2026 report says "few candidates described two adaptations that could be applied to the circuit, which would produce a positive output". If an inverting amplifier gives a negative output and you need a positive one, you have several options, and you should be able to name two: (1) feed it a negative input voltage (inverting × negative = positive); (2) follow it with a second inverting stage (equal resistors, gain −1); (3) change to a non-inverting amplifier, where a positive input gives a positive output; (4) in a comparator, swap which side the signal and reference go, or put the sensor in the other position of the divider so the voltage moves the other way. Check each one against the formula before you write it.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"The inverting amplifier has a gain of −4.7 and I put in 2.5 V. My calculator says −11.75 V. Why does the scope say −9 V?"

Because an op-amp makes no energy of its own. It can only move its output between the voltages of its supply. The formula tells you what the output would be if it had unlimited supply. The supply decides what it can be. Always compare your answer with the rails.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • State the ideal op-amp rules and name which input is which.
  • Identify the configuration from a circuit and write down its formula from the Data Booklet.
  • Calculate $V_o$, then check it against the supply rails.
  • Choose $R_f$ and $R_i$ for a gain (remember the "1 +" in the non-inverting case).
  • Apply the comparator rule exactly as printed ($V_i \lt V_{ref}$ gives a positive output).
  • Distinguish two-state (comparator) from proportional (difference amplifier) control, in the words of the situation.

7 · Putting it together: whole-system questions ANALOGUE · integration

The big questions on the paper rarely test one device. They hand you a system and ask for several things in a row: a voltage at the sensor, whether the comparator is positive or negative, a base resistor, the power in a transistor, and then a description of how the whole thing works. Each answer feeds the next. This chapter shows how to chain the devices and how to write the description.

The signal chain

Almost every system is the same chain with different parts plugged in:

  1. Sensor and divider turn the physical quantity into a voltage ($V_i$).
  2. Comparator compares $V_i$ with a reference $V_{ref}$ and gives a two-state output, $+V_{CC}$ or $-V_{CC}$ (or 0 V).
  3. Base resistor turns that output into a base current (taking off the 0.7 V).
  4. Transistor amplifies the base current, switching a collector current. (Or a MOSFET, switched by its gate voltage.)
  5. Relay or load does the work: fan, pump, buzzer, lamp. A coil gets a flyback diode.

Trace the signal through the ship's systems

Pick a system and slide the physical quantity. Every stage lights up with its own value, so you can see exactly where the decision is made and what each stage hands to the next one.

Worked example 19 · Extended · the temperature-controlled fan

The circuit above switches on a fan when the engine-room temperature reaches 50 °C. The supply is 9.0 V. The thermistor is the lower resistor of the sensor divider, with fixed resistor $R$ above it. $R_3$ and $R_4$ are both 10 kΩ. The thermistor has 3.6 kΩ at 50 °C and 2.5 kΩ at 60 °C. The relay coil has a resistance of 180 Ω and the transistor has $h_{FE} = 100$ and $V_{BE} = 0.7$ V. The comparator output is 9.0 V when saturated positive.

(a) Show that $V_{ref}$ is 4.5 V. (b) Calculate $R$ so that the comparator switches at 50 °C. (c) Calculate $V_i$ at 60 °C and state the comparator output. (d) Calculate the largest base resistor that will operate the relay. (e) Describe how the system operates as the temperature rises through 50 °C. (f) State the purpose of the diode.

Show solution [15 marks]
  1. (a) $V_{ref} = 9.0 \times \dfrac{10}{10+10} = 4.5$ V Equal resistors share the supply equally.
  2. (b) The comparator switches when $V_i = V_{ref} = 4.5$ V. The voltage across the thermistor is then 4.5 V, so the voltage across $R$ is $9.0 - 4.5 = 4.5$ V. Equal voltages mean equal resistances: $\dfrac{V_R}{V_{th}} = \dfrac{R}{R_{th}} \Rightarrow R = 3.6\ \text{k}\Omega$. The voltage across $R$ is found by taking away; the ratio then does the rest.
  3. (c) $V_i = 9.0 \times \dfrac{2.5}{3.6 + 2.5} = 3.69$ V. This is less than $V_{ref}$, so the output saturates positively: +9.0 V. $V_i \lt V_{ref}$ gives a positive output.
  4. (d) Coil current $I_C = 9.0/180 = 50$ mA, so $I_B = 50/100 = 0.50$ mA. Then $R_B = \dfrac{9.0 - 0.7}{0.50 \times 10^{-3}} = 16.6\ \text{k}\Omega$. The base resistor sees the comparator output (9.0 V) less the 0.7 V across the base–emitter junction. Any smaller standard value (15 kΩ) is fine.
  5. (e) Description, in the order things happen: as the temperature rises the thermistor's resistance falls, so its share of the supply, $V_i$, falls. When $V_i$ drops below $V_{ref}$ (at 50 °C) the comparator output saturates positive. A current flows through $R_B$ into the transistor base, so the transistor switches on and a collector current flows through the relay coil. The relay contacts close and the fan runs.
  6. (f) The relay coil is inductive. When the transistor switches off, the collapsing magnetic field would produce a large voltage spike, which could damage the transistor. The diode conducts the current and protects the transistor.

Typical mark allocation: (a) 1; (b) 3; (c) 2; (d) 3; (e) 4; (f) 2. Notice that (e) needs the cause-and-effect chain: every statement leads to the next.

Worked example 20 · Extended · the bilge-water alarm

Captain Reid wants a buzzer to sound when seawater reaches probes in the bilge. Two probes are the lower part of a divider with a 10 kΩ fixed resistor above them, across 9.0 V. Dry, the probes are an open circuit of about 1.0 MΩ; wet, seawater bridges them and the resistance is 2.2 kΩ. The probe voltage $V_i$ goes to the inverting input of a comparator with $V_{ref} = 4.5$ V (supply 9.0 V and 0 V). The comparator output drives an npn transistor ($h_{FE} = 150$) through a base resistor; the buzzer takes 30 mA.

(a) Calculate $V_i$ when the probes are dry and when wet. (b) State the comparator output in each case and say whether the buzzer sounds. (c) Calculate the probe resistance at which the alarm just sounds. (d) Calculate the largest base resistor, assuming a 9.0 V output and $V_{BE} = 0.7$ V. (e) State one change so that the buzzer sounds when the water is below the probes.

Show solution [10 marks]
  1. (a) Dry: $V_i = 9.0 \times \dfrac{1000}{10 + 1000} = 8.91$ V. Wet: $V_i = 9.0 \times \dfrac{2.2}{10 + 2.2} = 1.62$ V.
  2. (b) Dry: $V_i \gt V_{ref}$ (8.91 V > 4.5 V), so the output saturates negatively (here, falls to 0 V): the transistor has no base current, so the buzzer is silent. Wet: $V_i \lt V_{ref}$ (1.62 V < 4.5 V), so the output saturates positive (+9.0 V), the transistor turns on and the buzzer sounds. Water makes the probe resistance fall, which lowers $V_i$ below $V_{ref}$.
  3. (c) The alarm just sounds when $V_i = 4.5$ V, so the voltage across the probes equals the voltage across the fixed resistor: probe resistance $= 10\ \text{k}\Omega$. For any lower probe resistance the buzzer sounds.
  4. (d) $I_B = \dfrac{30}{150} = 0.20$ mA; $R_B = \dfrac{9.0 - 0.7}{0.20 \times 10^{-3}} = 41.5\ \text{k}\Omega$ at most. Pick a smaller standard value such as 33 kΩ.
  5. (e) Swap the two inputs of the comparator; or swap the fixed resistor and the probes in the divider. Either reverses which way the comparator switches.

A real-world note: bare probes in seawater can corrode if direct current flows through them for long. Here the point is the circuit, not the corrosion.

Worked example 21 · Description · a lamp that comes on at dusk

A fixed resistor and an LDR are connected in series across 9.0 V, and the junction goes to the base of an npn transistor with a lamp in its collector. Describe how the circuit operates as it gets dark.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. As it gets darker, the resistance of the LDR increases.
  2. So the LDR takes a bigger share of the supply: the voltage across it (the base–emitter voltage) rises.
  3. When this reaches about 0.7 V, a base current flows and the transistor starts to turn on.
  4. A larger collector current flows through the lamp, which lights.

A real-world limit: with the 237 kΩ resistor of worked example 6, this divider can supply only about $8.3 / 237\,000 = 35\ \mu$A of base current. With $h_{FE} = 100$ that switches at most 3.5 mA, enough for an LED but not a lamp. To switch a bigger load you would add a second transistor, or use a comparator in front of the output stage as in worked example 19.

Writing a description that earns the marks

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2024 and 2026 course reports

The 2024 report says "questions requiring a detailed written response proved to be challenging, and often candidates provided a non-descriptive answer which lacked the detail required to achieve marks at this level." The 2026 report adds that few candidates "correctly described the speed of the motor as the voltage dropped". The recipe below is the cure.

  1. Start at the cause (the thing that changes: "as the temperature rises…").
  2. Follow the signal in order, one device per sentence, saying what changes and in which direction (increases, decreases, rises above, falls below).
  3. Use the quantity's name: "resistance of the thermistor", "voltage at the inverting input", "base current", "collector current". Never "it" or "the signal".
  4. Name the threshold when one is crossed: "when $V_i$ falls below $V_{ref}$", "when $V_{GS}$ exceeds the threshold voltage", "when $V_{BE}$ reaches 0.7 V".
  5. End with the physical result in the language of the question: "the fan starts", "the pump stops", "the motor slows down".
  6. Mention any protection component (diode) if the question asks about every component.

Compare two answers to "describe what happens as the voltage at the motor falls", in a proportional circuit. A weak answer: "the motor changes." A strong answer: "As the voltage across the motor falls, the current through it decreases, so it produces less torque and the motor turns more slowly." The strong answer names the quantity, its direction, and the physical result.
