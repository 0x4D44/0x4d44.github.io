1 · Forces and equilibrium STRUCTURES & FORCES

Forces are vectors

A force is a push or a pull. It is measured in newtons (N); structures are so heavy that we usually use kilonewtons (kN), where $1\ \text{kN}=1000\ \text{N}$. A force has a size and a direction, so it is a vector. Two 10 kN forces pulling the same way make 20 kN. Pulling opposite ways they make 0. Pulling at right angles to each other they make about 14 kN, in between. That last answer is why we need a method for adding forces that are not in line.

A structure that is standing still is in equilibrium: nothing is speeding up or slowing down or starting to turn, so all the forces on it balance. Most of structural engineering is using that one idea to work out forces you cannot measure from forces you know.

Tension and compression

Every force inside a structural member either stretches it or squeezes it.

  • Tension pulls the member longer. A rope, a chain, a shroud, a tie-rod: they are good in tension. A rope cannot take anything else, because it goes slack if you push it.
  • Compression squeezes the member shorter. A mast, a strut, a stone pier: they carry compression.

Think about what the member does to the joints at its ends. A member in tension pulls each joint towards the member. A member in compression pushes each joint away from the member. We will use that rule every time we draw a node diagram in chapter 3, so make it automatic now.

Concurrent and non-concurrent forces

The line of action of a force is the straight line it acts along. If the lines of action of all the forces on a body pass through one point, they are concurrent. Concurrent forces can push a body along, but they cannot twist it: the ring on the end of a mast's forestay is a good example. If the lines of action do not meet at one point, the forces are non-concurrent, and they can twist the body as well as push it. A beam with loads at different places is the classic case.

Resolving a force into components

The trick that makes every structures question possible is to replace one slanted force by two forces at right angles that together do exactly the same job. These are its components. We take one horizontal and one vertical, so that the two equilibrium equations can be written separately.

For a force $F$ at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal:

$$F_h=F\cos\theta\qquad F_v=F\sin\theta.$$

The component next to the angle uses cosine. The component opposite the angle uses sine. If you are given the angle to the vertical instead, the two swap: $F_h=F\sin\alpha$ and $F_v=F\cos\alpha$. Draw the little right-angled triangle and label the angle before you write a cosine or a sine; never pick them from memory.

The reverse also works. Given the two components, the force is $F=\sqrt{F_h^2+F_v^2}$ and its angle to the horizontal is $\tan\theta=\dfrac{F_v}{F_h}$.

Worked example 1 · Calculation · resolving a stay

The forestay of HMS Ratchet's mainmast carries a tension of 24 kN and makes an angle of 50° with the horizontal deck. Calculate the horizontal and vertical components of the force the forestay exerts on the masthead. [2]

Show solution [2 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $F_h=F\cos\theta=24\cos50^\circ=15.4\ \text{kN}$ The horizontal component is next to the 50° angle, so it uses cosine. Keep the full calculator value; round only the final answer.
  2. $F_v=F\sin\theta=24\sin50^\circ=18.4\ \text{kN}$ The vertical component is opposite the angle: sine. State the unit.

Check: $\sqrt{15.4269^2+18.3851^2}=24.0$ kN ✔. Common slip: calculator in radians mode. $\cos50$ should be $0.643$, not $0.965$.

Resultant and equilibrant

Several forces acting at a point can always be replaced by a single force with exactly the same effect. That single force is the resultant, $R$. To find it, resolve every force into horizontal and vertical components, add the components, and rebuild:

$$R_h=\Sigma F_h,\qquad R_v=\Sigma F_v,\qquad R=\sqrt{R_h^2+R_v^2},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{R_v}{R_h}.$$

Be careful with the quadrant. The calculator's $\tan^{-1}$ only gives angles between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. Look at the signs of $R_h$ and $R_v$ to see which way the resultant points.

The equilibrant, $E$, is the one extra force that would put the system in equilibrium. It is equal in size to the resultant and opposite in direction. Resultant and equilibrant are not two different calculations: find one and you have the other.

Worked example 2 · Calculation · resultant and equilibrant

Two ropes pull on a ring-bolt on the quarterdeck. One pulls with 8 kN due east. The other pulls with 6 kN in a direction 120° anticlockwise from east (that is, 60° above due west). Calculate (a) the size and direction of the resultant force and (b) the equilibrant. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\Sigma F_h=8+6\cos120^\circ=8-3=5.00\ \text{kN}$ (east) Add the horizontal components. $\cos120^\circ$ is negative: the second rope pulls westwards.
  2. $\Sigma F_v=0+6\sin120^\circ=5.20\ \text{kN}$ (up) Add the vertical components. The 8 kN rope has none.
  3. $R=\sqrt{5.00^2+5.196^2}=7.21\ \text{kN}$, at $\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{5.196}{5.00}\right)=46.1^\circ$ above east Both components are positive, so the resultant points up and to the east: no quadrant correction.
  4. Equilibrant: 7.21 kN at 46.1° below due west (that is, towards the south-west) Same size, opposite direction. Opposite means add 180° to the direction, or reverse both component signs.

The triangle and polygon of forces

There is a picture to go with all this. If three forces on a point are in equilibrium, drawn head to tail in any order, they make a closed triangle. If there are more than three forces, they make a closed polygon. The picture works because the forces add up to zero, so following them one after another brings you back to where you started.

You can use this two ways. By scale drawing: choose a scale (say 1 cm = 5 kN), draw the known forces head to tail at their true angles, draw the closing line, then measure its length and angle with a ruler and protractor. Or by calculation: treat the triangle as a normal triangle and use the sine rule, or (usually quicker, and always safe) go back to horizontal and vertical components. A scale drawing is only as accurate as your ruler, so quote its answers to two significant figures and check them by calculation where you can. A question that says "calculate" wants the calculation.

The closing side of an open polygon of known forces is their resultant. Reverse its arrow and it is the equilibrant.

Solving for an unknown force and angle

A typical question gives you a ring in equilibrium under several forces, with one or two things unknown, and asks you to find them. The method never changes:

  1. Draw the point (the ring, the joint) and every force acting on it, with angles marked.
  2. Resolve each force into horizontal and vertical components.
  3. Write $\Sigma F_h=0$ and $\Sigma F_v=0$.
  4. You now have two equations. If there are two unknowns, they are a pair of simultaneous equations: solve by substitution (or elimination).
  5. If the unknown is a force and its angle, solve for its two components, then rebuild $F=\sqrt{F_h^2+F_v^2}$ and $\tan\theta=F_v/F_h$.

Worked example 4 · Calculation · unknown force and angle

A fitting on the mainmast is pulled by a force of 15 kN at 30° above the horizontal towards the east, and by a force of 10 kN at 45° above the horizontal towards the west. A third force $F$ keeps the fitting in equilibrium. Calculate the size of $F$ and the angle it makes with the horizontal. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Take east and up as positive. The known forces give $\Sigma F_h=15\cos30^\circ-10\cos45^\circ=12.990-7.071=5.919$ kN (east) and $\Sigma F_v=15\sin30^\circ+10\sin45^\circ=7.5+7.071=14.571$ kN (up). Resolve both known forces and add their components.

2 · Moments and beams STRUCTURES & FORCES

The turning effect of a force

Push on the end of a long spanner and the nut turns. Push the same amount close to the nut and it does not. A force turns things, and how much it turns depends on how far from the pivot you apply it. That turning effect is the moment of the force:

$$M=Fx$$

where $F$ is the force and $x$ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. The unit is the newton metre (N m), or kN m for big structures. A moment is clockwise or anticlockwise, so pick one direction as positive and stick to it.

The word perpendicular is doing all the work. The distance that counts is the shortest distance from the pivot to the line of action, which meets that line at a right angle. Two ways to get it right when the force is slanted:

  1. Resolve the force. Split it into a component along the lever and a component at right angles to the lever. Only the component at right angles turns the lever: $M=(F\sin\varphi)\times L$, where $\varphi$ is the angle between the force and the lever. The component along the lever passes through the pivot and has no moment.
  2. Find the perpendicular distance. Extend the line of action, drop a perpendicular from the pivot, and use $x=L\sin\varphi$ in $M=Fx$. Same answer.

Worked example 5 · Calculation · moment of an inclined force

A cadet pushes on the end of a 0.60 m capstan bar with a force of 250 N. The force makes an angle of 30° with the bar. (a) Calculate the moment of the force about the capstan axis. [2] (b) A rope wound round the capstan barrel, of radius 0.15 m, exactly balances this moment. Calculate the tension in the rope. [1]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) Perpendicular component of the force $=250\sin30^\circ=125\ \text{N}$ Only the part at right angles to the bar turns it.
  2. $M=Fx=125\times0.60=75\ \text{N m}$ Multiply the force by the distance. The 2024 course report notes that a few candidates did not multiply the forces by the distance from the fulcrum.
  3. (b) $T\times0.15=75$, so $T=500\ \text{N}$ The rope's force acts at right angles to the radius of the barrel, so the distance is simply the radius.

The principle of moments and the three conditions

A body that is not turning has no net moment. The principle of moments says that, about any point,

$$\Sigma M_{\text{clockwise}}=\Sigma M_{\text{anticlockwise}}\qquad(\Sigma M=0).$$

Put that together with the force equations from chapter 1 and you have the three conditions for a flat structure to be in equilibrium. They are all in the Data Booklet:

$$\Sigma F_h=0\qquad\Sigma F_v=0\qquad\Sigma M=0$$

You may take moments about any point you like, and a clever choice saves work: pick a point where an unknown force acts. That force has zero perpendicular distance, so it drops out of the equation.

Supports and reactions

A support pushes back on the structure with a reaction force. The Higher course uses two kinds of support.

  • A hinge (pin) lets the beam turn but not move sideways or up and down. It can push in any direction, so it has two unknown reaction components: a vertical $A_y$ and a horizontal $A_x$.
  • A roller lets the beam turn and slide along the surface it sits on. It can only push at right angles to that surface, so it has one unknown reaction. On a level surface that is vertical. On a slope it is tilted, at right angles to the slope.

A beam with one hinge and one roller has three unknown reaction components, and there are three equilibrium equations: just enough. That arrangement is called simply supported.

Uniformly distributed loads

Planking, a snow load, or the weight of the beam itself spreads along the beam evenly. This is a uniformly distributed load (UDL), quoted in kN per metre. To use it in a moment equation, replace it by one point load:

$$\text{total load}=w\times\text{length of the UDL},\qquad\text{acting at the middle of that length.}$$

Take care with a partial UDL: the length is the length the load covers, not the length of the beam, and the middle is the middle of the loaded part. The distance to use in the moment is from your pivot to that middle point.

Finding the reactions of a simply supported beam

The same eight steps solve every beam in the course:

  1. Draw the beam with its supports, every load and the unknown reactions (a free-body diagram; chapter 4).
  2. Resolve any inclined load into vertical and horizontal components.
  3. Replace each UDL by its point load at the middle of its length.
  4. Take moments about the hinge (or another support). Roller reaction $\times$ distance = sum of (vertical load $\times$ distance). This gives the roller reaction.
  5. Balance the vertical forces: hinge $A_y$ + roller = total downward load. This gives $A_y$.
  6. Balance the horizontal forces. The roller gives none, so $A_x$ equals the total horizontal load, in the opposite direction.
  7. If the hinge has both components, combine them: $R_A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}$ at $\tan\theta=A_y/A_x$. A question that asks for "the reaction" at a hinge wants the magnitude and the direction.
  8. Check by taking moments about the other support. If the check fails, hunt the slip.

3 · Frames and nodal analysis STRUCTURES & FORCES

Pin-jointed frames

A frame (or truss) is a structure built from straight members joined together at their ends. The dock bridge, the derrick that lifts the guns and the masts with their stays are all frames. For the Higher course we make three simplifying assumptions, which are very close to the truth for a real truss:

  • the members are joined at joints (also called nodes) by smooth pins, so a member is free to turn about its end;
  • loads and reactions are applied at the joints, not in the middle of a member;
  • the weights of the members are ignored.

With those assumptions, each member has force at only two points, its two ends, and the member is in equilibrium. Two forces can only balance if they are equal, opposite and lie along the same line, which is the line of the member. So each member is a two-force member: it carries a single force along its own axis, either tension or compression. That is why one number and a letter (T or C) describe a whole member.

The shape that makes a frame stiff is the triangle. A four-sided pin-jointed frame can fold flat; add one diagonal and the two triangles cannot change shape without a member changing length. Count to check a frame is just stiff enough: with $m$ members, $j$ joints and $r$ reaction components, a frame that is simply stiff needs $m+r=2j$ (two equilibrium equations per joint). The Warren truss below has $7+3=10=2\times5$.

The method of joints

Nodal analysis (the method of joints) applies the concurrent-force equations of chapter 1 to one joint at a time. All the member forces meeting at a joint pass through the joint, so they are concurrent: $\Sigma F_h=0$ and $\Sigma F_v=0$ are enough.

  1. Reactions first. Treat the whole frame as one beam (chapter 2) and find the support reactions.
  2. Choose a joint with no more than two unknown members, usually a support joint. (At a joint with three unknowns you have only two equations.)
  3. Draw the joint on its own with every force on it: the load or reaction if there is one, the member forces you already know, and one arrow for each unknown member. Assume every unknown member is in tension: arrow pointing away from the joint, along the member.
  4. Resolve the inclined members into horizontal and vertical components.
  5. Write $\Sigma F_h=0$ and $\Sigma F_v=0$ and solve for the two unknowns.
  6. Read the sign. Positive: you guessed right, the member is in tension (T). Negative: it is in compression (C); quote the size without the minus sign and write C.
  7. Move to the next joint, now carrying the member forces you have found with their true directions. Repeat until all members are done. The last joint is a free check: its forces should balance.

The rule for drawing the arrows on a joint diagram is the one from chapter 1. Tension: the member pulls the joint, so the arrow points away from the joint along the member. Compression: the member pushes the joint, so the arrow points towards the joint.

Worked example 11 · Calculation · the derrick, with simultaneous equations

The derrick is a frame ABCD. The mast AB is vertical and 4 m high, A is pinned to the deck, the boom AC rises from A to the tip C (3 m across and 1 m up), the rope BC (topping lift) joins the mast head to the boom tip, and the backstay BD joins the mast head to the deck at D, 3 m on the other side of A. A 20 kN gun hangs from C. (a) Calculate the size and nature of the forces in AC and BC. (b) Calculate the force in BD and in AB. [8]

Show solution [8 marks, typical allocation]

Reading a direction from coordinates. A member that runs $a$ across and $b$ up has length $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. Its force has horizontal component $\dfrac{a}{\text{length}}\times F$ and vertical component $\dfrac{b}{\text{length}}\times F$: these are just $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ of the member's angle. For AC, 3 across and 1 up, the length is $\sqrt{10}$.

  1. Joint C has the load and only two members, so start there (no reactions needed). Directions from C: to A is $\left(-3,-1\right)/\sqrt{10}$, so cos = $0.9487$ to the left and sin = $0.3162$ downwards; to B is $\left(-3,+3\right)/\sqrt{18}$, so 45° up and to the left. Work out the slopes from the coordinates: 3 across and 1 up for AC; 3 across and 3 up for BC.
  2. $\Sigma F_h=0$: $-0.9487\,F_{AC}-0.7071\,F_{BC}=0$ Assume both are tension (arrows away from C).
  3. $\Sigma F_v=0$: $-0.3162\,F_{AC}+0.7071\,F_{BC}-20=0$ The 20 kN load pulls down.
  4. Add the two equations: $-1.2649\,F_{AC}=20$, so $F_{AC}=-15.8$ kN: compression, 15.8 kN. Elimination: the $F_{BC}$ terms cancel when you add. The boom is a strut.
  5. Then $F_{BC}=\dfrac{-0.9487\times(-15.81)}{0.7071}=+21.2$ kN: tension, 21.2 kN. The topping lift is a rope, so it had better come out in tension.
  6. Joint B. BC is in tension, so it pulls B towards C: components $21.21\cos45^\circ=15.0$ kN to the right and $15.0$ kN down. The backstay BD (tension, away from B) has components $0.6F_{BD}$ to the left and $0.8F_{BD}$ down; AB (assumed tension, away from B) points down. $\Sigma F_h=0$: $15.0-0.6F_{BD}=0$, so $F_{BD}=+25.0$ kN: tension. BD is the 3-4-5 triangle, so its slope gives clean numbers: $\cos=3/5$, $\sin=4/5$.
  7. $\Sigma F_v=0$: $-15.0-0.8\times25.0-F_{AB}=0$, so $F_{AB}=-35.0$ kN: compression, 35.0 kN. The mast takes the weight of everything hanging from its head.

Sometimes a member comes out as exactly 0: if three members meet at a joint with no load, and two of them lie along one straight line, the third carries nothing. You do not need this shortcut for the course, because the equations find the zero anyway (see the Pratt bridge in the Frame Table), but it is useful as a check. A zero-force member still matters: it stops the frame folding if the load moves.

4 · Diagrams of structures STRUCTURES & FORCES

Four pictures of one structure

Before you can calculate anything about a structure you have to draw it in a way that shows the forces. There are four kinds of drawing you will meet, and they do different jobs.

The space diagram is the picture in the question. The skill is turning it into a free-body diagram, and that is what the marks in a "draw the diagram" question are for.

Cut it free

The idea behind a free-body diagram is to imagine cutting the structure free from everything else and replacing everything you cut away by the force it was exerting. The ground under a hinge is replaced by two reaction arrows. A stay you have cut is replaced by a tension arrow along the stay. A cart is replaced by its weight. After that nothing else touches the structure, so the three equilibrium equations apply to it directly.

The rules for a good diagram

  • An arrow is a force. Its tail is where it acts and its head shows the direction. Draw it along the line of action, at its true angle.
  • Use the standard support symbols from chapter 2: a hinge (triangle on a pin) and a roller (triangle on wheels). Never draw a bare dot for a support.
  • Show unknown reactions as arrows too, drawn in a sensible direction. If your sums give a negative answer, the arrow points the other way; do not redraw it.
  • Dimensions go on the diagram. Every distance needed for a moment, measured from a single point, and every angle.
  • Units and labels. kN, kN/m, m and degrees. Letters for the points (A, B, C…) and for the unknowns.
  • Draw the structure as a line or bar, not as a picture. A beam is a rod; a truss member is a straight line between two joints.
  • Show a slack rope as slack. A rope cannot push, so if it goes loose, leave it out (or dash it) and do not give it a force.

A few conventions for frames: a member in tension is often drawn in red and one in compression in blue, or marked T and C, and in a joint diagram the tension arrows point away from the joint (chapter 3).

Draw it: building a free-body diagram in six layers

The "draw it" checklist

Run down this list every time, whether you are drawing the diagram or being asked to complete one. It takes ten seconds, and a missing arrow costs a mark that the calculation could never earn back.

  1. Structure alone. Is every piece of the surroundings cut away?
  2. Supports. Hinge, roller or tie, and the right number of reaction arrows at each (two at a hinge, one at a roller)?
  3. Every load. Point loads, inclined loads at their true angle, UDLs as a row of arrows with kN/m, and weights?
  4. Every reaction. Does the number of unknowns match the number of equations you have?
  5. Dimensions. Can you read every moment arm off the diagram?
  6. Labels and units. Letters, kN, m, degrees?
  7. Sense check. Does the pattern make sense? Loads press down, reactions push up, stays pull.

5 · Materials and their properties MATERIALS

Why the material matters

A frame diagram tells you the force in a member. It does not tell you whether the member will survive it. That depends on what it is made of. HMS Ratchet is built from oak, iron and copper, and each was chosen for what it does well. Oak planking is tough and cheap. Iron fittings are strong. Copper sheathing on the hull below the waterline keeps weed and shipworm away, and it is soft enough to be rolled into thin sheets. (Sailors also learnt that iron bolts rot quickly when they touch copper in sea water, which is a good reminder that a material has to be chosen with its neighbours in mind.) Minecraft, of course, does not do any of this: every block is exactly as strong as every other until it is not there. In the real Rigging Loft you have to measure.

The properties you need

The course specification lists six properties: strength, elasticity, plasticity, ductility, malleability and brittleness. The table adds two more that you will need to talk about materials sensibly: stiffness and toughness. Learn them as pairs of opposites where you can.

PropertyWhat it meansOn the ship
Strengthhow large a stress the material can withstand before it breaks or deforms for gooda shroud that holds the mast up in a gale
Stiffnesshow little it stretches or bends elastically under load: a high Young's modulus $E$a yard that must not sag
Elasticityreturns to its original size and shape when the load is removeda spring in a gun-lock
Plasticityis permanently deformed once the elastic limit is passed, and keeps its new shapea bent bolt that stays bent
Ductilitycan be stretched a long way into a wire, with a large plastic extension before it breaksiron chain that sags and stretches before it parts
Malleabilitycan be hammered, bent or rolled into shape (in compression) without crackingcopper sheets for the hull

Brittlenessbreaks suddenly, with little or no plastic deformation and no warninga cast-iron cannon that bursts

Strong is not the same as stiff. A strong material takes a big force before it fails. A stiff material barely stretches under a force. Steel and aluminium are both fairly strong, but steel's modulus is nearly three times larger, so a steel rod stretches only about a third as much under the same stress. A rope is strong and not stiff at all. Keep those two ideas apart: they are the commonest confusion in the whole topic.

The yard must hardly bend, even under the full sail's weight.
A cast-iron cannon bursts without any warning.
The anchor cable must take a 300 kN pull without parting.
Copper is hammered flat into thin sheets to cover the hull.
A copper ingot is drawn out into a long length of wire.
An iron chain link stretches and thins visibly before it finally breaks.

The tensile test

To measure these properties we pull a specimen until it breaks. A tensile-testing machine grips a bar of known cross-section area $A$ and original length $l$, stretches it slowly, and records the force $F$ and the extension $x$ ($=\Delta l$) all the way to fracture. Plotting force against extension gives a load-extension graph. Dividing every force by $A$ and every extension by $l$ turns it into a stress-strain graph, $\sigma=F/A$ against $\varepsilon=\Delta l/l$. It has the same shape, but now it is a property of the material and not of that particular bar, so we can compare steel with aluminium directly.

Reading the curve

  • Elastic region. From the origin to the limit of proportionality the graph is a straight line: stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law). Unload at any point and the specimen goes back to its original length. Its slope is the Young's modulus $E$.
  • Elastic limit. The most stress the material can take and still spring fully back. For metals it is very close to the limit of proportionality, so we often treat the two as one.
  • Yield point. Soft steels show a sudden give: the stress drops slightly and the strain increases a lot with almost no extra load. After this the material has yielded and is in the plastic region. Metals without a clear yield point (aluminium alloys, for example) are given a proof stress, found by drawing a line parallel to the elastic slope, offset by 0.2% strain.
  • Strain hardening follows: the metal gets stronger as it is deformed, so the stress rises again.
  • Ultimate tensile stress (UTS). The highest point on the stress-strain graph: the largest stress the material ever carries.
  • Necking and fracture. A ductile metal now thins at one place (a neck), the force needed falls, and the specimen breaks. A brittle material has almost no plastic region and breaks suddenly near the end of its straight line.
  • Permanent set. Unload in the plastic region and the graph comes back down on a line parallel to the elastic slope, not on the way it went up. It reaches zero stress at a strain that is not zero. That leftover is the permanent deformation.

The key-point buttons jump to each feature of the curve. The brittle cast iron shows how different a curve can look. (The strain axis is schematic, so that the small elastic part is visible.)

Worked example 13 · Description · applying and removing a load

A test specimen of mild steel is gradually loaded in a tensile-testing machine until it is well past its yield point and the load is then removed. Describe what happens to the specimen. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]

6 · Stress, strain, safety and strain gauges MATERIALS

Stress and strain

Stress is the force on each unit of cross-section area. A thick rope and a thin cord can carry the same tension, but the cord is under far more stress, and it is stress, not force, that decides when a material fails.

$$\sigma=\frac{F}{A}$$

Here $F$ is the force acting at right angles to the cross-section and $A$ is the area of that cross-section. The SI unit is the pascal, $1\ \text{Pa}=1\ \text{N/m}^2$, but it is tiny, so we use the megapascal: $1\ \text{MPa}=1\ \text{N/mm}^2=10^6\ \text{Pa}$. Convert the area to mm² when the force is in N and you want N/mm², or to m² when you want Pa. A round bar has $A=\dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}$ and a tube has $A=\dfrac{\pi}{4}(D^2-d^2)$.

Strain is the fractional change in length:

$$\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta l}{l}$$

where $\Delta l$ is the extension and $l$ is the original length, in the same units. Strain is a ratio, so it has no unit. Strain gauge makers quote it in microstrain ($1\ \mu\varepsilon=10^{-6}$).

Young's modulus

In the elastic region stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law). The constant of proportionality is the Young's modulus of elasticity, $E$:

$$E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$

It is the slope of the straight part of the stress-strain graph, and it measures stiffness. A high $E$ means a large stress for a small strain. The Data Booklet gives $E$ in kN/mm², which is the same as GPa: mild steel is $196\ \text{kN/mm}^2=196\ \text{GPa}=196\times10^9\ \text{Pa}$. Combining the three formulas gives the extension of a bar directly:

$$\Delta l=\frac{F\,l}{A\,E}.$$

You are not given this form, so derive it in the exam as three short steps: find $\sigma$, find $\varepsilon=\sigma/E$, then $\Delta l=\varepsilon\,l$. It also shows why a stiff, thick, short member stretches least.

The commonest arithmetic slip in this chapter is a power of ten. Type a value and see it in every unit, and see how the area of a round bar and the stress it carries change with diameter.

Factor of safety

No engineer designs a member to be loaded right up to its breaking stress. Loads are uncertain, materials vary, and failure would be disastrous. So we deliberately make the member stronger than it needs to be, by a margin called the factor of safety:

$$\text{factor of safety}=\frac{\text{ultimate load}}{\text{safe working load}}=\frac{\text{ultimate stress}}{\text{safe working stress}}$$

It has no unit. A factor of safety of 4 means the member could carry four times its working load before breaking. The ultimate stress comes from the Data Booklet: the ultimate tensile stress for a member in tension, the ultimate compressive stress for one in compression. Cast iron shows why this matters: its tensile ultimate stress is only 120 to 160 N/mm², but its compressive stress is 600 to 900. A cast-iron column is excellent. A cast-iron tie is not.

The sizing recipe uses the factor of safety backwards, to find how big a member must be:

  1. Safe working stress = ultimate stress ÷ factor of safety.
  2. Required area = working load ÷ safe working stress.
  3. Diameter from $A=\pi d^2/4$, or tube wall from $A=\pi(D^2-d^2)/4$.
  4. Round up to a standard size: a bar a hair too small is unsafe, one a hair too large is merely heavy.

Worked example 15 · Calculation · bolt diameter from a factor of safety

A mild steel bolt must support a tensile load of 85 kN on the dock-bridge hinge with a factor of safety of 4. The ultimate tensile stress of mild steel is 430 N/mm². Calculate the minimum diameter of the bolt. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Safe working stress $=\dfrac{430}{4}=107.5\ \text{N/mm}^2$ The factor of safety reduces the allowed stress.
  2. $A=\dfrac{F}{\sigma}=\dfrac{85\,000}{107.5}=790.7\ \text{mm}^2$ Force in newtons and stress in N/mm² give area in mm². Do not mix in m².
  3. $A=\dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}$, so $d=\sqrt{\dfrac{4\times790.7}{\pi}}=31.7\ \text{mm}$ Keep every digit of $A$ on the calculator.
  4. A bolt of at least 31.7 mm; in practice use the next standard size up (32 mm). State the answer to three significant figures and the practical consequence.

Worked example 16 · Calculation · tube wall thickness

A tubular mild steel strut of outer diameter 80 mm carries a compressive load of 120 kN. The factor of safety is 3 and the ultimate compressive stress of mild steel is 430 N/mm². Calculate the minimum wall thickness of the tube. [5]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Safe working stress $=\dfrac{430}{3}=143.3\ \text{N/mm}^2$ A compression member, so use the ultimate compressive stress.
  2. $A=\dfrac{120\,000}{143.33}=837.2\ \text{mm}^2$ The metal area the tube needs.
  3. $A=\dfrac{\pi}{4}(D^2-d^2)$, so $D^2-d^2=\dfrac{4\times837.2}{\pi}=1066.0$ Rearrange for the difference of the squares first.
  4. $d^2=80^2-1066.0=5334.0$, so $d=73.03\ \text{mm}$ The inner diameter, from the outer diameter given.
  5. $t=\dfrac{D-d}{2}=\dfrac{80-73.03}{2}=3.48\ \text{mm}$ The wall is half the difference of the diameters. Rounding $d$ to 73 before subtracting would give 3.5, which is still fine, but rounding earlier than that can move the answer visibly.

Elastic strain energy

Stretching a bar takes work, and in the elastic region that work is stored in the bar and given back when it is released. This is elastic strain energy. On a load-extension graph it is the area under the line, and for the straight Hooke's-law line that area is a triangle:

$$E_s=\tfrac12Fx$$

where $F$ is the final force and $x$ is the extension, so the unit is the joule (J) only if $x$ is in metres. Because the force is proportional to the extension, doubling the load doubles the extension and so quadruples the stored energy.

A stretched elastic band stores strain energy. So does a ship's cable, which is why a parting cable can whip back with lethal force: all that stored energy is released at once.

Worked example 17 · Calculation · strain energy and the units trap

A steel shroud stretches by 1.6 mm when the tension in it is 35 kN. The shroud is within its elastic region. (a) Calculate the strain energy stored. [2] (b) The tension is increased to 70 kN. Calculate the new strain energy. [2]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) $E_s=\tfrac12Fx=\tfrac12\times35\,000\times1.6\times10^{-3}$ Force in newtons, extension in metres: $1.6\ \text{mm}=0.0016\ \text{m}$.
  2. $E_s=28\ \text{J}$ With the unit! The 2024 course report notes that a few candidates lost this mark by not giving the correct units.
  3. (b) The load doubles, so the extension doubles to 3.2 mm: $E_s=\tfrac12\times70\,000\times3.2\times10^{-3}$ Hooke's law: the extension is proportional to the force while the shroud stays elastic.
  4. $E_s=112\ \text{J}$, four times the original, not twice. Energy goes as the square of the load.

Common slip: using $x=1.6$ with $F$ in newtons gives 28 000, which is wrong by a factor of a thousand.

Strain gauges

How do you measure the strain in a structure that is actually carrying a load, such as a stay, a boom or a glider spar? With a strain gauge: a thin zig-zag of metal foil on a plastic backing, glued onto the surface. When the surface is stretched, the foil stretches with it and becomes longer and thinner, so its resistance rises. When it is squeezed, its resistance falls. The change is tiny, and is described by the gauge factor $GF$:

$$\frac{\Delta R}{R}=GF\times\varepsilon\qquad\text{(for a metal-foil gauge }GF\approx2\text{)}$$

A strain of 0.1% (a thousand microstrain) changes the resistance by only 0.2%, which is 0.24 Ω on a 120 Ω gauge. You cannot see that on a multimeter, so the gauge is made one half of a potential divider. At rest the divider output is exactly half the supply (2.5 V for a 5 V supply). Strain moves it by a few millivolts, and a difference amplifier subtracts the 2.5 V reference and multiplies what is left by a gain $G$ (about 500) so that a microcontroller or a meter can read it.

Temperature is the trap. A warm gauge also changes its resistance, with no strain at all, and the electronics cannot tell the difference. The cure is a dummy gauge: an identical gauge glued on the same material, at the same temperature, but not carrying any load. It goes in the other half of the divider, so the two temperature changes cancel and only the strain remains. A still better arrangement mounts two gauges on opposite faces of a bending beam, where one is stretched and one is squeezed, so their signals add.

The gauge bench: strain, resistance and voltage

Load the beam, then change the circuit: a fixed resistor, a dummy gauge, or a pair on opposite faces. Warm the beam with the temperature slider and watch which circuit still gives a clean reading.

Worked example 18 · Calculation · reading a strain gauge

A strain gauge of resistance 120 Ω and gauge factor 2.0 is glued onto a mild steel stay. It forms a potential divider with a fixed 120 Ω resistor across a 5 V supply, the gauge being the lower resistor. When the stay is loaded the gauge strain is $8.0\times10^{-4}$. Calculate (a) the change in resistance of the gauge, (b) the divider output voltage, (c) the stress in the stay (E = 196 kN/mm²) and (d) the output of a difference amplifier of gain 500 that subtracts 2.5 V. State why a dummy gauge is used. [7]

Show solution [7 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) $\Delta R=GF\times\varepsilon\times R=2.0\times8.0\times10^{-4}\times120=0.192\ \Omega$, so the gauge is now 120.192 Ω Rearrange $\Delta R/R=GF\,\varepsilon$.
  2. (b) $V=5\times\dfrac{120.192}{120+120.192}=2.502\ \text{V}$ Potential divider: the output is the supply times the lower resistance over the total. It has moved by only 2.0 mV from 2.5 V.
  3. (c) $\sigma=E\varepsilon=196\times10^9\times8.0\times10^{-4}=1.57\times10^8\ \text{Pa}=157\ \text{MPa}$ This is how a strain gauge tells you the stress: measure strain, multiply by $E$.
  4. (d) $V_o=G(V-2.5)=500\times0.0020=1.0\ \text{V}$ The amplifier gain turns a 2 mV change into something a meter can read. Keep the unrounded 1.998 mV to get $0.9992\approx1.0$ V.
  5. A dummy gauge, unloaded and at the same temperature, cancels the resistance change caused by temperature, so only the change due to strain appears in the output. The 2024 report found the "purpose of strain gauge B" in a question challenging. Say: unstrained, same temperature, cancels temperature effects.

7 · Choosing and justifying a material MATERIALS

The job comes first

No material is best at everything, so choosing one is a matter of matching the job to the properties. Start from the load: is the member in tension, compression or bending? Is it loaded once or over and over? Must it stay the same shape (stiff), or give warning before it fails (ductile)? Then add the real-world conditions: salt water, cost, mass, how easily it can be shaped or joined. Only then look at the Data Booklet.

Some pairings come up again and again:

  • Tension members (stays, shrouds, ties, cables): high ultimate tensile stress and good ductility, so that they stretch and warn rather than snap.
  • Compression members (struts, masts, piers): high ultimate compressive stress and high stiffness. Cast iron and concrete are strong in compression and weak in tension.
  • Beams in bending have one face in tension and one in compression, so they need both.
  • Parts that must be formed (hull plates, sheathing): malleability or ductility, so they can be rolled or pressed without cracking.
  • Anything that must not move (a yard, a gun mounting, a spar): stiffness, a high $E$.

Justifying a choice with the Data Booklet

Descriptions score only if they are specific. “Titanium is stronger” earns less than “titanium alloy has a yield stress of 950 N/mm² against 250 N/mm² for aluminium alloy”. For every point in your answer give three things:

  1. The property, in the correct word (strength, stiffness, ductility…).
  2. The evidence: the figure with its unit, from the Booklet or the graph, for both materials if you are comparing.
  3. The consequence for the job: what this property does for that member.

Compare like with like: yield with yield, ultimate with ultimate, $E$ with $E$. And, if the question gives a load, a quick calculation of the stress or the factor of safety is the strongest evidence you can offer.

Worked example 19 · Extended answer · justify a material

An Air Cadet glider's wing spar can be made from aluminium alloy or titanium alloy. Using the Data Booklet, justify the use of titanium alloy for a spar that must be both slim and strong. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Strength: titanium alloy has a yield stress of 950 N/mm², nearly four times that of aluminium alloy (250 N/mm²). Property, evidence for both, comparison.
  2. Consequence: so a spar of smaller cross-section can carry the same load without yielding, which keeps the spar slim. Link the evidence to the job given in the question ("slim").
  3. Ultimate stress: its ultimate tensile stress is 1000 N/mm² against 300 N/mm², so it can be given a high factor of safety. A second independent piece of evidence.
  4. Stiffness: its Young's modulus is 110 kN/mm² against 70 kN/mm², so it bends less under the same stress and the wing keeps its shape. A third property. (Cost is the counter-argument: titanium is more expensive and harder to work, which is a fair point to add if asked for a drawback.)
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"Why not make everything out of the strongest material on the list?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "Because strength is only one thing on the list, and it is not free. The strongest alloy may cost twenty times as much, be hard to weld, or be less ductile, so that it fails suddenly. A hull needs thousands of planks, not one spar. An engineer asks: what is the least expensive material that does the job with an adequate factor of safety? That is a calculation, and then a judgement you must be able to defend."
