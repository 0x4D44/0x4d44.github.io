The Signal Locker · digital electronic control and programmable control

Digital & programmable control

Digital electronic control is the part of the course where a careful, tidy method earns nearly all the marks. The question-paper range for digital electronic control (which includes programmable control) is 15 to 25 marks. The 2024 course report said "Candidates performed very well in areas such as digital electronics and structures calculations", so the marks are there to be taken, and the ones that are lost are lost to small, avoidable slips. This deck shows you the slips.

Master Shipwright Morag Kerr has led 1805 (Blockhaven) Squadron below decks on HMS Ratchet to the Signal Locker. Along one wall hang the Popham flag lockers (Popham signals are coloured flags the navy hoisted in combinations to send messages) and the signal lamps. On the other wall is the ship's redstone relay board, a panel of torches, repeaters and dust. "Everything in this room," she says, "is a code built from things that are either one way or the other. A flag is hoisted or it is not. A lamp is lit or it is dark. Learn to think in two states and you can describe, build and program the whole ship's control system."

The deck has six chapters: logic gates (chapter 1), going between specifications, truth tables, Boolean expressions and diagrams (chapter 2), NAND equivalents and counting ICs (integrated circuits, the chips that gates come in) (chapter 3), programmable control with microcontrollers (chapter 4), pulse-width modulation (chapter 5) and putting it all together the way an exam question does (chapter 6). Every chapter ends with a pre-flight check. Work through the widgets: they are there because watching a circuit or a program run teaches more than reading about it.

1 · Digital vs analogue, and logic gates DIGITAL · logic functions

Two kinds of signal

An analogue signal can take any value in a range. The voltage from a temperature sensor, the depth read from a sounding line and the pressure in a boiler are all analogue: they change smoothly, and every tiny change matters. A digital signal can take only a small, fixed set of values. In digital electronics that set is nearly always two values: 1 (high, ON, true, often about +5 V) and 0 (low, OFF, false, about 0 V).

The Signal Locker is full of digital codes. A Popham signal flag is either at the masthead or it is not. A signal lamp is lit or dark, and a lamp flashing a pattern of long and short is a digital message. In the game, a redstone torch or repeater is digital (powered or not), whereas redstone dust, whose strength has 16 steps from 0 to 15, is a loose analogy for an analogue signal: it carries a level, not just a yes or no. (It is only an analogy, because a real analogue signal can take any value in between, not just whole steps.)

Why bother with only two values? Because a two-valued signal is easy to protect. If the receiver only has to decide "is this nearer 0 or nearer 1?", a bit of noise on the wire does not matter. 📘 Logic levels and truth tables

A logic gate is a circuit that turns one or more digital inputs into one digital output according to a fixed rule. A truth table lists every possible combination of inputs and the output each one gives. With $n$ inputs there are $2^n$ rows: 4 rows for two inputs, 8 for three, 16 for four. By convention the rows are written in binary counting order, with the first input as the leftmost digit: 000, 001, 010, 011, 100, and so on. Keep to that order so that nobody (including the marker) can lose their place.

The six gates

The course asks for six gates: AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR and EOR, with up to four inputs. A gate's shape tells you its job. AND has a flat back and a rounded front. OR has a curved back that comes to a point. NOT is a triangle. A small circle (a bubble) on an output means "inverted", so NAND is "AND with a NOT on the end", and NOR is "OR with a NOT on the end". EOR (exclusive OR, also written XOR) is an OR with an extra curved line at the back.

AND, OR, NAND and NOR can have two, three or four inputs. NOT always has one. EOR has two. A three-input AND gate is 1 only if all three inputs are 1. A four-input NOR gate is 1 only if all four inputs are 0. The rules never change, only the number of rows in the table.

🪔 Aside: is a redstone torch a NOT gate?

Yes, and it is the best everyday NOT gate there is. A redstone torch sits on a block. When the block has power the torch goes out, and when the block has no power the torch is lit. Input 1 gives output 0; input 0 gives output 1. Run two input wires into the same block, with one torch on it, and you have a NOR gate: the block is powered if either input is on (that is an OR), and the torch then goes out (that is the NOT). Real chips do exactly the same job with transistors. (A torch also takes a moment, about a tenth of a second, to change. Real gates take a few nanoseconds, but they too are not instant.)

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"OR gives 1 when either input is 1, so why does 1 and 1 give 0 for an EOR? Isn't 'either' the same as OR?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "In everyday English 'either' sometimes means 'one or the other but not both', and that is the EOR. An ordinary OR means 'one or the other or both'. The E stands for exclusive: it excludes the case where both are on. Remember it as 'the inputs are different'. Two switches at the same position give 0; two in different positions give 1. That is why a lantern with a switch at each end of the gun deck is an EOR."

2 · From specification to truth table, expression and diagram DIGITAL · design and analysis

A control problem arrives as a sentence: "the alarm sounds if the hatch is open and the master switch is on". Your job is to turn the sentence into something you can build. There is a fixed route, and it can be walked in either direction. Learn the route and a whole page of exam marks becomes mechanical.

📘 The notation used in this deck

Boolean expressions describe a circuit in algebra. This deck uses: AND as a dot, $A \cdot B$; OR as a plus, $A + B$; NOT as a bar over the top, $\overline{A}$; and EOR as a circled plus, $A \oplus B$. A bar over a longer expression inverts all of it, so $\overline{A \cdot B}$ is a NAND and $\overline{A + B}$ is a NOR. AND binds tighter than OR, exactly like multiplication before addition: $A \cdot B + C$ means $(A \cdot B) + C$. Check which symbols the question paper uses and write in its style, but always make the meaning unmistakable, with brackets if in doubt.

Specification to truth table

Give every input a letter and decide what 1 means for each (switch pressed, hatch open, temperature too high). Write all $2^n$ input rows in binary order. Then, for each row, read the specification and decide whether the output should be 1 or 0. This is the slow, careful step, and the one where the most thought goes. Do it row by row, never in your head all at once.

Truth table to Boolean expression: sum of products

The reliable method is the sum of products. Look only at the rows where $Q = 1$. For each such row, write an AND term that is true for that row alone: use the input as it is where the row has a 1, and the inverted input (with a bar) where the row has a 0. Then OR all these terms together. Finally, if the question asks for it or if it saves gates, simplify.

The reason it works: each AND term is "switched on" by exactly one row, and the OR collects every row that must give 1. Nothing else can make the output 1.

Expression to logic diagram, and diagram to expression

To draw a circuit from an expression, work from the right (the output) backwards: the last operation is the gate nearest the output. $Q = A \cdot \overline{B} + C$ ends with an OR, whose inputs are the product $A \cdot \overline{B}$ (an AND, fed by $A$ and a NOT of $B$) and the plain input $C$. To go the other way, from a diagram to an expression, label the output of every gate with the expression it produces, starting at the inputs and working along. Each wire carries an expression until you reach $Q$.

📘 Identities you may use to simplify

These are always true, and you can check each with a two-row truth table: $B + \overline{B} = 1$; $B \cdot \overline{B} = 0$; $A + A\cdot B = A$; $A + A = A$; $A \cdot A = A$; $\overline{\overline{A}} = A$. De Morgan's rules turn an AND into an OR: $\overline{A \cdot B} = \overline{A} + \overline{B}$ and $\overline{A + B} = \overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}$. Chapter 3 uses the second form to build ORs from NANDs.

Worked example 3 · Table to expression · sum of products

A circuit has inputs $A$, $B$, $C$ and output $Q$. $Q = 1$ for the input combinations 001, 011 and 111 only. Write an expression for $Q$, and simplify it.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. One AND term per row where $Q = 1$. Row 001: $A=0, B=0, C=1$ gives $\overline{A} \cdot \overline{B} \cdot C$. Row 011: $\overline{A} \cdot B \cdot C$. Row 111: $A \cdot B \cdot C$. Inverted where the row has a 0.
  2. OR them: $Q = \overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}\cdot C + \overline{A}\cdot B\cdot C + A\cdot B\cdot C$. This is the unsimplified answer, and it is correct.
  3. Simplify. The first two terms differ only in $B$, so $\overline{A}\cdot C\cdot(\overline{B}+B) = \overline{A}\cdot C$. That leaves $Q = \overline{A}\cdot C + B\cdot C = C\cdot(\overline{A} + B)$.

Check by testing a row: 101 gives $C=1$ but $\overline{A}+B = 0+0 = 0$, so $Q = 0$, which matches the table (101 is not in the list).

Worked example 4 · Diagram to expression

An EOR gate takes $A$ and $B$ and gives $X$. A NOR gate takes $C$ and $D$ and gives $Y$. An AND gate takes $X$ and $Y$ and gives $Q$. Write the expression for $Q$.

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. Label each gate output: $X = A \oplus B$ and $Y = \overline{C + D}$.
  2. The last gate is an AND of $X$ and $Y$: $Q = (A \oplus B)\cdot\overline{C + D}$. Use brackets so the EOR is clearly a unit; without them the dot and the circled plus could be misread.

Worked example 5 · Four inputs · the hatch alarm

A lamp $Q$ must light when the master switch $A$ is on, the pressure switch $D$ is not on, and at least one of the switches $B$ or $C$ is on. (a) Write the expression. (b) Give the sum of products from the truth table.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Three conditions joined by "and": $Q = A \cdot (B + C) \cdot \overline{D}$. "At least one of B or C" is an OR; "not on" is the bar.
  2. The table has 16 rows. $Q = 1$ needs $A = 1$ and $D = 0$ with $B$ or $C$ on: rows 1010, 1100 and 1110. Order the inputs A, B, C, D.
  3. Sum of products: $Q = A\cdot\overline{B}\cdot C\cdot\overline{D} + A\cdot B\cdot\overline{C}\cdot\overline{D} + A\cdot B\cdot C\cdot\overline{D}$.
  4. Compare: the factorised form needs one OR, one AND of three, and one NOT. The sum of products needs far more. Always look for the shorter form when the question asks you to minimise.

Worked example 6 · Expression to diagram

Draw a logic diagram for $Q = A \cdot \overline{B} + C$.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. The output is a two-input OR. One input is $C$ directly. The last operation in the expression is the plus.
  2. The other input comes from a two-input AND of $A$ and $\overline{B}$.
  3. $\overline{B}$ comes from a NOT gate on input $B$.

Label every input and the output, and leave the gate symbols clear. Do not let wires cross unless they must, and mark any junction with a dot.

3 · NAND equivalents, universality and counting ICs DIGITAL · NAND equivalents

Every circuit in chapter 2 used a mixture of gate types. A real board built that way would need several different chips, most of them mostly empty. It is cheaper and tidier to build the whole circuit from a single kind of gate. That kind is the NAND, and the reason is a remarkable fact.

NAND is universal

Any logic function at all can be built from NAND gates alone. A NAND gate is therefore called a universal gate. (NOR is universal too, but the course asks for NAND.) Three small recipes are all you need.

  • NOT from a NAND: join the inputs together. With $A$ on both inputs, $\overline{A \cdot A} = \overline{A}$.
  • AND from NANDs: a NAND followed by a NOT (a NAND with its inputs tied). Two NANDs.
  • OR from NANDs: invert each input first, then NAND them: $\overline{\overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}} = A + B$. Three NANDs. This is De Morgan's rule in action: "NOT (NOT A AND NOT B)" is just "A OR B".

The double-inversion trick

To convert a whole circuit, you do not need to be clever. Follow a fixed routine:

  1. Swap every gate for its NAND version: an AND becomes a NAND followed by a NOT; an OR becomes inverted inputs into a NAND; a NOR gets an extra NOT after it; a NOT becomes a tied NAND.
  2. Where two NOTs sit directly in series, cancel both. A NOT of a NOT is the original signal, so a pair of bubbles on a wire can be removed without changing the function. This is the double-inversion trick, and it is where most of the saving comes from.
  3. Merge duplicates. If two NOTs invert the same signal, one will do.
  4. Count the gates, then the chips.

Pick a circuit. Press Next step to swap the gates, add the double inversions, cancel the pairs and merge duplicates. The truth table is checked after every step to prove the function has not changed. New gates are shaded gold.

Minimising gates and counting ICs

The assignment and the question paper both reward the least number of logic gates possible by converting to NAND equivalents. The 2025 report says many candidates "did not use the least number of logic gates possible by converting to NAND equivalents". The routine above is how you get there.

Gates come in integrated circuits (ICs, which we met in the introduction): small black chips with several identical gates inside. The common 7400 series are in 14-pin packages, with pin 14 to the +supply and pin 7 to 0 V. Three of them matter for NAND work:

ChipContainsGates per chip

Other chips in the family, such as the 7404 (hex NOT) and the 7408, 7432, 7402 and 7486 (quad 2-input AND, OR, NOR and EOR), exist too. Use the data sheet the question gives you. The chip contents and pin numbers above are the usual 74-series facts, but the question's data sheet always overrules them. The question may list different chips, and you must use what it lists.

Two rules govern the count. First, a gate with fewer inputs than the chip offers can still be used by tying the spare inputs to a used one: a 7410 gate with all three inputs joined works as a NOT. Second, a chip is bought whole. Five 2-input NANDs need two 7400s, even though the second chip is almost empty. The cost of a design is the number of chips, not the number of gates, and the 2026 report said that candidates "did not refer to factors such as manufacture and/or cost in relation to the number of Integrated Circuits used" because they counted gates instead. When a question asks about cost or manufacture, say that fewer ICs means a smaller circuit board, fewer soldered joints, less power, and a lower cost.

"If I use a 4-input NAND from a 7420 as a NOT, is that cheating?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "No. Tying the inputs together is exactly how you use a gate that is bigger than you need. But think about the count: a 7420 holds only two gates. If you need three 4-input gates, you need two chips. Always tot up the gates of each size, and then divide into chips."

Worked example 8 · NAND equivalent · three gates

Convert $Q = A \cdot B + C$ (an AND feeding an OR) to NAND gates only, using the least number of gates. How many 7400 chips are needed?

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Swap: the AND becomes NAND plus NOT. The OR becomes a NAND with a NOT on each input. That is five gates before tidying.
  2. The AND's output NOT and the OR's input NOT sit in series on one wire: cancel the pair. What remains is $X = \overline{A\cdot B}$ and $\overline{C}$ going into the final NAND.
  3. $Q = \overline{X \cdot \overline{C}}$. The gates are: $X$ = NAND($A$, $B$); $\overline{C}$ = NAND($C$, $C$); $Q$ = NAND($X$, $\overline{C}$). 3 gates.
  4. All are 2-input NANDs, and a 7400 holds four. So one 7400.

Check by De Morgan: $\overline{\overline{A\cdot B}\cdot\overline{C}} = A\cdot B + C$. ✔

Worked example 9 · ICs · the capstan enable line

The capstan ENABLE signal is $E = A\cdot B\cdot C\cdot\overline{D}$, an AND of four terms (one of them inverted). Build it from NAND gates and state the number of gates and ICs, using the 7400 (quad 2-input) and the 7420 (dual 4-input).

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. Invert $D$ with a NAND with its inputs tied: $\overline{D}$ = NAND($D$, $D$). 1 gate (2-input).
  2. A four-input NAND of $A$, $B$, $C$, $\overline{D}$ gives $X = \overline{A\cdot B\cdot C\cdot\overline{D}}$. 1 gate (4-input).
  3. An AND is a NAND followed by a NOT, so tie the inputs of one more NAND: $E$ = NAND($X$, $X$). 1 gate (2-input).
  4. Total: 3 gates. Two of them are 2-input (one 7400) and one is 4-input (one 7420). So 2 ICs.

Mind the trap: "3 gates" is not the same question as "2 ICs". The 2026 report says candidates counted gates instead of chips. Answer exactly what is asked, and say which chips.

Worked example 10 · EOR · the four-NAND exclusive OR

Show that an EOR gate can be made from four 2-input NANDs.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $X = \overline{A\cdot B}$ (NAND of $A$ and $B$).
  2. $P = \overline{A\cdot X}$ and $R = \overline{B\cdot X}$ (two more NANDs, each taking one input and $X$).
  3. $Q = \overline{P\cdot R}$. Test: $A=B=0$ gives $X=1$, $P=R=1$, so $Q=0$. $A=1, B=0$ gives $X=1$, $P=0$, $R=1$, so $Q=1$. $A=B=1$ gives $X=0$, $P=R=1$, so $Q=0$. ✔ EOR.

One 7400 holds exactly these four gates, which is why this circuit is a favourite.

4 · Programmable control: microcontrollers, flowcharts and pseudo-code PROGRAMMABLE · control routines

Logic gates are wired to do one job for ever. A microcontroller is a tiny computer on a chip that can be given a different job just by changing its program. It is the brain of a washing machine, a car's engine management, and the Signal Locker's lamp panel. For the course you meet it as a block with inputs, a process (the program) and outputs: the systems-approach model again.

Inputs, outputs and pin numbers

The course uses control routines with up to four inputs and four outputs. Digital inputs read 0 or 1 (a switch, a push button, a logic board's output). Analogue inputs read a voltage and turn it into a number using an ADC, an analogue-to-digital converter. In this deck an 8-bit ADC (it outputs a number made of 8 binary digits) gives a reading from 0 to 255, because the biggest 8-bit number is $2^8 - 1 = 255$. A reading of 0 is 0 V and 255 is the full supply voltage. (Check the range in the question: it may use 0 to 1023 for a 10-bit ADC.) Each pin has a number, and a flowchart must name it. In this deck the pins are written in1 to in4 (digital inputs), an1 to an4 (analogue inputs) and out1 to out4 (outputs).

The ADC staircase

Move the slider to change the input voltage. The converter turns it into a whole number: a staircase, not a smooth line. Watch how a small number of steps loses detail.

Flowcharts

A flowchart shows the program as boxes joined by arrows. The standard shapes are: a rounded terminal (START and END), a parallelogram for input and output, a rectangle for a process (a calculation or a delay), and a diamond for a decision with one arrow in and two out, labelled Yes and No. The flow always follows the arrows.

The course documents say that a flowchart needs "sufficient and appropriate detail, such as input and output pin numbers and a delay unit". In other words, write Output 1 = 1, never "turn on the lamp"; write Wait 0.5 s, never "wait".

Pseudo-code and loops

You do not have to write code. You may be asked to read a short program and find faults, and to draw a flowchart. Pseudo-code here is just a way to practise thinking in steps: a simplified, English-like version of a program. A program normally runs from top to bottom and then stops. To keep a control system alive it has to loop back:

  • An infinite loop repeats for ever. It is written with a label at the top and a jump at the bottom: main: ... goto main. A flowchart does the same with an arrow from the last box back to the first box (after START). Almost every control program is an infinite loop, because the controller must go on watching its inputs as long as the power is on. A program without that return runs once and stops.
  • A finite loop repeats a set number of times, controlled by a counter variable: set $n = 0$; each pass add 1; leave the loop when the test fails. "Flash five times" is a finite loop.
  • A delay pauses the program. It must have a unit: wait 0.5 s or wait 200 ms, never just wait 2.

Decisions: logic and arithmetic

A decision tests a condition and branches. A logic decision tests an input pin: if in1 = 1. Conditions may be combined with AND, OR and NOT: if in1 = 1 and in2 = 0. An arithmetic decision compares a number: if an1 > 153, or if n < 5. Analogue readings are usually scaled first. To change a 0 to 255 reading into a temperature from 0 to 100 °C, the program calculates $\text{temp} = \text{an1} \times 100 \div 255$.

🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"What is the difference between a flowchart and pseudo-code? Do I need both?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "They say the same thing in two ways. In the exam you are more likely to be asked to draw a flowchart, or to read a short program and correct it. The flowchart is better for seeing the loops; the pseudo-code is quicker to read line by line. Practise moving between the two until it is automatic."

Worked example 11 · Flowchart · the gun-port alarm

A red lamp (output 1) must flash, and a buzzer (output 2) must sound, whenever a port lid is open (input 1 = 1) and the gun has not been run out (input 2 = 0). Otherwise both are off. Describe the flowchart.

Show solution [5 marks]
  1. START, then an infinite loop that returns to the decision. Mark for the return arrow.
  2. Decision: Input 1 = 1 AND Input 2 = 0? with Yes and No both labelled.
  3. Yes: Output 2 = 1 (buzzer on), Output 1 = 1 (lamp on), Wait 0.5 s, Output 1 = 0, Wait 0.5 s, and back to the decision. Delays carry units.
  4. No: Output 1 = 0 and Output 2 = 0, and back to the decision.

Worked example 12 · Practice: finite loop · flash five times

Write pseudo-code that flashes the lamp on output 1 exactly five times (0.5 s on, 0.5 s off) and then stops.

Show solution [4 marks] 
n = 0
repeat:
  out1 = 1
  wait 0.5 s
  out1 = 0
  wait 0.5 s
  n = n + 1
  if n < 5 then goto repeat
(end of program)
  1. The counter starts at 0 before the loop.
  2. Each pass flashes once and adds 1 to $n$.
  3. The test is n < 5: after five flashes $n = 5$, the test fails, and the program leaves the loop. With n <= 5 there would be six flashes. Test the first and last pass in your head.

Worked example 13 · Analogue scaling · a temperature alarm

A temperature sensor on an1 gives 0 to 255 for 0 to 100 °C. (a) The ADC reads 150. What is the temperature? (b) The alarm must trip above 60 °C. What reading is the threshold?

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. (a) $\text{temp} = 150 \times 100 \div 255 = 58.8\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Multiply first, then divide, and round only at the end.
  2. (b) $\text{reading} = 60 \times 255 \div 100 = 153$. So the test is an1 > 153, or equivalently temp > 60 after scaling.
  3. So 150 is just below the trip point, as the answer to (a) shows.

5 · Pulse-width modulation PROGRAMMABLE · motor control

A microcontroller pin is digital: it can be fully ON (5 V) or fully OFF (0 V), and nothing in between. How, then, can it run a motor slowly? By switching the motor on and off so fast that the motor sees only the average. This is pulse-width modulation (PWM). The ship's lamp dimmers in the Signal Locker work the same way.

Duty cycle and average voltage

A PWM signal repeats every period $T$. Within each period it is ON for a time $t_{on}$. The fraction of the period it is on is the duty cycle $D$, quoted as a percentage:

$$D = \frac{t_{on}}{T} \qquad V_{avg} = D \times V_s \qquad f = \frac{1}{T}$$

A 50 % duty cycle on a 12 V supply gives an average of 6 V; 25 % gives 3 V; 100 % is permanently on. The pulses must be fast enough that the motor's inertia smooths them: a frequency from hundreds of hertz up to tens of kilohertz. (None of the three PWM relations, $D = t_{on}/T$, $V_{avg} = D \times V_s$ and $f = 1/T$, is in the Higher data booklet, which gives only the symbol $f$ and the unit hertz, so learn all three.)

PWM scope: duty cycle, average voltage and motor speed

Move the duty slider and watch the scope trace, the average-voltage line and the spinning motor. Then try Describe the speed to see how an exam answer is built.

The motor driver

A microcontroller pin supplies only a few milliamps, far too little for a motor. The pin therefore drives the base (or gate) of a transistor, and the transistor switches the motor current from the supply. A diode connected across the motor (a flyback diode) protects the transistor from the voltage spike when the motor current is switched off.

Why PWM, not a series resistor?

You could slow a motor with a resistor in series with it. It works, but the resistor wastes power: the voltage the motor does not get is dropped across the resistor, and the current through it makes heat. At the same current, the fraction of the supply power wasted is the fraction of the voltage dropped. PWM switches the transistor fully on or fully off, so the transistor itself dissipates almost nothing, and nearly all the power drawn from the supply goes to the motor.

🪔 Aside: dimming a redstone lamp

In Minecraft you can imitate PWM with a redstone clock that pulses a lamp faster than you can see: a longer pulse makes the lamp seem brighter. Real PWM is the same idea at hundreds of hertz up to tens of kilohertz.

Describing the speed of the motor

The 2026 report (in an analogue circuit question) said that few candidates "correctly described the speed of the motor as the voltage dropped". The same skill applies to PWM. An answer needs three linked statements: the quantity that changes (the duty cycle, or the average voltage), the effect on the current or torque, and the effect on the speed of the motor, with direction. For example: as the duty cycle falls, the average voltage across the motor falls, so less current flows and less torque is produced, so the motor slows down; at a very low duty cycle the torque cannot overcome friction and the motor stops.

Worked example 14 · Average voltage

A 12 V supply drives a motor through a PWM output at a duty cycle of 60 %. Calculate the average voltage across the motor.

Show solution [2 marks]
  1. $V_{avg} = D \times V_s = 0.60 \times 12$.
  2. $V_{avg} = 7.2\ \text{V}$. Unit needed.

Worked example 15 · Period and duty

A scope shows a PWM signal that is high for 2 ms and has a frequency of 250 Hz. Calculate the period and the duty cycle. Separately: a 9 V supply gives an average of 4.5 V; what is the duty cycle?

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $T = 1/f = 1/250 = 4\ \text{ms}$.
  2. $D = t_{on}/T = 2/4 = 50\ \%$.
  3. Separately: $D = V_{avg}/V_s = 4.5/9 = 50\ \%$, so on a 250 Hz signal $t_{on} = 2$ ms. Both routes agree.

Worked example 16 · Program · scaling a dial to a duty cycle

A speed dial on an1 reads 0 to 255. The program calculates duty = an1 × 100 ÷ 255 and sends it to the PWM on output 1 from a 12 V supply. The dial reads 192. Find the duty cycle and the average voltage.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $\text{duty} = 192 \times 100 \div 255 = 75.3\ \%$.
  2. $V_{avg} = 0.753 \times 12 = 9.04\ \text{V}$.
  3. Keep the unrounded duty in the second step. The 2024 report notes that rounding intermediate answers made some final answers wrong.

Worked example 17 · Efficiency · PWM against a series resistor

A motor needs 7.2 V at 0.50 A from a 12 V supply. (a) Find the power wasted if a series resistor is used. (b) Compare with PWM.

Show solution [4 marks]
  1. The resistor drops $12 - 7.2 = 4.8$ V.
  2. Power wasted: $P = VI = 4.8 \times 0.50 = 2.4$ W. The supply provides $12 \times 0.50 = 6.0$ W, so $2.4/6.0 = 40\ \%$ is wasted as heat.
  3. With PWM at $D = 7.2/12 = 60\ \%$ the transistor is either fully on or fully off, so it wastes almost nothing (a small switching loss). Almost all of the supply power is delivered to the motor.
  4. PWM is therefore more efficient, and the circuit runs cooler.

Worked example 18 · Describe · the speed as the voltage drops

The duty cycle is reduced from 80 % to 40 %. Describe what happens to the motor.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. The average voltage across the motor halves, because $V_{avg} = D \times V_s$.
  2. Less current flows and less torque is produced, so the motor slows down.
  3. The motor runs slower, in steady running at about half its previous speed, and below a low duty cycle it stops because the torque cannot overcome friction.

Say "slows down" and "average voltage falls", in that order, and give the cause between them.

6 · Putting it together: an exam-style question DIGITAL + PROGRAMMABLE

Exam questions in this area join the chapters together. A specification becomes a truth table, then an expression, then a NAND circuit, and the output of that circuit is read by a microcontroller program that drives a motor. Here is a question in that style, set on the capstan of HMS Ratchet. Try it on paper before you open the solution.

🎖 The capstan interlock

The capstan motor may run only if: the pawls are lifted clear ($A$ = 1), the guard rail is in place ($B$ = 1), and the heave button is pressed ($C$ = 1), and not if the emergency stop is pressed ($D$ = 1). When the motor runs, a running lamp is on and the motor speed is set by a speed dial.

Worked example 19 · Exam style · the capstan interlock

(a) State the Boolean expression for the ENABLE signal $E$. (b) Draw the logic diagram. (c) Convert it to NAND gates and say how many gates and ICs are needed. (d) ENABLE is connected to input 1 of a microcontroller. The speed dial is on analogue input 1 (0 to 255), the motor is on output 1 and the lamp on output 2. Draw a flowchart that runs the motor with PWM at a duty cycle of dial × 100 ÷ 255 % while ENABLE is 1 and switches both outputs off otherwise.

Show solution [13 marks]
  1. (a) $E = A \cdot B \cdot C \cdot \overline{D}$. 1 mark. Only one row of the 16-row truth table, 1110, gives $E = 1$.
  2. (b) One 4-input AND, with $D$ passing through a NOT. 2 marks: AND with four inputs; NOT on $D$.
  3. (c) NAND version: $\overline{D}$ from a tied NAND, a 4-input NAND of $A, B, C, \overline{D}$, and a tied NAND to restore the AND. 3 gates, 2 ICs (one 7400 for the two 2-input gates and one 7420 for the 4-input gate). 4 marks including the correct reasoning on ICs: a gate count alone does not answer "how many ICs".
  4. (d) Flowchart: START, then a loop. Decision "Input 1 = 1?" Yes: duty = an1 × 100 ÷ 255, then PWM output 1 = duty and output 2 = 1. No: PWM output 1 = 0 and output 2 = 0. Both paths return to the decision. 6 marks. They go for the loop, correct pin numbers, the decision, the scaling, both branches and the lamp.

The capstan board: logic plus microcontroller

Flip the four switches and watch the NAND logic board decide ENABLE. Then turn the speed dial and see what the microcontroller does with it. The flowchart shows which boxes ran.

Worked example 20 · Calculation · the dial

The dial reads 191 and the supply is 12 V. Find the average voltage across the capstan motor.

Show solution [3 marks]
  1. $D = 191 \times 100 \div 255 = 74.9\ \%$.
  2. $V_{avg} = 0.749 \times 12 = 8.99\ \text{V}$.
  3. If $E = 0$ the answer would be 0 V, whatever the dial says: the logic board has the final say. This shows why the NAND board and the program belong to one system.

Technique for descriptive answers

Not every mark is a calculation. The 2024 report said "Questions requiring a detailed written response proved to be challenging". Some rules for descriptions in this topic:

  • Use the words of the question and of the component: "duty cycle", "average voltage", "the output goes high", "the loop returns to the start".
  • Give the cause, the link, and the effect, in that order: because the duty falls, so the average voltage falls, so the motor slows.
  • For a test, write the input you set, the output you expect, and what you saw. Never write only "it worked".

Mistakes markers see

These are drawn from the course reports (the quotations) and from the checks in this deck.

  • Counting gates when the question asks for ICs. The 2026 report: candidates "did not refer to factors such as manufacture and/or cost in relation to the number of Integrated Circuits used".
  • Not minimising. The 2025 report: many candidates "did not use the least number of logic gates possible by converting to NAND equivalents".
  • Claiming everything works. The 2026 report: some "stated that all specification points were met, when the performance of their amended flowchart did not provide evidence to support this".
  • A flowchart with no pin numbers or no delay unit, or with no way back to the start.
  • A non-descriptive answer for the motor speed: few "correctly described the speed of the motor as the voltage dropped" (2026, an analogue circuit question; the same skill applies to PWM).
  • Rounding too early, which can make a final answer wrong (2024).
⚠ Exam lookout · the final check

Before you hand in a digital answer, ask four questions. Does my truth table have every row, in order? Does my expression match the table? Is my NAND count right for gates and for chips? Does my flowchart have pin numbers, units and a loop?

  • take a specification through truth table, expression, logic diagram and NAND equivalent;
  • count gates and ICs from a data sheet;
  • connect a logic output to a microcontroller flowchart that uses PWM and scaling;
  • write a descriptive answer with cause, link and effect.
