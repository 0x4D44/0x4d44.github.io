Systems, energy & the impact of engineering

Before you can design a circuit, a gearbox or a truss, you need the big picture: what engineers actually do, how to break a problem into a system, how a machine corrects its own mistakes, and how to count energy honestly. This deck is the foundation for everything else, and it is worth 10 to 17 marks in the question paper (the spec's range for this theme). It also feeds the calculation habits you will use in every other deck.

What this deck covers. Engineering roles and disciplines; the systems approach and control diagrams; negative feedback, closed-loop, two-state and proportional control; energy audits, the energy formulae and efficiency; manipulating and combining formulae; the social, economic and environmental impacts of engineering, sustainability and emerging technologies. A table at the end maps every chapter to the course specification.

1 · What engineers do: disciplines, phases and teamwork ROLES & DISCIPLINES

Engineering is using science and mathematics to solve practical problems, then building and testing the solution so that it works safely, affordably and reliably. An engineer is the person who takes responsibility for making that happen. Notice the three constraints hiding in that sentence: safe, affordable, reliable. Engineering is rarely about the perfect answer. It is about the best answer that can actually be built, within a budget, on time, and without hurting anyone.

The course asks about the role of the professional engineer within a project, including communication and team working, and the skills and specialist knowledge required. Let us take those in turn.

Engineering disciplines

Nobody can be expert in everything, so engineering is split into disciplines. Each discipline has its own specialist knowledge (what the engineer knows) and its own typical tasks (what the engineer does). Big projects need several disciplines working together, because real machines mix mechanics, electricity, structures and software.

The line between disciplines is blurred in practice. A mechatronics engineer works across mechanical, electronic and software work at once; a modern robot arm needs all three. When an exam question names a discipline, answer from that discipline's knowledge: ask yourself "what does a civil engineer know that nobody else on the team does?"

The phases of a project

A project does not go straight from idea to finished product. Engineers work through phases. Different organisations name them differently, but the logic is always the same: understand the need, work out a solution, make it, prove it works, then keep it working.

Communication and team working

No engineer works alone on a large project. A bridge needs civil engineers for the structure, mechanical engineers for any moving span, electrical engineers for the lighting and drives, and software engineers for the control system. Each depends on the others' work, so the places where one discipline meets another (the interfaces) are where mistakes most often creep in.

Engineers communicate through: drawings and models (the shared picture of what will be built), specifications (what it must achieve), reports and test records (evidence that it does), meetings (to settle disagreements early), and written and spoken updates to clients who are not engineers and need plain language. Good team working means agreeing roles, sharing information promptly, respecting each other's expertise, meeting deadlines others depend on, and being honest about problems instead of hiding them.

Why does this matter for results? A mechanical engineer who changes the size of a motor without telling the electrical engineer may leave the cables too thin for the new current. A civil engineer who is not told about a heavier machine may under-design the floor beneath it. Communication prevents expensive, sometimes dangerous, errors.

Skills and specialist knowledge

Two different things are sampled. Specialist knowledge is the discipline's content: a civil engineer's knowledge of how a beam bends, an electronic engineer's knowledge of how a transistor switches. Skills are the abilities used on any project: problem solving, mathematics, using CAD and simulation software, drawing and reading drawings, practical building and testing, communication, planning and managing time and money, and working safely.

How to write a Higher answer about an engineer's role

Questions in this area ask you to describe or explain a role in a named project. The 2026 course report records that few candidates "described two roles of a civil engineer in the development phase" and that few "described two examples of how a mechanical engineer may apply mathematics in the design of the drive system". The pattern is clear: when a question asks for two, many candidates give one, or give the same one twice in different words. Use this recipe for every role mark:

📘 The role + how/why recipe
  1. Role: state a specific task this discipline does in the project, in this phase.
  2. How or why: say how they do it (a method, a calculation, a tool) or why it matters (what goes wrong without it).
  3. Two means two: if the question says "two roles", write two separate, clearly different paragraphs. Number them.
  4. Use the context: name parts of the actual project. "Checks that the pier foundations can support the turbine tower" beats "makes sure it is safe".

A bare job title earns nothing ("the civil engineer designs the structure" is a role with no how/why and no context). Compare the weak and strong versions in the widget below, which builds role answers for four projects.

2 · The systems approach: sub-systems, block diagrams and control diagrams SYSTEMS APPROACH

Engineers tame complicated machines with one idea: do not look at the whole thing at once. Draw a boundary round the part you care about, and ask three questions. What goes in? What happens to it? What comes out? That is the systems approach.

📘 System, sub-system, input, process, output
  • A system is a collection of parts that work together to do a job. It has a boundary: what is inside is the system, and what is outside is its surroundings.
  • A sub-system is a smaller system inside a bigger one, with a job of its own. A sub-system can itself contain smaller sub-systems.
  • An input is what enters the system (energy, material, information or a signal). The process is what the system does to it. The output is what leaves.

The great wheel on the quarterdeck is a good example. Input: the helmsman turns the wheel through some angle. Process: the steering gear converts that movement into force on the rudder. Output: the rudder angle. But the steering gear is made of smaller parts (wheel, linkage, hydraulic pump, rudder) and each of those is a sub-system when you zoom in.

Block (system) diagrams

A block diagram (also called a system diagram) shows a system as labelled boxes joined by arrows. Each box is a sub-system and states what it does. Each arrow shows what passes between the boxes, and the arrow head shows the direction. It deliberately leaves out how each box works inside, so that you can see the structure.

  • Draw the flow from left (inputs) to right (outputs), and label the arrows if the thing carried is not obvious (for example "water level signal").
  • Name each block by its function ("detects water level", "switches pump") rather than only by a part ("float").
  • Keep one box for one job. If a box does two things, split it.

Complex systems

A complex system has several sub-systems that work together, and often interact. The whole ship is one: propulsion, steering, electrical supply, fire-fighting and bilge pumping are each a system, and each is part of the ship system. Engineers draw the big picture first, then a separate diagram for each sub-system, then for the sub-systems inside those. This is called working top-down, and it lets a team share out the work: each discipline takes the sub-systems it is expert in, agreeing the arrows between them.

Control diagrams

Some systems need to control something: keep a temperature, a water level or a heading at a wanted value. A control diagram is a block diagram that shows how. The basic form is still input → process → output, but each block now has a name from control engineering.

The stove in the captain's cabin is fitted with a thermostat. The captain sets the temperature he wants. A temperature sensor measures the cabin temperature. 3 · Feedback and control: error, two-state and proportional FEEDBACK & CONTROL

Captain Reid orders the helmsman to hold a heading of 090°. A cross-wind pushes the bow round. A good helmsman notices the compass drifting, compares it with 090°, and turns the wheel to bring it back. That sequence (measure, compare, correct) is exactly what a closed-loop control system does automatically. This chapter explains how it works and why it works better than just setting the wheel and hoping.

Open loop and closed loop

Open loop

The controller acts on the input only. It never checks the result.

  • Simple and cheap; nothing to go wrong in a feedback path.
  • Cannot correct for disturbances or for its own inaccuracy.
  • Fine when the process is predictable: a timer on a lamp, a fixed fuse on a signal gun.

Closed loop

The output is measured and fed back so that the controller acts on the error.

  • Corrects for disturbances automatically.
  • Needs a sensor and a comparator, so costs more and is more complex.
  • Can become unstable if badly designed (it can oscillate).

Error and error detection

📘 Error

$$\text{error} = \text{desired value} - \text{actual value}$$

The error is the gap between what we want and what we have. Its sign tells you which way to correct: with desired 090° and actual 084° the error is $+6^\circ$ (turn towards a larger heading); if actual is 096° the error is $-6^\circ$ (turn the other way). When the error is zero, the controller has nothing to do.

The block that works out the error is the comparator or error detector (drawn as a circle with a $+$ and a $-$, a summing junction). In an electronic controller it is built from op-amps: a comparator circuit gives a two-state answer, "above or below the reference", and a difference amplifier gives an output proportional to the difference between two voltages, $V_o = \frac{R_f}{R_i}(V_2 - V_1)$. You will build both in the analogue electronics deck; for now, remember that "error detection" is the subtraction in the feedback loop.

The role of negative feedback

Feedback is called negative when the fed-back signal is subtracted from the input at the comparator, so a rise in the output reduces the drive that caused it. Negative feedback is self-correcting: if the output goes too high, the error goes negative and the controller pushes the output back down; if it goes too low, the opposite happens. That is why it reduces the effect of disturbances and keeps the output near the desired value.

Positive feedback adds the fed-back signal to the input, so a rise in the output increases the drive that caused it. The output runs away from the starting value until something limits it (a microphone placed next to its own loudspeaker squeals; a snowball rolling down a slope grows faster as it grows). Positive feedback is not useful for holding a value steady, but engineers use it deliberately where a fast, decisive change or a latched state is wanted, for example in latches and oscillators.

Two-state control

The simplest closed-loop controller has only two outputs: fully on or fully off. A cabin thermostat is the classic example. When the temperature falls below the lower limit the stove goes on; when it rises above the upper limit the stove goes off.

Why does the temperature never sit exactly at the target? Because of delay. The air heats up after the stove goes on, and keeps heating a little after it goes off. The temperature overshoots the upper limit, then cools and undershoots the lower limit. The result is a continual swing called hunting (or cycling). A gap between the switch-on and switch-off values (the switching band or hysteresis) stops the stove clicking on and off many times a second, but makes the swing larger.

  • Advantages: simple, cheap, robust, and easy to design and set up; efficient for slow processes where small swings do not matter.
  • Disadvantages: the output is never steady, only swinging about the target; the actuator is slammed fully on and off, so it wears out faster; a narrow band means rapid switching, a wide one means large swings.

Proportional control

A proportional controller makes the output proportional to the error:

$$\text{controller output} = K_p \times \text{error}$$

where $K_p$ is the gain. A big error gets a big correction, a small error a small one, and as the error shrinks the correction fades smoothly. This avoids the violent on-off action and (for moderate gain) settles without hunting.

There is a catch called steady-state offset. Suppose a steady cross-wind needs a constant 6° of rudder to hold the ship on course. A proportional controller only gives rudder when there is an error, so a rudder of 6° requires an error of $6 \div K_p$ degrees. The ship therefore settles slightly off the desired heading and stays there. The offset is $$\text{offset} = \frac{\text{output needed to cancel the disturbance}}{K_p}.$$ Raising the gain shrinks the offset, but a high gain over-reacts: the output overshoots and the loop starts to oscillate. Good control is a compromise, and more advanced controllers (which you do not need for Higher) add extra terms to remove the offset.

Worked example 4 · Calculation · error and proportional output

A boiler in the galley has a proportional pressure controller. The desired pressure is 6.0 bar and the pressure sensor reads 5.2 bar. The controller opens the fuel valve by 25 % for each 1.0 bar of error. Calculate the error and the valve opening. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. error $= 6.0 - 5.2$ Error is desired minus actual.
  2. error $= 0.8$ bar Positive: the pressure is too low and needs more fuel.
  3. valve opening $= 25 \times 0.8 = 20\ \%$ Gain 25 % per bar times the error. State the unit.

Common slip. Subtracting the other way round (actual minus desired) gives $-0.8$ bar, which would seem to close the valve. Always desired minus actual.

Worked example 5 · Description · a two-state situation

The cabin stove is switched on when the temperature falls to 18 °C and switched off when it rises to 22 °C. Explain why the cabin temperature is not steady, and state one advantage of this type of control. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. This is two-state control: the stove is either fully on or fully off. Identify the type, using the situation.
  2. After the stove switches off at 22 °C the temperature keeps rising slightly, then falls to 18 °C; after switching on at 18 °C it keeps falling slightly before it rises. The temperature therefore swings (hunts) between about 18 °C and 22 °C and is never constant. The reason: lag in the heating, plus the switching band. Use the 18 and 22 from the question.
  3. Advantage: simple and cheap, since the thermostat only needs to switch on and off. One clear advantage, in a few words.

Worked example 6 · Calculation · steady-state offset

The autopilot is a proportional controller with a gain of 1.5° of rudder per degree of heading error. The desired heading is 090°. (a) The actual heading is 084°. Calculate the rudder angle. [2] (b) A steady cross-wind means that 6° of rudder is needed to hold a steady course. Calculate the heading error when the ship settles. [2] (c) The gain is doubled. State the effect on the heading error and one possible disadvantage. [2]

Show solution [6 marks, typical allocation]
  1. (a) error $= 090 - 084 = 6^\circ$, so rudder $= 1.5 \times 6 = 9^\circ$ Error first, then multiply by the gain.
  2. (b) the controller must output 6°, so error $= 6 \div 1.5$ Rearrange output $= K_p \times$ error.
  3. error $= 4^\circ$ The ship settles 4° off course: steady-state offset.
  4. (c) the error halves, to $6 \div 3.0 = 2^\circ$ Offset is inversely proportional to gain.
  5. a disadvantage of high gain: the rudder over-reacts, so the heading overshoots and may oscillate (become unstable). The trade-off in a sentence.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If more gain shrinks the offset, why not make the gain enormous and be done with it?"

Flight Lieutenant Murray: "Because the ship takes time to respond. A huge gain means a tiny error produces a big rudder, and by the time the ship has turned, it has gone past the target, so the controller swings the rudder hard the other way. Try it on the widget: push the gain to the top and watch the heading swing. Control is a trade between a small offset and a calm response."

4 · Energy and efficiency: audits, formulae and combining them ENERGY & EFFICIENCY

Every machine on HMS Ratchet takes energy in, does something useful with some of it, and lets the rest escape. Engineers account for that energy the way a purser accounts for rations: carefully, and to the last joule. This chapter gives you the vocabulary, the formulae from the Data Booklet and, most importantly, the habit of combining them to solve a problem.

Energy, power and conversion

📘 Energy and power

Energy is the capacity to do work. It is measured in joules (J). Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another or transferred. Power is the rate at which energy is transferred or converted. It is measured in watts (W), where $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J s}^{-1}$.

$$P = \frac{E}{t}$$

A 60 W lantern converts 60 joules of electrical energy every second.

Energy comes in many forms: chemical (fuel, batteries, food), electrical, kinetic (moving objects), gravitational potential (height), elastic or strain (a stretched rope, a compressed spring), thermal (heat), light, sound and nuclear. Machines are chains of conversions.

Energy audits and efficiency

An energy audit lists, for a device or system: the energy (or power) input; the useful output; the wasted output (the losses); and the efficiency. Because energy is conserved, the outputs always add up to the input: $$E_{in} = E_{useful} + E_{wasted}.$$ Efficiency says what fraction of the input ends up as useful output:

$$\eta = \frac{E_{out}}{E_{in}} = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}$$

($\eta$ is the Greek letter "eta".) Both forms are in the Data Booklet. The power form works because over the same time $t$, $E = Pt$ on both top and bottom, and the $t$ cancels. Efficiency has no unit. Give it as a decimal (0.76) or a percentage (76 %), and make sure it is never above 1 (100 %): a machine cannot give out more energy than it takes in.

The energy formulae

The Data Booklet gives you these. You are not asked to remember them, but you must know which one to pick and what each symbol means.

Work done

$$E_w = Fd$$

$F$ in newtons (N), distance $d$ in metres (m) in the direction of the force. Energy in J.

Kinetic energy

$$E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2$$

Mass $m$ in kg, speed $v$ in m s⁻¹. Note the square: double the speed, four times the energy.

Gravitational potential energy

$$E_p = mgh$$

$g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$, $h$ is the height change in metres. Energy in J.

Electrical energy

$$E_e = VIt$$

Voltage $V$ in volts, current $I$ in amperes, time $t$ in seconds.

Heat energy

$$E_h = cm\Delta T$$

Specific heat capacity $c$ (water: 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), mass $m$ in kg, temperature change $\Delta T$ in K or °C (the same size).

Power

$$P = \frac{E}{t}$$

Also $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $P = V^2/R$ for electrical power, and $P = Fv$ for mechanical power.

Notice that $E_w = Fd$ is the bridge between forces and energy: lifting an object of weight $mg$ through a height $h$ requires work $Fd = mg \times h$, which is exactly $E_p$. And a force acting through a distance to speed something up, or to slow it with friction, links to $E_k$ and $E_h$.

Choose a formula, move the sliders, and read the substitution and the answer. The bar compares the answer with the energy in a cannonball, a lifted anchor, a kettle boil and so on.

Conservation of energy in motion

When nothing wastes energy, kinetic and potential energy simply swap. A lantern swinging at the end of a rope is highest (all $E_p$, no $E_k$) at the ends of its swing, and fastest (all $E_k$, minimum $E_p$) at the bottom. With friction in the pivot and air resistance, some of the energy becomes heat $E_h$ on every swing, and the swings die away. The total never changes.

The swinging lantern: energy changes form but is never lost

Release the lantern from any angle. The bars show potential, kinetic and heat energy. With no friction the potential and kinetic bars always add to the same total; with friction, the heat bar grows and the swing dies away.

Combining formulae: the method

Most Higher energy questions chain two or more formulae. The route is always the same.

  1. Draw the energy story. What form is the energy in at the start, and at the end? Name the conversions.
  2. Calculate the energy (or power) of the useful stage with the formula that fits it ($E_p$, $E_k$, $E_h$, $E_w$ ...).
  3. Link to the input with efficiency, $E_e = VIt$, or $P = E/t$.
  4. Rearrange before you substitute, and keep full calculator values until the last line.
  5. Check units (J, W, s, kg, m) and say what your answer means.

Worked example 7 · Calculation · the chain pump

The chain pump lifts 2400 kg of water through a height of 3.5 m in 60 s. The electric motor driving it takes an input power of 1.8 kW. Calculate (a) the useful output power of the pump and (b) the efficiency of the system. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $E_p = mgh = 2400 \times 9.8 \times 3.5$ The useful output is the gain in potential energy of the water.
  2. $E_p = 82\,320\ \text{J}$, so $P_{out} = E/t = 82\,320 \div 60 = 1372\ \text{W}$ Power is energy per second. Keep the full value: 1372 W.
  3. $\eta = P_{out} \div P_{in} = 1372 \div 1800$ Convert 1.8 kW to 1800 W first so both powers are in the same unit.
  4. $\eta = 0.762$ (76.2 %) No unit. Under 1, as it must be.

Common slip. Leaving the input as 1.8 (kW) against an output in W, which makes the efficiency 1000 times too small.

Worked example 8 · Calculation · regenerative braking

An electric launch of mass 1800 kg is travelling at 6.0 m s⁻¹ and brakes to rest. A regenerative braking system converts 40 % of its kinetic energy into electrical energy stored in a battery. (a) Calculate the energy stored in the battery. [3] (b) For how long could this energy power a 25 W lamp? [2] (c) State what happens to the rest of the kinetic energy. [1]

Show solution [6 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1800 \times 6.0^2 = 32\,400\ \text{J}$ Square the speed before multiplying.
  2. energy stored $= 0.40 \times 32\,400 = 12\,960\ \text{J}$ 40 % is a factor of 0.40 of the kinetic energy: the efficiency of the recovery.
  3. $t = E/P = 12\,960 \div 25 = 518.4\ \text{s}$, about 520 s (8.6 minutes) Rearranged from $P = E/t$. Unit: seconds.
  4. The remaining 60 % becomes heat (in the brakes, motor, wiring and surroundings). Energy is conserved: it is not lost, but wasted as heat.

Worked example 9 · Calculation · electric heater and water

An electric heater of power 3.0 kW heats 2.0 kg of water from 15 °C to 65 °C. 85 % of the electrical energy supplied becomes heat in the water. (Specific heat capacity of water = 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.) (a) Calculate the energy gained by the water. [2] (b) Calculate the electrical energy supplied. [2] (c) Calculate how long the heater is switched on. [1]

Show solution [5 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\Delta T = 65 - 15 = 50\ \text{K}$ and $E_h = cm\Delta T = 4180 \times 2.0 \times 50 = 418\,000\ \text{J}$ Heat gained by the water is the useful output.
  2. $E_{in} = E_{out} \div \eta = 418\,000 \div 0.85 = 491\,765\ \text{J}$ (4.9 × 10⁵ J) Rearranged from $\eta = E_{out}/E_{in}$.
  3. $t = E/P = 491\,765 \div 3000 = 164\ \text{s}$ $E_e = Pt$ with $P = VI$ folded in. Use 3000 W, not 3.0.

Common slip. Using the input energy 491 765 J in $E_h = cm\Delta T$ to find a temperature rise. The water only gets the useful part.

Worked example 10 · Calculation · raising the anchor

The capstan raises an anchor and chain of total mass 1400 kg through 6.0 m in 80 s. The electric motor takes 1.8 kW. Calculate (a) the work done on the anchor, (b) the useful output power, (c) the efficiency, and (d) the energy wasted. [6]

Show solution [6 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $F = mg = 1400 \times 9.8 = 13\,720\ \text{N}$ The force needed to lift the load at steady speed equals its weight.
  2. $E_w = Fd = 13\,720 \times 6.0 = 82\,320\ \text{J}$ Work done is force times distance moved.
  3. $P_{out} = E/t = 82\,320 \div 80 = 1029\ \text{W}$ The useful power.
  4. $\eta = 1029 \div 1800 = 0.572$ (57.2 %) Efficiency from the powers.
  5. $E_{in} = P_{in}t = 1800 \times 80 = 144\,000\ \text{J}$ Energy the motor takes in.
  6. $E_{wasted} = 144\,000 - 82\,320 = 61\,680\ \text{J}$ Input minus useful output: friction, motor heating.

Worked example 11 · Calculation · conservation of energy

A cannonball falls freely from the main yard, 12 m above the deck. Ignoring air resistance, calculate its speed on reaching the deck. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. all $E_p$ becomes $E_k$: $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$ Equate the two energies.
  2. the mass cancels: $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 12}$ The answer does not depend on the mass, which is why it was not given.
  3. $v = 15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (15.3 m s⁻¹ before rounding) Two or three significant figures; units m s⁻¹.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"The energy 'wasted' by the motor: where does it go? Is it destroyed?"

Master Shipwright Kerr: "Never destroyed. It becomes heat in the motor windings and bearings, a little sound and vibration, and spreads into the air of the ship. That energy still exists, but it is spread so thin and so cool that you cannot use it. We call it wasted because it is no longer useful, not because it has vanished."

💡 Exam tip

Braking is an $E_k$ to $E_h$ conversion: the brake pads and discs warm up. A question that tells you how much of the kinetic energy appears as heat in a given mass of metal is asking you to equate $\tfrac12 mv^2$ (times the fraction) with $cm\Delta T$. Watch for two different masses: the moving vehicle and the metal being heated.

✔ Logbook: you must be able to…
  • name forms of energy and describe conversions; state that energy is conserved;
  • complete an energy audit: inputs, useful outputs, wasted outputs, efficiency;
  • use $E_w = Fd$, $P = E/t$, $E_k$, $E_p$, $E_e = VIt$ and $E_h = cm\Delta T$ with correct units;
  • calculate efficiency from energies or powers, and use it backwards to find the input;
  • combine two or three of these formulae to solve a multi-stage problem.

5 · Calculation craft: formulae, units, prefixes and rounding CALCULATIONS

Marks are lost in engineering calculations far more often by small slips than by not knowing the physics: a unit left unconverted, a formula rearranged wrongly, an answer rounded too early. The specification asks you to be able to manipulate and combine given formulae, and this chapter makes that a reliable routine. The same routine serves every other deck.

Transposing a formula

To transpose (rearrange) a formula, do the same thing to both sides until the quantity you want stands alone. Think of a balance: what you do to one pan, you do to the other.

The last row comes from the analogue deck. The 2024 course report says that many candidates found it "challenging to manipulate the voltage divider formula $V_1/V_2 = R_1/R_2$ to find the value of $R_1$". It is the same skill as every other row: multiply both sides by $R_2$ to leave $R_1$ alone.

Combining formulae

When one stage of a problem feeds the next, you can either calculate a number at each stage, or substitute one formula into another and calculate once. The second is often shorter and always avoids rounding trouble. For the pump, with efficiency $\eta = \dfrac{mgh}{Pt}$ you can find the mass moved in one step. For electrical power, $P = VI$ and $V = IR$ combine into $P = I^2R$.

💡 Algebra first, numbers last

Rearrange the formula (or combined formula) for the unknown before you put any numbers in. You then do a single calculation on the calculator and there is no intermediate value to round.

Units and decimal prefixes

Formulae only work when every quantity is in its base unit: kilograms, metres, seconds, newtons, volts, amperes, ohms. Whenever you see a prefix, a minute, a millimetre or a rev/min, convert before you substitute.

Traps worth memorising:

  • Area converts by the square of the length factor: $1\ \text{mm}^2 = (10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$, not $10^{-3}$. So $50\ \text{mm}^2 = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$.
  • Rotational speed: formulae with $n$ want revolutions per second. Divide rev/min by 60: $47\ \text{rev/min} = 0.783\ \text{rev/s}$.
  • Time: minutes and hours must be turned into seconds for $E = Pt$ and $E_e = VIt$.
  • Stress: $1\ \text{N mm}^{-2} = 1\ \text{MPa} = 10^6\ \text{N m}^{-2}$. Keep $F$ in N and area in mm² for answers in N mm⁻², or in m² for pascals; never mix.
  • The Data Booklet prints decimal points as raised dots ("9·8", "0·7"). They are ordinary decimal points.

To check a conversion, ask: should the number get bigger or smaller? Going from mm to m (a bigger unit) makes the number smaller. Going from kN to N (a smaller unit) makes the number bigger.

The conversion drill: units and prefixes

Type the converted value as an ordinary number (no units, no powers of ten: write 0.0047, not 4.7×10⁻³). The checker accepts small rounding differences. Try to get a streak of five.

Significant figures, and never rounding too early

A significant figure is a digit that carries information. Leading zeros do not count (0.0047 has two), trailing zeros after a decimal point do (4.70 has three). Round the final answer to the number of significant figures the question asks for; the rounding instructions are printed on the front cover of the question paper. If no instruction applies, three significant figures is a sensible, safe choice, and it is never worth giving more than the data justify.

⚠ Exam lookout · from the 2024 and 2026 course reports

The 2024 report says: "Some candidates rounded intermediate calculated answers, which made their final answer incorrect." The 2026 report advises candidates to "not round intermediate values during calculation and only apply rounding to the final answer". The 2024 report also notes that "many candidates consistently used the π button on their calculators". Use the π key, never 3.14 or 22/7, unless the question tells you to.

Why does early rounding hurt? Every rounded value carries a small error, and later steps can magnify it: squaring doubles the percentage error, dividing by a number close to another can inflate it, and a sum of several small errors can push the final digit across a rounding boundary. The widget lets you see this happen on a real chain of calculations.

The honest purser: what rounding too early costs

Pick a problem. The left column keeps every calculator digit; the right column rounds each intermediate value to the number of significant figures you choose (and, optionally, uses 3.14 for π). Compare the final answers.

Use your calculator's memory: store each answer (the Ans key or a letter memory) and recall it, rather than retyping a rounded figure. When you write working down, you may write the intermediate value to a few figures for the marker to read, but go on using the full value on the calculator.

Worked example 12 · Calculation · rearranging first

A hoist on the gun deck lifts a load through 5.0 m in 40 s. The motor input power is 3.0 kW and the system is 65 % efficient. Calculate the mass of the load. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\eta = \dfrac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} = \dfrac{mgh/t}{P_{in}}$ Combine: $P_{out} = E_p/t = mgh/t$.
  2. $m = \dfrac{\eta P_{in} t}{gh}$ Transpose for $m$ before any numbers go in.
  3. $m = \dfrac{0.65 \times 3000 \times 40}{9.8 \times 5.0}$ $P_{in}$ in W; $\eta$ as a decimal.
  4. $m = 1591.8\ldots = 1590\ \text{kg}$ (3 s.f.) One calculation, one rounding, unit kg.

Worked example 13 · Calculation · prefixes and time

A signal lamp draws 250 mA from a 24 V supply for 45 minutes. Calculate the electrical energy used, in kilojoules. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $I = 250 \times 10^{-3} = 0.250\ \text{A}$ and $t = 45 \times 60 = 2700\ \text{s}$ Convert mA to A and minutes to seconds before substituting.
  2. $E_e = VIt = 24 \times 0.250 \times 2700 = 16\,200\ \text{J}$ The formula, correct substitution, answer in joules.
  3. $E = 16.2\ \text{kJ}$ Convert as requested: divide by 1000.

Worked example 14 · Calculation · transposing the divider formula

In a voltage divider, $R_2 = 4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$. The voltage across $R_1$ is 2.4 V and across $R_2$ is 3.6 V. Calculate $R_1$. [3]

Show solution [3 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $\dfrac{V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{R_1}{R_2}$, so $R_1 = \dfrac{R_2 V_1}{V_2}$ Transpose first: multiply both sides by $R_2$.
  2. $R_1 = \dfrac{4700 \times 2.4}{3.6} = 3133.3\ \Omega$ $R_2$ is converted to ohms. $V_1$ and $V_2$ are both in volts, so their unit cancels.
  3. $R_1 = 3.13\ \text{k}\Omega$ (3 s.f.) Sense-check: $R_1$ should be smaller than $R_2$, because $V_1$ is smaller than $V_2$. It is.

Worked example 15 · Calculation · the cost of rounding early

A winch drum of diameter 0.35 m turns at 47 rev/min and pulls a rope with a force of 5600 N. The winch is 82 % efficient. Calculate the electrical energy it takes in during 5.0 minutes. Then repeat the calculation rounding each intermediate value to 2 significant figures, with $\pi = 3.14$, and compare. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. $n = 47 \div 60 = 0.78333\ldots\ \text{rev/s}$ and $v = \pi d n = \pi \times 0.35 \times 0.78333\ldots = 0.86132\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ The rim speed of the drum is the rope speed. Keep the full values on the calculator.
  2. $P_{out} = Fv = 5600 \times 0.86132\ldots = 4823.4\ \text{W}$ Mechanical power at the rope.
  3. $P_{in} = P_{out} \div \eta = 4823.4 \div 0.82 = 5882.2\ \text{W}$ and $E_{in} = P_{in}t = 5882.2 \times 300 = 1\,764\,656\ \text{J}$ $t = 5.0 \times 60 = 300$ s.
  4. Final answer: $E_{in} = 1.76 \times 10^6\ \text{J}$ (3 s.f.). Rounding only at the end.

The rounded-early version gives $n = 0.78$, $v = 3.14 \times 0.35 \times 0.78 = 0.857 \to 0.86$, $P_{out} = 4816 \to 4800$, $P_{in} = 5853 \to 5900$, and $E_{in} = 1\,770\,000\ \text{J} = 1.77 \times 10^6\ \text{J}$. The last digit is wrong, even though every step "looked" accurate. That is the mark lost.

6 · The impacts of engineering: social, economic, environmental, sustainable IMPACTS

Every engineering project changes the world a little. A bridge changes how people travel, a wind farm changes what the sea horizon looks like, a robot changes who does a job. Captain Reid puts it bluntly: "A ship that sinks the harbour's fishing is a poor ship, however fast she sails." The course asks you to weigh social and economic impacts (positive and negative), environmental impacts (positive and negative), the sustainability of solutions, and emerging technologies and their impact. Marks here are earned by balanced, specific, explained points, not by slogans.

Three kinds of impact, each with two sides

Impacts of engineering. Every project has both positive (+) and negative (−) effects; a Higher answer says which it is describing.

A few real Scottish anchors show both sides. The Falkirk Wheel replaced a flight of locks with one rotating boat lift, linking two canals, which brought back through-travel and visitors (positive social and economic) but needed a large construction project and materials (cost, resource use). An offshore wind farm such as Beatrice in the Moray Firth generates electricity with no fuel burned (positive environmental), but its construction and the cables disturb the sea bed and marine life (negative environmental), and the jobs it creates have to be weighed against the cost of building and connecting it.

Sustainability and life-cycle thinking

📘 Sustainability

A solution is sustainable if it meets present needs without using up resources or damaging the environment in a way that stops future generations meeting theirs. It has three strands that must be balanced: environmental (does it harm the planet?), economic (can it be afforded over its whole life?) and social (is it fair and does it serve people?).

To judge sustainability, engineers look at the life cycle of a product, from raw material to grave, and ask where the impacts occur at each stage.

  • Renewable resources are replaced naturally on a human timescale (wind, tidal, solar, sustainably grown timber); non-renewable resources are used up much faster than they form (coal, oil, gas, most metal ores). Renewable does not mean harmless: it is a statement about the source, not about the whole life cycle.
  • Design for the whole life. A longer-lasting, repairable product with a lower running energy can be more sustainable even if it costs more to make. Using fewer parts and fewer different materials makes recycling easier.
  • Recycling reduces the raw material and energy needed, and avoids waste going to landfill, but collection, sorting and reprocessing also cost energy and money.
  • The energy used to make something is sometimes called its embodied energy. A product with a large embodied energy needs a long life to pay it back.

Emerging technologies

An emerging technology is one that is new, or still developing quickly, so that its full effects are not yet known. Because they are new, the best answers weigh benefits against risks and say what is still uncertain. These are examples, with a balanced view. Every claim below is deliberately general.

How to write a Higher extended answer

The 2024 course report says that questions needing a detailed written response "proved to be challenging, and often candidates provided a non-descriptive answer which lacked the detail required to achieve marks at this level". The cure is a structure that forces detail. Use point, explanation, consequence for every impact you describe:

📘 Point → Explanation → Consequence
  1. Point: state the impact and its type (social, economic, environmental) and whether it is positive or negative.
  2. Explanation: say how or why it happens, in the context of the technology named in the question.
  3. Consequence: say what follows for people, money or the environment, and for whom.

Aim for balance: when the question says "discuss" or "evaluate", include at least one positive and one negative, and finish with a short judgement.

Weak

"Wind turbines are good for the environment and give people jobs, but some people don't like them."

Why it scores poorly: three vague slogans, nothing explained, nothing specific to the question.

Strong

"Offshore wind turbines generate electricity without burning fuel (point: positive environmental), so the electricity they supply replaces power from gas-fired power stations that release carbon dioxide (explanation). This lowers the carbon emissions of the national grid and slows climate change (consequence)."

Worked example 16 · Extended response · discuss an impact

A company proposes to replace the diesel engine of a coastal ferry with a battery-electric drive charged from the grid. Discuss the possible impacts of this change. [6]

Show solution [6 marks, typical allocation: mark points like these]
  1. Environmental (+): no exhaust emissions or fuel burned while sailing, so local air quality in the harbour improves and carbon dioxide is cut, especially if the grid electricity is largely renewable. Point, explanation and consequence in one sentence.
  2. Social (+): the ferry is quieter and has less engine vibration and fumes, so passengers and nearby residents have a more pleasant journey. Names who benefits.
  3. Economic (+): electricity per mile may cost less than diesel, and an electric drive has fewer moving parts, so servicing costs fall. Running cost and maintenance.
  4. Economic (−): the batteries and charging equipment at the pier are expensive to buy and to install, so the up-front cost is high. The cost that has to be set against the savings.
  5. Environmental (−): batteries need mined materials and energy to make, and at the end of their life must be recycled or disposed of, and the grid electricity may not all be renewable. Life-cycle thinking.
  6. Social (−) or judgement: the range per charge may limit the routes and crossings, and engine-room jobs may change. A final judgement such as "overall beneficial on a short, regular route if the electricity is renewable" completes the answer. A balanced conclusion.

Common slips. Only positives, or only negatives; saying "it's green" without explaining; forgetting the life cycle of the battery.

Worked example 17 · Description · sustainability of a material

Two materials are being considered for the hull sheathing of a new research vessel: aluminium alloy, which can be recycled, and a plastic composite, which is difficult to recycle. Compare the sustainability of the two. [4]

Show solution [4 marks, typical allocation]
  1. Manufacture: producing aluminium from ore uses a lot of energy, and extraction mines a non-renewable resource; the composite also needs energy and (often) oil-derived raw materials. Both have impacts at the start of the life cycle.
  2. Use: both are corrosion-resistant if properly treated, so a long service life is possible, which spreads the impacts of making them over many years. Lifetime counts.
  3. End of life: the aluminium can be recycled into new metal, so less new ore is mined and less energy is used than starting from ore. Recycling closes the loop.
  4. The composite is hard to separate and recycle, so more of it is likely to end up as waste. Overall aluminium is the more sustainable choice at end of life, though its manufacturing energy is high. A reasoned judgement that mentions both sides.
🎖 Cadet Sergeant Rahman asks…

"If wind power is renewable, how can a wind farm have a negative environmental impact?"

Captain Reid: "Renewable describes where the energy comes from: the wind will never run out. It does not mean the project costs the environment nothing. Steel and concrete for the foundations have to be produced, cables are laid across the sea bed, and birds and marine life can be disturbed. When you weigh the impacts, you weigh the whole life cycle, not just the fuel."
