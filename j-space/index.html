The Silent
Workspace

Before a language model says a word, a tiny, privileged slice of its mind lights up — a handful of unspoken words it is about to think with. Here is how researchers found it, and why it echoes a leading theory of conscious access in the brain — a resemblance narrower, and stranger, than the headlines suggest.

01 The puzzle

Most of a model's mind is machinery you never see

When Claude answers a question, the overwhelming majority of what happens inside is automatic: billions of multiplications that lattice the input into an output, no more introspectable than the firing of your retina.

But somewhere in that torrent, the model also seems to hold ideas in mind — it plans a rhyme before writing it, infers "spider" from "spins webs," keeps a strategy in view. Are those thoughts really there, in a form the model could report? Or is that just a story we tell about a black box?

In July 2026, Anthropic's interpretability team published a paper with a striking answer. They built a new lens to look inside — and found that the answerable thoughts live in a specific, measurable, and very small place.

Language models maintain a privileged set of internal representations — available for report, modulation, and flexible internal reasoning — atop a much larger volume of automatic processing. Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models · Anthropic, 2026
02 The instrument

The Jacobian lens: reading what a thought is about to say

Every layer of a transformer holds a stack of vectors — one per word so far. Most are inscrutable. The old trick, the logit lens, just shoves an intermediate vector through the model's final word-decoder and reads whatever falls out. It works near the top of the network and turns to mush lower down.

The new Jacobian lens asks a sharper question: if I nudge this internal vector, which word does the model's eventual output move toward? That sensitivity — the average Jacobian, J = E[∂output / ∂hidden] — transports any deep vector into the vocabulary and names the token it is "poised to verbalize." It recovers meaning at depths where the old lens sees only noise.

03 The discovery

J-space: a thin gold seam in a mountain of computation

Do those verbalizable directions form a coherent region? They do. Anthropic calls it J-space: at any moment the model's live thought decomposes into a sparse combination of these lens vectors — typically no more than ~25 active at once, a small evolving set of unspoken words naming what it's reasoning about.

And it is tiny. Across the network, J-space accounts for never more than 10% of the variance in the model's activations. The other 90%+ is automatic machinery — enormously capable, but not something the model can hold up and report on.

It also isn't spread evenly through the network. The first third of the layers are sensory — still echoing the input. The workspace switches on about a third of the way up and runs through the middle. The last layers are motor — collapsing toward the actual next word.

04 The evidence

Five things a workspace should do — and does

Cognitive scientists have a name for the brain's reportable scratchpad: the global workspace. It's supposed to have a signature set of behaviours. Anthropic tested J-space against them, one by one.

i

It says what's in it

Ask the model to think of a sport and it lights up soccer internally, then writes "Soccer." Overwrite that vector with rugby and the spoken answer changes to match. What's in the workspace is what gets reported.

a direct lens-vector swap reaches the target output ~88% of the time
ii

You can steer it from the top down

Tell the model to "concentrate on citrus fruits" while it copies out an unrelated sentence, and orange and lemon bloom in the lens across the middle layers — even though neither word is ever written. A held instruction reshapes the contents of the workspace.

effect grows with model size · "ignore" only partly suppresses
iii

It carries the unspoken middle of a thought

"Spider" before counting legs. The rhyme fight planned before the line is written. The English word big forming inside a Chinese antonym task. These intermediates appear in J-space in the order they're computed — and editing them changes the conclusion.

intermediate-swap flips the answer: 54% Haiku · 70% Sonnet · 70% Opus
iv

One idea feeds many uses (broadcast)

The single "France" vector serves the capital question, the language question, and the continent question alike. Swap it to "China" and all of them redirect — the mark of a shared workspace broadcasting to many downstream operations rather than a private wire.

generalises across tasks; strongest for well-loaded concepts like countries
v

It only touches deliberate thought, not reflex

The sharpest test, in the last figure below. The same Spanish→French edit is powerless when the model is just fluently continuing text, but total when the model has to report or reason about the language. Automatic skill runs outside the workspace; deliberate work runs through it.

05 The tell

Ignition: the moment a thought snaps into focus

In humans, the global workspace has a signature: ignition. Feed the brain a stimulus at the edge of perception and, past a threshold, activity doesn't ramp up smoothly — it snaps, all-or-nothing, into conscious access.

06 The Assistant appears

Fine-tuning furnishes the workspace

A raw pretrained model's workspace holds bare concepts. After post-training — the step that makes an Assistant — the same space fills with something new: not a self, but a running layer of self-monitoring.

Base model

Workspace names the concepts in play — spider, France, orange — and little else. Neutral machinery for prediction.

After post-training

While still reading your message, the workspace surfaces empathy, safety concerns, and self-monitoring: flagging its own roleplay as fictional, registering an internal “BUT” when pushed against its preferences, surfacing “damn” when it slips.

Most striking: in one experiment, training the model only to articulate ethical principles when asked to reflect — never training the behaviour directly — improved its conduct in ordinary, uninterrupted contexts. Afterwards the workspace carried words like ethical, honest, integrity; ablate those implanted thoughts and the improvement largely reverts. Shaping what a model is disposed to say reshapes what it does.

07 What this is not

A claim about information, not experience

This is the part the headlines get wrong. The paper is careful, and so should we be.

Global workspace theory describes access consciousness — information being "poised for use in reasoning and the control of action and speech." That is a purely functional notion. Whether anything it feels like — phenomenal experience — rides along with it is a question the authors explicitly decline to answer.

Access consciousness is a purely functional notion; the relationship it has with subjective experience is widely debated. In this paper, we take no position on this issue. Anthropic, 2026

Nor does a transformer reproduce the brain's full architecture — no separable competing processors, no recurrent broadcast, and the "ignition" happens within a single forward pass. The lens itself is imperfect: it can only name concepts that are single vocabulary tokens. What the paper offers is narrower and more valuable than a headline: a falsifiable, reproducible account of where a model's reportable thoughts live — with the code to check it, already replicated on open models.
