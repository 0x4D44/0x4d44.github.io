An interactive field guide to the tooth

Root and Crown

A tooth is the hardest thing your body builds and the only organ it cannot repair. Break a bone and it knits; cut skin and it closes; dissolve a millimetre of enamel and it is gone for good. Everything that follows — the filling, the bigger filling, the crown, the root canal, the gap — is a consequence of that one asymmetry. This guide follows a tooth from the inside out and from birth to extraction: how it is built, why it hurts, how acid takes it apart a few micrometres at a time, what actually happens when the nerve inside dies, and what dentistry can and cannot buy back.

Almost everyone reading this has had a filling, and almost nobody has been told what a filling actually is — a hole cut deliberately larger than the disease, packed with a material that will fail on a schedule, in a tooth that has already lost the ability to heal. This guide explains the whole system.

The main text tells the story in plain English: what a tooth is made of, what destroys it, what hurts and why, and what each repair really costs in years. Where the honest biology or engineering is worth seeing — the chemistry of dissolving enamel, the pressure physics that make toothache uniquely awful, the survival statistics behind "how long will this last?" — it is folded into a panel like the one below. Open it or skip it; the story reads either way. And every chapter but the closing lexicon carries live apparatus you can drive: push a probe through the layers, trigger a nerve and watch two different pain fibres respond, run an acid attack in a mouth you control, age a tooth eighty years, pressurise an inflamed pulp until it dies, compare restorations over thirty years, work through a root canal step by step, and price up the endgame.

Panels like this hold the quantitative version — the solubility chemistry of hydroxyapatite, the hydrodynamic theory of dentine sensitivity, annual failure rates and survival curves, the low-compliance pressure argument for why pulpitis hurts the way it does. Nothing in the main narrative depends on opening them.

Three numbers recur. Enamel begins to dissolve below pH 5.5 — the "critical pH", and the reason the frequency of sugar matters far more than the amount. A tooth contains roughly 45,000 dentinal tubules per square millimetre near the pulp, each a fluid-filled pipe running to the nerve, which is why a tooth can feel cold at all. And the pulp sits in a rigid chamber that cannot expand, which is the single fact behind every story of toothache in this guide.

This is not medical advice

This is a science explainer written for curiosity, not a clinical resource. It describes how teeth and treatments work in general; it cannot tell you what is happening in your mouth, and the ranges quoted for treatment survival are population averages that say nothing about an individual tooth. If something hurts, see a dentist — and if you have facial swelling, fever, or difficulty swallowing or breathing, treat that as an emergency and seek urgent care.

The one asymmetry that explains everything

Start here, because every chapter is a consequence of it. Your body maintains itself continuously: bone remodels, skin turns over, liver regenerates. A tooth does almost none of this. Once it has erupted, the cell layer that built the enamel is gone — shed as the tooth comes through the gum. There is no enamel factory left. Enamel is not even really alive: it is about 96% mineral by weight, a dense weave of hydroxyapatite crystals with almost no protein and no cells at all.

So enamel is a strictly finite resource. You are issued a fixed quantity at eruption, and from that moment the account only goes one way.

The asymmetry

Enamel can remineralise — lost mineral ions can be redeposited into a crystal lattice that is still standing, which is what fluoride and saliva do, and why an early "white spot" lesion can be arrested. But enamel cannot regenerate: once the lattice itself has collapsed and a cavity has formed, no biological process rebuilds it. There is no scaffold left to rebuild onto.

That is the whole of restorative dentistry in one sentence. Every filling, inlay, crown and implant exists because this one tissue crossed a threshold from which biology has no route back.

The other tissues do a little better, and the differences matter:

What each tissue can do about damage
TissueLiving?Response to damageConsequence
EnamelNo — no cells Remineralises early lesions only; cannot rebuild lost structure Cavities are permanent; they get filled, not healed
DentineYes — via the pulp Lays down tertiary dentine beneath a threat, walling off the pulp Slow decay may be outrun; fast decay is not
PulpYes Inflames — but is trapped in a rigid chamber and cannot swell Inflammation strangles it; pulpitis is often a one-way door
Cementum & ligamentYes Remodels and can partly reattach Gum disease is treatable; lost bone rarely comes fully back
Alveolar boneYes Remodels constantly — but resorbs when the tooth goes An empty socket shrinks; implants have a time window

Read down that table and the clinical world reorganises itself. Gum disease is a chronic condition you manage. Decay is damage you repair. And the pulp — the one living part locked in a box that cannot expand — is where the emergencies come from.

Why toothache is its own category of pain

People who have had both will tell you a bad tooth beats a broken bone, and there is a structural reason rather than a poetic one. Inflame almost any tissue and it swells: the swelling is uncomfortable but it relieves pressure by expanding into the space around it. The pulp cannot. It is enclosed on all sides by rigid dentine, with one exit — a hole at the root tip roughly the width of a human hair.

A low-compliance compartment

Inflammation raises tissue pressure. In a chamber with rigid walls, that pressure has nowhere to go, so it compresses the very blood vessels supplying the tissue. Reduced blood flow causes more inflammation, which raises the pressure further. It is a compartment syndrome the size of a grain of rice, and it explains the whole clinical picture: the severity, the throbbing that tracks your pulse, why it is worse lying down, and why the process tends to run to completion rather than settling.

It also explains the strangest symptom in dentistry, the one that sends people to bed with a glass of iced water: as a pulp dies, cold can start to relieve the pain instead of provoking it. That reversal is worth a chapter of its own, and it gets Chapter 5.
