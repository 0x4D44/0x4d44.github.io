CHAPTER 1

The Hardest Thing You Own

Enamel is the hardest substance your body makes and the only one it can never make again. Under it lies dentine: softer, wetter and drilled through with tens of thousands of microscopic pipes per square millimetre, each one a line to the nerve. Four tissues, one junction that stops the whole thing shattering, and a ligament fine enough to feel a hair.

A tooth is a ceramic cap bonded to a shock absorber, hung in bone on a sling of collagen and wired to a nerve with a vocabulary of one word. Every clause in that sentence is load-bearing, and this guide is what happens when one of them fails.

Four tissues and a suspension

The part you can see is the crown, the part in the jaw is the root, and the line where they meet is the cervical margin — the neck. Almost everything interesting in dentistry happens near it, because that is where the armour runs out.

The tooth proper is four tissues. Three are hard: enamel over the crown, cementum over the root, and dentine forming the bulk of both. One is soft — the pulp, a small volume of vessels and nerves in a chamber inside the crown, draining down a canal in each root.

The tooth is not fixed to the jaw. The root hangs in its socket on the periodontal ligament, a sheet of collagen bundles anchored in cementum at one end and bone at the other, with the gum sealing the join. Cementum, ligament, alveolar bone and gum are the periodontium: a separate organ from the tooth it holds, with its own diseases.

Set the figure above to attachment and the tooth vanishes, leaving only what holds it in.

Enamel: ninety-six per cent mineral, nought per cent repair

Clench on a back tooth and you generate several hundred newtons — adult maximum bite force in the molar region is usually measured between 300 and 800 N. You do that thousands of times a day, on a surface that is warm, wet, coated in bacteria and periodically soaked in acid. The material that takes it is barely biological.

By weight, enamel is about 96% mineral — 95 to 97% in the standard histology texts — with roughly 1% protein and the rest water. Bone is about 65% mineral and full of living cells. Enamel has no cells, no blood supply and no nerves: a crystal, grown biologically and then abandoned.

The crystal is hydroxyapatite, laid down as needle-like crystallites tens of nanometres across and bundled into rods, or prisms, each 4 to 8 micrometres wide, running from the junction with the dentine out to the surface. Near the cusps neighbouring bundles weave past one another — decussation — which makes any crack that tries to cross them take an expensive route.

The result is the hardest tissue in the body: Knoop microhardness around 270 to 350, against roughly 50 to 70 for dentine. Over a molar cusp the cap is up to 2.5 mm thick. At the neck it thins to a knife edge and stops.

And it is finished. The cells that build enamel — ameloblasts — form a single layer over the developing crown, secrete the matrix, mineralise it, and are then lost as the tooth erupts. There is no reserve population and no second run. From the day a tooth appears in your mouth, the enamel you have is the enamel you will ever have.

The rule everything else follows from

Enamel remineralises: where the lattice is still standing, mineral can move back into it from saliva, which is how an early white-spot lesion is arrested and what fluoride helps with. Enamel does not regenerate: once the lattice has collapsed into a cavity, there is no scaffold to rebuild onto and no cell left that knows how.

Dentine: the living part, and the part that hurts

Under the enamel is a tissue with completely different manners. Dentine is about 70% mineral by weight, with roughly 20% collagen and 10% water, and the collagen is the difference. Dentine is not as hard as enamel and does not try to be. It is tough: it absorbs energy and resists cracking rather than indentation.

It is also perforated. Dentine is a dense field of parallel tubes, the dentinal tubules, each running from the pulp outwards. Garberoglio and Brännström counted them under the electron microscope in 1976, and their figures are still the ones quoted: near the pulp roughly 45,000 tubules per square millimetre, about 2.5 micrometres across; at the junction with the enamel roughly 20,000 per square millimetre and under a micrometre wide. Near the pulp they take up about a fifth of the surface area. At the junction, about one per cent.

So the floor of a deep cavity is not a solid barrier. It is a sieve pointing at the nerve, and it gets coarser the deeper you go — which is why "how deep is it?" is the first question a dentist asks about a hole, and what Chapter 6 turns on.

Dentine is also alive, at second hand. The odontoblasts that make it line the outer edge of the pulp and leave a cellular process trailing in each tubule as they retreat inwards. How far that process extends is genuinely argued over — the inner third, or the full thickness. What is not argued is that the tubules carry fluid, that the fluid moves when the surface is disturbed, and that the pulp notices. That mechanism is Chapter 2.

Being alive, dentine can also respond. Under a slow threat the odontoblasts thicken the tubule walls until they close, and lay down fresh tertiary dentine on the pulp side of the damage. A tooth losing a slow race against decay is quietly building a new wall behind the old one. Against fast decay it loses: the wall goes up at the pace of biology and the acid does not wait.

The junction that stops it shattering

Now the elegant part. A very hard, very brittle ceramic sits on a soft, wet, tough substrate, and the two have to stay married for eighty years. Bond a ceramic to a compliant backing in engineering and the interface is usually the first thing to go.

The dentino-enamel junction does not. It is not a flat glue line but a scalloped field of interlocking domes tens of micrometres across, deepest under the cusps where the loads are highest, and the transition from one tissue to the other is graded rather than abrupt.

Imbeni and colleagues measured the consequence directly, driving cracks through the enamel of extracted human teeth (Nature Materials, 2005). The cracks did not run along the interface and lift the cap off. They crossed it and were arrested almost immediately in the soft mantle dentine beyond, which blunted them and stopped them dead.

A natural composite

That is why teeth chip rather than explode, and why craze lines in old enamel are usually harmless. Enamel is allowed to be brittle because it never works alone: it is a wear surface bonded to an energy-absorbing core through an interface that turns a running crack into a stopped one.

It also sets the bar for every filling ever placed: a restoration has to reproduce, with glue and a few minutes of chairside time, a bond the tooth took years to grow. It never quite manages, and the margin is where fillings fail.

The suspension: cementum, ligament, bone and gum

Below the neck, enamel gives way to cementum: a thin layer about 45 to 50% mineral by weight, softer than dentine, let alone enamel. It is not armour. Its job is anchorage — collagen fibres are embedded in it as it forms, so the tooth has something a ligament can be tied to.

Between cementum and bone lies the periodontal ligament, a layer of collagen bundles only about 0.15 to 0.38 mm wide — the range Coolidge measured in 1937 and the one still quoted. The bundles run obliquely, embedded at one end in cementum and at the other in the socket wall, so a bite pulls them into tension instead of crushing the tooth into the bone. Your teeth hang. They do not sit.

The ligament suspends and cushions, carries the blood supply that keeps bone and cementum remodelling — which is what makes orthodontic movement possible — and it is packed with mechanoreceptors.

Why you can feel a hair between your teeth

Bite on a hair — 50 to 100 micrometres of keratin — and you know instantly that it is there and which tooth it is under. Studies of interocclusal thickness discrimination, notably Jacobs and van Steenberghe's work on oral tactile function, put the detection threshold for natural teeth at a few tens of micrometres, commonly quoted around 10 to 20 µm. Implant-supported teeth, which have no ligament, are several times coarser.

That sensitivity is not in the tooth. It is in the ligament around it. The ligament localises superbly; the pulp cannot localise at all. When a patient can point straight at the offending tooth, the trouble has usually spread out of the pulp and into the ligament — a change of address Chapter 5 depends on.

Above the bone sits the gingiva, and its dimensions are surprisingly consistent: Gargiulo, Wentz and Orban measured a sulcus about 0.69 mm deep, a junctional epithelium of about 0.97 mm and a connective tissue attachment of about 1.07 mm — roughly two millimetres of attachment above the crest of the bone.

Where enamel meets cementum at the neck, the two do not always meet cleanly. The classic survey reproduced in every oral histology text finds cementum overlapping enamel in around 60% of cases, an edge-to-edge join in around 30%, and in the remaining 10% a gap that leaves bare dentine exposed. Those teeth start life with a strip of unprotected, tubule-riddled dentine at the gum line.

The pulp, briefly

At the centre is the only soft tissue in the tooth: loose connective tissue, blood vessels, nerve fibres and, around its edge, the odontoblasts. Everything enters and leaves through the apical foramen, an opening a fraction of a millimetre across at the root tip. Two facts about it drive the rest of this guide. The pulp is enclosed by rigid dentine, so when it inflames it cannot swell — only pressurise. And its nerves report one thing: a tooth cannot feel warmth or touch or texture. It feels pain, and decides for itself what counts.

Molar, on the layout

"Ceramic on the outside, cable ducting in the middle, one small tenant at the centre who complains about everything. The ligament does the feeling. I do the chewing. Nerv here does the screaming, and only ever gets the one line."

Thirty-two of them, and how to name one

An adult set is 32 teeth in four quadrants of eight, replacing the 20 of the child's set, and each shape is a tool. Incisors are chisels for shearing. Canines have the longest roots in the mouth and guide the jaw as it slides sideways. Premolars are the transition. Molars are millstones: broad, multi-cusped, multi-rooted, and collecting most of the damage in this guide.

The third molars are the least reliable members of the set. Pooled estimates put congenital absence of at least one at roughly a fifth of people, with reported figures running from a few per cent to more than a third depending on population. They are the only teeth that are routinely optional.

Naming one needs a notation. Teeth are counted outwards from the midline in each quadrant, 1 for a central incisor to 8 for a third molar. FDI notation prefixes the quadrant, so the upper left second molar is tooth 27; Palmer notation brackets it instead, which is why a British dentist says "upper left seven" out loud. The chart is in Chapter 9.

Why the layers explain the rest of the guide

Read a tooth from the outside in and you are reading a gradient. Hardness falls, mineral falls, water rises, cells appear, and sensitivity climbs from nothing to unbearable. Decay works down that gradient; every restoration works back up it, trying to re-establish a seal the tooth grew for itself and cannot grow again.

Which leaves one question. If enamel has no nerves and dentine's nerves are in the pulp, why does cold air on a tooth feel like a needle? The answer is not the one most people assume, and it is the next chapter.
