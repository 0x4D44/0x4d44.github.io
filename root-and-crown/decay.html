CHAPTER 3

The Acid Clock

Nothing eats your teeth. Decay is not rot, and it is not an infection in the everyday sense of catching something. It is a running balance: a mineral that dissolves in acid, a bacterial film that makes acid whenever it is fed, and a saliva that puts the mineral back. A tooth lasts as long as the putting-back keeps up. This chapter is about what tips the balance — and about the most counter-intuitive fact in dentistry, that how often you eat sugar matters far more than how much.

Untreated decay in adult teeth is, by a wide margin, the most common disease on earth. The World Health Organization's 2022 Global Oral Health Status Report puts it at around two billion people, with another half a billion children affected in their baby teeth. Nothing else comes close. The mechanism behind all of it fits on a beer mat.

It is dissolution, not digestion

Start by deleting the cartoon. Bacteria do not gnaw at enamel or bore into it. They sit on the outside of the tooth in a film, they are fed sugar, and they excrete acid as waste. The acid does the damage. Enamel is about 96% mineral by weight — hydroxyapatite, near enough calcium phosphate — and calcium phosphate dissolves in acid. That is the entire disease.

The distinction is not pedantry; it tells you what to measure. If decay were an invasion you would count bacteria. Because it is a dissolution, what predicts damage is how long the surface spends below the pH at which its mineral dissolves. Time under acid is the currency — and it is spent both ways. Saliva at rest is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate, so left alone it puts mineral back rather than taking it. A tooth is not a fortress under siege. It is an account with deposits and withdrawals, and a cavity is what happens after years of being overdrawn.

The balance

Demineralisation minus remineralisation, integrated over years. Caries — the proper name for the disease — is not an event but the net result of tens of thousands of reversible cycles, only the last of which is visible. By the time a dentist finds a hole, the interesting part happened long ago.

The film that makes the acid

Wipe a tooth clean and within seconds it is dirty again. Salivary proteins adsorb onto the enamel and form the acquired pellicle, a molecular skin a fraction of a micrometre thick. It is not a problem in itself — it lubricates and slows acid diffusion — but it is the landing platform. Bacteria bind to the pellicle, not to enamel, and the rest of the community assembles on top of them.

That community is dental plaque, and calling it a smear of germs badly undersells it. It is a biofilm: a layered city with channels and gradients, encased in a sticky matrix the bacteria build out of your sugar. The mouth supports something over 700 bacterial species-level taxa, catalogued in the Human Oral Microbiome Database. And a few hundred micrometres of that film is enough to make the surface beneath it a separate chemical world: saliva sluicing over the top can sit at pH 7 while the fluid pressed against the enamel is two units more acidic. Which is why swilling water around is not a fix.

The villain, demoted

Strep is Streptococcus mutans, the bacterium that has taken the blame for tooth decay since the 1960s. He is genuinely good at his job — he turns sugar into lactic acid fast, he survives the acid he makes, and he glues himself down with polymers built from sucrose.

But he is not the disease. He is a symptom of it. Plenty of caries-free mouths are full of him, and some actively decaying lesions have none at all.

Two words describe a dangerous plaque. Acidogenic means it makes acid from fermentable carbohydrate. Aciduric means it keeps working in the acid it has made, while its neighbours cannot. Streptococcus mutans and the lactobacilli are the classic names, and both are both. For decades the model was essentially infectious: find the pathogen, remove it, cure the disease.

That model has not survived the evidence. S. mutans is routinely detectable in mouths that never decay, and molecular studies — Aas and colleagues, 2008 — have found active lesions where it could not be detected at all. The modern reading, from Philip Marsh's ecological plaque hypothesis of 1994 and its extension by Takahashi and Nyvad in 2008, is that caries is a shift in a whole community, not the arrival of a villain. Feed a plaque sugar repeatedly and it is acidic for hours a day. Acid-hating species decline; acid-tolerant ones flourish and make more acid still. It is the changed neighbourhood, not any one resident, that dissolves the tooth.

The number that governs everything: critical pH

Enamel does not dissolve gradually as things get more acidic. It has a threshold. Above it the surrounding fluid is supersaturated with respect to hydroxyapatite and mineral deposits; below it the fluid is undersaturated and mineral leaves. That tipping point is the critical pH, and for enamel it sits at about 5.5.

Now the fact that reorganises the chapter. Root surfaces are not enamel. Where the gum has receded, what is exposed is cementum and then dentine — roughly 70% mineral by weight against enamel's 96%, with a carbonate-rich, more soluble apatite. Root surfaces start dissolving at around pH 6.2 (reported values run from about 6.0 to 6.7; this guide uses 6.2). That threshold sits so much closer to the resting pH of plaque that an exposed root is underwater for several times as long in a normal day. Recession changes the game completely, which is why root caries is largely a disease of later life.

Go deeperwhy 5.5, and why it is not a constant

Hydroxyapatite dissolves by consuming protons:

Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 + 8H+ ⇌ 10Ca2+ + 6HPO42− + 2H2O 3.1

Whether that runs left or right depends on how the ion product of calcium and phosphate in the fluid compares with the mineral's solubility product. The critical pH is the pH at which the two are equal — saturation, where nothing dissolves and nothing deposits.

So, as Colin Dawes argued in a much-cited 2003 paper in the Journal of the Canadian Dental Association, 5.5 is not a constant like a melting point but a property of the fluid bathing that particular tooth. Reported values span roughly 5.1 to 6.5; the single figure is a useful average, and this guide uses it as one.

The Stephan curve

In the 1940s Robert Stephan pushed miniature antimony electrodes into human dental plaque and did what nobody had managed before: he watched the pH change in real time after a glucose rinse. The curve he traced is the most important graph in preventive dentistry.

It has a characteristic shape. Plaque pH falls steeply within a couple of minutes as bacteria ferment the sugar to lactic, acetic and formic acids. It bottoms out in the first five to twenty minutes. Then it climbs back to resting pH over roughly twenty to sixty minutes, as saliva buffers the acid, dilutes it and carries it away.

Look hard at that asymmetry, because it is where the counter-intuitive result comes from. The fall is fast and the recovery is slow. Worse, the depth of the fall is remarkably insensitive to how much sugar arrived: a mouthful saturates what the bacteria can metabolise in the time available, so a small sweet drops the pH nearly as far as a large dessert.

So each separate encounter with fermentable carbohydrate costs a roughly fixed toll: one dip, one slow climb, one block of time below critical pH. The tolls add up by count. Eat a biscuit and you pay once. Break the same biscuit into six pieces across an afternoon and you pay six times.

Set the slider to 3 and read the minutes below critical pH. Now set it to 9 — the same total sugar, taken in more sittings — and watch the number roughly treble. Then switch the surface to exposed root and see the same nine-moment day cross four hours.

That is the whole argument, and it is why dentists ask about grazing, sipping and bedtime drinks in a way that can feel like an interrogation about morals rather than chemistry. It is not about morals. A day with three eating occasions and a day with nine can contain identical sugar and produce completely different amounts of time under acid.

The uncomfortable corollary

Foods that feel virtuous can be terrible on the clock and foods that feel indulgent can be fine. A slice of cake with lunch is one dip. Dried fruit picked at through an afternoon, or a juice sipped over an hour, is a long series of them. Frequency is blind to how healthy something sounds — it counts only fermentable carbohydrate arriving at the plaque, and lingering starch counts too.

This is a description of the chemistry, not a diet.

Saliva is the whole defence

Everything that pulls the curve back up is saliva, and it does four jobs. It buffers — bicarbonate is the workhorse in stimulated saliva, phosphate matters more at rest, and bacteria themselves generate alkali from urea and arginine. It clears, diluting acid and swallowing it away. It supplies, keeping calcium and phosphate high enough that resting saliva is supersaturated with respect to enamel, so the moment the pH recovers the surface starts repairing itself. And it coats, laying down the pellicle.

Typical unstimulated whole salivary flow is around 0.3 to 0.4 millilitres a minute; chewing pushes it several times higher. Hyposalivation is usually defined at or below about 0.1 millilitres a minute unstimulated. That is not a small loss of comfort — it is the removal of the only repair system the tooth has.

Which is why xerostomia — dry mouth — is quietly catastrophic for teeth. Hundreds of prescription drugs list it among their effects, and the risk compounds when several are taken together. Radiotherapy to the head and neck can put the salivary glands in the treatment field and damage them permanently, producing what clinicians call radiation caries: lesions that wrap around the necks of the teeth and can wreck a healthy dentition within a year or two. Sjögren's syndrome does something similar more slowly.

These patients are doing nothing wrong. They are playing the same game with the defence removed — the clearest natural experiment there is for how much work saliva was doing all along.

Fluoride, mechanistically

Fluoride is the most effective thing ever done about tooth decay, and for a long time almost everyone had the mechanism wrong. The assumption was that swallowing it in childhood built better enamel from the inside. It does a little of that. But the effect is overwhelmingly topical: it happens after the tooth has erupted, at the surface, in the plaque fluid — an argument Fejerskov, Thylstrup and Larsen made forcefully in 1981, and it has held. It is also why a paste you spit out works. Three mechanisms, in descending order of importance:

What a fluoride ion actually does at the enamel surface
MechanismWhat happensEffect
A better mineral When mineral redeposits with fluoride present, the hydroxyl in the lattice is replaced to give fluorapatite or fluorhydroxyapatite The repaired surface dissolves only below about pH 4.5 rather than 5.5 — a threshold ordinary plaque dips often fail to reach
Catalysed repair Fluoride adsorbs onto the crystal surface, speeding deposition from supersaturated saliva and inhibiting dissolution even at very low concentrations Remineralisation wins more of the daily exchanges; a reservoir of calcium fluoride on the surface releases fluoride as the pH falls
Antibacterial At high concentration fluoride interferes with bacterial glycolysis — the enolase enzyme and the proton-pumping ATPase Real, but a minor contributor at everyday concentrations

The second row is the one to keep. Fluoride does not armour a tooth. It shifts the odds of every remineralisation cycle, thousands of times a year, in the tooth's favour — and it does it in exactly the place where the fight happens.

Go deeperhow good is the evidence?

Unusually good. The Cochrane review by Marinho and colleagues in 2003, pooling dozens of randomised trials in children and adolescents, found a prevented fraction of about 24% (95% confidence interval roughly 21% to 28%) for decayed, missing and filled tooth surfaces against non-fluoride toothpaste. The 2019 update by Walsh and colleagues found the benefit rises with concentration.

That is a population-level reduction, not a guarantee for an individual tooth. But the direction and size of the effect have been stable across decades and study designs, which is more than can be said for most of dentistry.

How a lesion actually develops

Here is the part that surprises people who assume decay works from the outside in. It does not: the first tissue lost sits beneath an apparently intact surface.

Enamel is porous at a fine scale. Acid diffuses inward along the boundaries between crystals and prisms and dissolves mineral a few tens of micrometres down. The freed calcium and phosphate diffuse back out — but on the way they pass through more saturated fluid, and some reprecipitates there. The result is a subsurface lesion: a spreading region of demineralised enamel roofed by an intact surface layer. It is invisible, and poking it hard enough to feel anything is a good way to break the roof.

As the lesion grows it scatters light and the enamel above loses its translucency. This is the white spot lesion — chalky, matt, most visible when the tooth is dried. It is the last stage that can go backwards: with the surface layer intact, calcium, phosphate and fluoride diffuse in and reprecipitate, and a white spot can arrest, harden and stay put for the rest of a life, often picking up a brown stain as a souvenir.

Then the roof collapses. Cavitation is the one-way door in this chapter. Plaque now lives inside a hole no brush can reach, and no biological process rebuilds the missing structure. An arrested cavity is achievable; a healed one is not.

The shape of the damage

Enamel prisms run roughly perpendicular to the surface, and the lesion follows them: in enamel it forms a cone with its base at the surface and its point aimed at the dentino-enamel junction, the DEJ.

At the DEJ the geometry flips. Dentine is much less mineralised and dissolves faster, so the lesion spreads sideways along the junction and opens into a second cone, base at the DEJ, pointing at the pulp. That lateral spread undermines the enamel above it, which is now a shell over a void. One ordinary bite, years later, and a large piece falls away. The patient reports that a tooth broke suddenly on something soft. It had been failing quietly for years.

Go deeperhow fast, really?

Years, not weeks. Longitudinal radiographic studies that follow the same surfaces over time — Ingegerd Mejàre and colleagues on Swedish adolescents is the most quoted work — find that an enamel lesion between two teeth typically takes on the order of three to four years to reach dentine, with enormous variation between individuals and surfaces. Occlusal fissures, which trap plaque in a groove no bristle enters, run faster.

So a lesion found early usually has years of slack in it, which is why modern practice often monitors and remineralises rather than drilling on sight. The tooth is not passive meanwhile: the pulp answers a slow front by laying down tertiary dentine beneath it. A slow lesion may be outrun; a fast one is not — see Chapter 5.

What the acid clock actually says

Put it together and caries stops looking like bad luck or bad character and starts looking like arithmetic. A surface is under attack for a fixed number of minutes per sugar moment. Saliva sets how long each attack lasts. Fluoride sets how far the pH must fall before the mineral gives way, and how fast the repair runs afterwards. Recession moves the threshold from 5.5 to 6.2 and changes the whole game.

None of which makes decay simple in a real mouth: plaque is unevenly distributed, and the same diet in two mouths can produce different outcomes. But almost every effective intervention in dentistry pulls one of the four levers on this page — the frequency, the film, the saliva or the mineral. A tooth is rarely destroyed by one bad day. It is destroyed by a clock that spent too many hours, over too many years, on the wrong side of pH 5.5.
