CHAPTER 9

The Lexicon

One word in this guide means two completely different things, and most misunderstandings in a dental surgery start there. A crown is the part of your tooth above the gum — and it is also the manufactured cap that gets cemented over it when that part has gone. Nobody says which they mean, because to a dentist the context is obvious and to everyone else it is not. This last chapter is the decoder: the vocabulary, the numbering, every figure in the guide with its source, and how the whole trade got here.

Dentistry has a peculiar dialect. Part of it is ordinary Greek and Latin doing ordinary work — endodontics is simply "inside the tooth" — and part of it is a private shorthand of numbers and initials that sounds, to a person lying flat with a suction tube in their cheek, exactly like a bingo call. Neither part is difficult. It is just never explained.

So here it is, in four pieces. A glossary of nearly ninety terms, each in one or two plain sentences. The three rival systems for naming a tooth, and what "upper left seven" actually points at. The quantities this guide leans on, gathered into a single table with the range they really occupy and the study or textbook they came from. And a timeline, because almost everything in the preceding eight chapters was invented by someone, usually more recently than you would guess.

The one rule for reading any of it

Every biological number is a distribution, not a value. Enamel is not 96% mineral; enamel is about 96% mineral, and the tooth next to it is slightly different. Treatment survival figures are worse than that: they are population averages drawn from studies with different patients, different operators and different definitions of failure. They describe how a thousand teeth behave. They say nothing whatever about one.

A working glossary

Alphabetical, and deliberately plain. Where a term has a common alternative — an American spelling, an older name, a word patients actually use — it sits beside the headword in monospace. Most terms set in bold in earlier chapters are here.

Glossary index jump to a letter
letter
terms defined —
abfraction
A wedge-shaped notch cut into the tooth right at the gum line. The name carries a theory — that the notch is caused by the tooth flexing under bite load — which is still argued over. Most such notches probably have more than one cause.
abrasion
Wear caused by something that is not another tooth — a hard brush, an abrasive paste, a pipe stem, a habit. It usually appears as a smooth groove at the neck of the tooth rather than a flattened cusp.
abscess
A walled-off pocket of pus. In a tooth it usually forms at the root tip, once the pulp inside has died and bacteria have spread out through the apex into bone.
abutment two senses
A tooth that carries something else — the teeth on either side of a bridge are its abutments. Also the connector screwed into an implant to hold the crown, which is a different object with the same name.
acquired pellicle
The film of salivary proteins that settles onto a clean tooth surface within seconds of cleaning it. Bacteria attach to the pellicle rather than to the enamel itself, so it is the foundation layer of every plaque deposit.
A-delta fibre A-δ
The thinly myelinated nerve fibre that carries the sharp, brief pain of cold, sweetness or a probe. It conducts fast, ends near the pulp–dentine border, and it is the fibre that fires when fluid moves in the tubules.
alveolar bone
The bone of the jaw that holds the tooth sockets. It exists because the teeth are there, and it shrinks away once they are not.
amalgam silver filling
A filling material made by mixing an alloy powder of silver, tin and copper with liquid mercury, which sets hard in minutes. Cheap, tolerant of a damp field and extremely durable; now in decline for cosmetic and environmental reasons rather than clinical ones.
ameloblast
The cell that makes enamel. Ameloblasts finish the job before the tooth erupts and are then lost with the rest of the enamel organ, which is the reason no more enamel is ever laid down.
apex
The tip of a root. Nerve and blood vessels enter the tooth here, through a small opening, and this is where infection from a dead pulp emerges into bone.
apical foramen
That opening: the door between the pulp and the rest of the body. It is a few tenths of a millimetre across and often sits slightly to one side of the anatomical tip rather than exactly at it.
apical periodontitis
Inflammation of the tissues around a root tip, driven by bacteria living inside a dead root canal. On a radiograph it appears as a dark patch where the immune response has destroyed bone.
apicoectomy root-end resection
Surgery that exposes a root tip through the gum, removes the last few millimetres of it and seals the cut end from behind. Used when infection persists and cleaning the canal again from above is impossible or has already failed.
attrition
Wear caused by tooth rubbing against tooth. Over decades it flattens cusps into tabletops; grinding accelerates it enormously.
bruxism
Clenching or grinding the teeth. Sleep bruxism and awake bruxism are now treated as two distinct behaviours with different causes; both load teeth far beyond anything chewing requires.
calculus tartar
Plaque that has taken up minerals from saliva and set into a hard deposit welded to the tooth. It cannot be brushed off, and its rough surface is an excellent home for more plaque.
caries tooth decay
The disease itself: acid produced by bacteria dissolving mineral out of the tooth faster than saliva can put it back. The hole is the end result, not the disease.
cavitation
The moment a demineralised enamel surface collapses inwards and the lesion becomes a hole. Up to that point the damage can still be arrested and re-hardened; after it the surface can no longer be cleaned, and nothing rebuilds it.
cellulitis
Infection spreading diffusely through soft tissue instead of collecting as a walled-off abscess. From a tooth it can track into the face and neck, which is why facial swelling with fever is treated as urgent.
cementum
The thin, bone-like layer covering the root, into which the fibres of the periodontal ligament are anchored. It is softer than enamel and starts dissolving at a higher pH, which is why an exposed root decays so readily.
cervical margin
The line at the neck of the tooth where the enamel of the crown gives way to the cementum of the root. Enamel is thinnest here, the gum seals against it, and it is the hardest place in the mouth to finish a filling or a crown well.
C fibre unmyelinated
The unmyelinated nerve fibre that carries dull, badly located, lingering pain. C fibres lie deeper in the pulp, need real tissue damage before they fire, and they are what makes an irreversibly inflamed pulp ache rather than twinge.
chlorhexidine
An antiseptic used as a mouthwash and sometimes as a root canal irrigant. It kills bacteria and clings to tooth surfaces for hours, but unlike sodium hypochlorite it dissolves no dead tissue.
composite white filling
A tooth-coloured filling material: a resin matrix packed with glass or ceramic filler, hardened on demand by blue light. It is bonded to the tooth rather than wedged into it, which is why the tooth can be cut more conservatively.
critical pH
The pH at which the fluid bathing a tooth is exactly saturated with respect to its mineral: above it the surface cannot dissolve, below it it can. Conventionally about 5.5 for enamel, but genuinely anywhere between 5.1 and 6.5 depending on the calcium and phosphate already in the plaque fluid.
crown sense 1 — anatomical
The part of the tooth covered by enamel: everything from the neck upwards. You grow one, once. Nothing can make you another.
crown sense 2 — restoration; "cap"
A manufactured shell of metal, ceramic or both, cemented over a tooth that has been trimmed down to receive it. Same word, entirely different object. When a dentist says "this needs a crown" they mean sense 2, and when they say "the crown is broken down" they usually mean sense 1.
cusp
A peak on the biting surface. Molars carry four or five; they interlock with the opposing tooth, guide the bite and take the highest stresses in the mouth.
cuspal coverage
A restoration that roofs over one or more cusps instead of sitting between them — an onlay or a crown. It stops a weakened cusp being levered off, and it is why a root-treated back tooth is usually covered rather than simply filled.
dentine dentin, US
The yellow, living bulk of the tooth beneath the enamel: about 70% mineral by weight, shot through with microscopic tubules and made by cells lining its inner surface. Softer than enamel but far tougher — it is the layer that stops cracks.
dentine hypersensitivity
Short, sharp pain from exposed dentine in response to cold, sweetness or touch, with no disease of the pulp behind it. Explained by fluid moving inside open tubules.
dentino-enamel junction DEJ; ADJ
The scalloped boundary where enamel meets dentine. It arrests cracks running inwards through the brittle enamel, and it is also the plane along which decay spreads sideways once it gets there, undermining sound enamel from below.
dry socket alveolar osteitis
Severe pain from an extraction socket a few days after the tooth comes out, when the blood clot breaks down and leaves bare bone exposed. It is not an infection in the usual sense.
EDTA
A chelating agent — a molecule that grips calcium ions and holds them in solution. In root canal treatment it is used to soften and remove the mineral smear left by the files so the disinfectant can reach the tubules.
enamel
The outer shell of the crown and the hardest tissue the body makes: roughly 96% mineral by weight, with no cells, no blood supply and no nerves. It can be re-hardened while its crystal scaffold still stands. It can never be rebuilt.
endodontics
The branch of dentistry dealing with the inside of the tooth — pulp, root canals and the tissues at the root tip. Literally "within the tooth".
erosion
Mineral dissolved off the tooth by acid that no bacterium made: fizzy drinks, citrus, wine, stomach acid. It thins whole surfaces and rounds edges rather than drilling a discrete hole.
extraction
Removing a tooth from its socket. Simple when the tooth can be gripped and eased out along the line of its roots; surgical when bone must be removed or the tooth divided first.
fluorapatite
The crystal formed when a fluoride ion takes the place of the hydroxyl group in hydroxyapatite. It dissolves at a lower pH than the mineral it replaced, which is the entire basis of the fluoride effect.
furcation
The fork where the roots of a multi-rooted tooth divide. Once gum disease has opened that space up, neither the patient nor the dentist can properly clean inside it.
gingiva gum
The collar of tissue that seals around the neck of each tooth. That seal is the only barrier between the mouth and the bone underneath.
gingivitis
Reversible inflammation of the gum caused by plaque sitting at the margin. Bleeding on brushing is the usual sign; no bone has yet been lost, and it settles when the plaque goes.
glass ionomer
A filling and lining material that sets by an acid-base reaction, bonds chemically to tooth mineral and releases fluoride slowly. Weaker than composite under load but far more tolerant of a surface that will not stay dry.
gutta-percha
A rubbery latex from Palaquium trees, formed into tapered cones and used to fill root canals once they are clean. Inert, dense on a radiograph and removable again if the canal ever has to be re-treated.
hydrodynamic theory
Brännström's explanation of dentine sensitivity: a stimulus makes the fluid inside the tubules move, and it is that movement, not the stimulus itself, that fires the nerves at the inner end. It is why cold, air, sweetness and touch all produce the same sharp pain.
hydroxyapatite
The calcium phosphate crystal that forms the mineral of enamel, dentine and bone — Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2. Its solubility in acid is the reason teeth dissolve at all.
implant
A titanium or ceramic screw placed into the jawbone to replace a root, onto which a crown is later fitted. It fuses directly to bone and has no ligament, so it neither moves nor feels like a tooth.
inlay
A solid restoration made outside the mouth — in a laboratory or a milling machine — and cemented into a prepared cavity. Unlike a filling, it is not built up in place.
irreversible pulpitis
Inflammation of the pulp past the point of recovery: pain that lingers after the stimulus is gone, often arriving unprovoked and worse lying down. The diagnosis is a prediction — this pulp will die whatever is done to the outside of the tooth.
lamina dura
The thin white line of dense bone lining the socket on a radiograph. Its disappearance around a root tip is one of the earliest radiographic signs that something inside the tooth has died.
Ludwig's angina
A rapidly spreading infection of the floor of the mouth and the neck, most often from a lower molar. It swells the tongue upwards and backwards and can obstruct the airway; it is a medical emergency, not a dental one.
MB2
The second canal in the mesiobuccal root of an upper molar: narrow, hidden beneath a shelf of dentine and the canal most commonly missed. A missed MB2 is among the best-documented reasons for a root canal treatment to fail.
microleakage
Seepage of fluid and bacteria along the microscopic gap between a restoration and the tooth around it. It is why fillings fail at their edges rather than in the middle.
necrosis
Death of tissue. A necrotic pulp is dead and usually infected; the tooth stops responding to cold and may slowly darken.
obturation
Filling and sealing the cleaned canal system, normally with gutta-percha and a cement sealer. It is the last step of a root canal treatment and the one that shows up on the radiograph — although the cleaning that preceded it did the real work.
occlusal
Of the biting surface. The occlusion is the way upper and lower teeth meet; an occlusal filling is one on the chewing surface.
odontoblast
The cell that makes dentine. Odontoblasts line the inner surface of the dentine with long processes reaching out into the tubules, and they keep laying down new dentine for as long as they are alive.
onlay
Like an inlay, but extended over one or more cusps to protect them from splitting. The usual middle ground between a large filling and a full crown.
osseointegration
The direct structural bond between living bone and a titanium surface, noticed by accident by Per-Ingvar Brånemark in the 1950s. Every dental implant in the world depends on it.
periodontal ligament PDL
The sheet of collagen fibres slung between root and socket wall, suspending the tooth rather than cementing it in place. It cushions the bite and carries the sensors that tell you how hard you are biting — which is why you can feel a hair between your teeth.
periodontitis
Gum disease that has gone on to destroy the ligament and the bone holding the tooth in. Unlike gingivitis, the damage does not fully reverse; treatment stops the loss rather than restoring it.
periodontium
The supporting apparatus of a tooth taken as one thing: gingiva, periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone. Dentistry treats it as a single organ because disease in any part of it reaches the rest.
plaque dental biofilm
An organised community of bacteria growing on the tooth inside a matrix they secrete themselves. Not "food debris": it is a living film with its own internal chemistry, and it is what makes the acid.
post and core dowel core
A rod cemented into a root canal to retain a build-up of material where too little tooth is left to hold a crown. It provides retention only — a post does not strengthen a root, and cutting the canal to receive one weakens it.
pulp "the nerve"
The soft tissue at the centre of the tooth: nerves, blood vessels, immune cells and the dentine-making odontoblasts, sealed inside a rigid chamber that cannot expand.
pulpectomy
Complete removal of the pulp from both the chamber and the canals — the clearing-out stage of a root canal treatment.
pulpitis
Inflammation of the pulp, from any cause. Split into reversible and irreversible according to whether it can settle once the irritant is removed.
pulpotomy
Removal of only the pulp inside the crown, leaving the living tissue in the roots and dressing over the cut surface. Long standard in children's teeth and increasingly used in adult teeth whose root pulp is still healthy.
radiolucent
Dark on a radiograph, because X-rays passed through easily. Bone destroyed by infection is radiolucent, and so is the pulp space and a carious lesion.
radiopaque
Light on a radiograph, because X-rays were absorbed. Enamel, metal and gutta-percha are radiopaque; filling materials are deliberately formulated to be, since otherwise nobody could see whether one had failed.
remineralisation
Calcium and phosphate from saliva being redeposited into a partly dissolved crystal lattice, greatly helped by fluoride. It repairs early damage and it is the reason an early lesion can be arrested — but it cannot rebuild a lattice that has already collapsed.
resorption
The body dissolving its own hard tissue, using the same cells that remodel bone. It can eat a root from the inside or the outside, usually silently, and after an extraction it shrinks the ridge that is left.
reversible pulpitis
Mild inflammation of the pulp: a sharp response to cold that stops within seconds of the cold being taken away. Remove the cause and the pulp recovers.
root canal treatment endodontic treatment; RCT
Cleaning, shaping, disinfecting and sealing the canal system of a tooth whose pulp is inflamed beyond recovery or already dead. The tooth stays. The tissue inside it does not.
rubber dam dental dam
A sheet of rubber clamped around the tooth to isolate it from the rest of the mouth. It keeps saliva off the working field and keeps instruments and irrigants from being swallowed.
secondary caries recurrent caries
New decay at the margin of an existing restoration. It is the commonest reason a filling is replaced, and it is genuinely hard to tell from a stained but sound edge — which is why some fillings are replaced that did not need to be.
secondary dentine
Dentine laid down slowly throughout life on the inner wall of the pulp chamber, narrowing it year by year. Unlike tertiary dentine it is not a response to injury — it is simply what living odontoblasts do.
sinus tract "gum boil"; fistula
A channel bored through bone and gum by pus escaping from an infected root tip, surfacing as a small spot that discharges. Because it relieves the pressure it usually stops hurting, which is exactly why it gets ignored.
smear layer
The film of ground-up dentine and debris that a bur or a file smears across a cut surface, plugging the mouths of the tubules. Whether to remove it, and with what, is a long-running argument in both restorative dentistry and endodontics.
sodium hypochlorite bleach; NaOCl
The principal root canal irrigant, used in dentistry at concentrations of roughly 0.5–6%. It is unique in dissolving dead organic tissue as well as killing bacteria, which is why nothing has displaced it in seventy years.
Stephan curve
The plot of plaque pH against time after a sugar challenge: a steep fall within minutes, then a slow climb back over the following half hour or more. Named for Robert Stephan, who published it in the 1940s, and the single most useful picture in cariology.
tertiary dentine reparative dentine
New dentine laid down by the pulp directly beneath a threat, as a wall. It is the tooth's only genuine defensive move, and it is slow — fast decay outruns it easily.
third molar wisdom tooth
The eighth and last tooth in each quadrant, erupting in the late teens or twenties if it erupts at all. A substantial minority of people never grow all four.
trismus
Restricted mouth opening, from muscle spasm, infection or surgery. It matters clinically because it puts the back teeth out of reach.
white spot lesion
The first visible stage of decay: a chalky, opaque patch where enamel has lost mineral beneath a surface that is still intact. It can be arrested and re-hardened, and it is the last stage at which nothing has to be cut.
working length
The distance from a fixed reference point on the crown to the point near the root tip at which canal preparation should stop. It is measured separately for every canal, before any of them are shaped.
xerostomia dry mouth
Loss of saliva, from medication, disease or radiotherapy. Saliva buffers acid, clears sugar and carries the minerals for repair, so losing it raises decay risk sharply and quickly.
zirconia
Zirconium dioxide ceramic: very strong, tooth-coloured and now widely used for crowns, bridges and implant components. Its strength allows thinner crowns, so less of the underlying tooth has to be cut away.

How teeth are numbered

A dentist reading a chart aloud is dictating coordinates, not making conversation. "Two-seven, mesio-occlusal composite" locates a specific surface of a specific tooth precisely enough for a stranger to find it a decade later. There are three systems in common use, they do not agree, and the world is split roughly along national lines about which one is correct.

FDI: two digits, and the one to learn

The FDI system — from the Fédération Dentaire Internationale, and standardised as ISO 3950 — is used almost everywhere outside the United States. Every tooth gets two digits. The first names the quadrant, the second names the position.

The mouth is divided into four quadrants by the midline and the bite. Facing the patient, they are numbered clockwise starting from the patient's upper right: quadrant 1 upper right, 2 upper left, 3 lower left, 4 lower right. Baby teeth use the same clockwise sweep with the digits 5, 6, 7 and 8.

Within a quadrant, teeth are numbered outwards from the midline. Position 1 is the central incisor — the front tooth beside the gap in a child's smile — and the count runs backwards to position 8, the third molar. Every quadrant has the same eight positions, so the same second digit always means the same kind of tooth.

Put the two digits together and the code is unambiguous. FDI 27 is quadrant 2, position 7: the patient's upper left second molar — the second tooth from the back on the upper left, assuming a wisdom tooth is there behind it. In the surgery it is read digit by digit, "two-seven", never "twenty-seven", precisely so nobody mistakes it for a quantity.

"Upper left seven", in ordinary words

Look in a mirror. Find the gap between your two upper front teeth, then count backwards along the left side of your upper jaw — your left, not the mirror's. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven. The seventh is a large grinding tooth near the back. That is FDI 27, the upper left second molar. If you have wisdom teeth, there is one more behind it. If you do not, the seven is your last tooth on that side — which is why sevens matter out of proportion to their fame.

Palmer: a diagram you can write

Palmer notation, still common among British dentists and orthodontists, does the same job pictorially. Imagine the mouth drawn as a cross seen from the front: the vertical stroke is the midline, the horizontal stroke is the bite. Each tooth is written as its number, 1 to 8, with a right-angle bracket showing which of the four sectors it lives in. Primary teeth use letters A to E instead of digits.

The bracket is the drawing. 7┘ has its lines below and to the right, so it sits in the sector above and to the left of the centre — the patient's upper right. └7 mirrors it into the upper left. It is elegant on paper and a nuisance in a database, which is one reason FDI won.

Universal: one to thirty-two

The American system ignores quadrants and just counts, starting at the patient's upper right third molar as number 1 and running right around the upper arch to the upper left third molar as 16. It then drops to the lower left third molar as 17 and runs back around to finish at the lower right third molar, 32. Primary teeth are lettered A to T on the same circuit. It is easy to say and impossible to reason with: nothing about "number 15" tells you what kind of tooth it is.

And the surfaces

The tooth number locates the tooth; a second code locates the damage on it. Each tooth has five named surfaces: occlusal (the biting surface), mesial (facing towards the midline), distal (facing away from it), buccal or labial (facing the cheek or lip) and lingual or palatal (facing the tongue or the palate). Fillings are named by the surfaces they occupy, so an "MO" is a filling on the mesial and occlusal surfaces, and an "MOD" wraps over the top from one side to the other — which, as Chapter 6 explains, is a much more serious loss of structure than the extra letter suggests.

Go deeperwhy three systems still exist

Palmer notation dates from the 1870s and predates the others; it was designed for a paper chart drawn as a diagram of the mouth, and it is genuinely faster to read at a glance. Its weakness is typographical: the brackets are not ordinary characters, so it survives handwriting far better than it survives software.

The Universal system was adopted by the American Dental Association and is entrenched in American practice, insurance coding and teaching. Its virtue is that a tooth has exactly one identifier with no symbol attached; its cost is that the identifier carries no anatomical meaning.

FDI two-digit notation was adopted in 1970 and is now ISO 3950. It is unambiguous, spoken easily, sorts and stores cleanly, and encodes both quadrant and tooth type in two characters — which is why it is the international standard and why this guide uses it.

Posterior composite longevityannual failure ≈1–3%; 10-year survival ≈70–90% Operator, patient risk and tooth size matter more than brand. Demarco et al., Dent Mater 2012; Opdam et al., J Dent Res 2014.
Posterior amalgam longevityannual failure ≈1–3%; 10-year survival ≈80–90% A Cochrane review found composite restorations failed roughly twice as often as amalgam in the trials available, on low-quality evidence. Rasines Alcaraz et al., Cochrane 2014; Opdam et al. 2014.
Glass ionomer longevity in load-bearing posterior teeth not confidently sourced Shorter than amalgam or composite in stress-bearing sites, but published survival estimates vary too much by material generation and study design to quote a single range honestly. This guide describes it qualitatively.
Single crown survival5-year ≈90–96%; 10-year ≈85–90% Varies by material and by tooth. Pjetursson et al. 2007; Sailer et al., Dent Mater 2015 (systematic reviews).
Root canal treatment, radiographic success≈68–85% (strict criteria); ≈75–90% (loose criteria) "Success" means healing of the lesion at the root tip, for treatments completed at least a year before review; switching from complete healing (strict) to complete-or-improving healing (loose) moves the estimate by roughly five to ten points. Ng et al., Int Endod J 2007–08 (systematic review and meta-analysis; pooled ≈74.7% strict, ≈85.2% loose).
Tooth survival after root canal treatment≈86–93% at 2–10 years; ≈97% retained at 8 years in a large insurance dataset Survival — the tooth still in the mouth — is a different and kinder measure than healing. Ng et al., Int Endod J 2010; Salehrabi & Rotstein, J Endod 2004 (over 1.4 million teeth).
Cuspal coverage after root canal treatmentuncrowned teeth lost ≈6× as often Retrospective cohort, so the crowned teeth may have started out in better condition. Aquilino & Caplan, J Prosthet Dent 2002.
Dental implant survival≈93–97% at 10 years Survival of the fixture, not freedom from complications. Moraschini et al., Int J Oral Maxillofac Surg 2015 (systematic review; pooled ≈94.6% at ten years or more).
Peri-implantitis prevalence≈20–22% of patients (reported range far wider) Estimates swing enormously with the case definition used. Derks & Tomasi, J Clin Periodontol 2015 (meta-analysis).
Dry socket incidence≈1–5% of routine extractions; up to ≈30% after surgical removal of impacted lower third molars Wide variation between studies and definitions. Standard oral surgery texts.
Radiographic detection threshold for caries≈30–40% mineral loss A lesion must lose roughly this much mineral before it appears on a radiograph, which is why radiographic lesions are always older and deeper than they look. Standard cariology texts; the exact figure is method-dependent.
Time for an approximal enamel lesion to reach dentine median ≈3–5 years in adolescent cohorts, with very wide individual variation Reported medians differ substantially between populations and eras. Mejàre et al., Caries Res; longitudinal Scandinavian cohorts. Treat as an order of magnitude, not a prediction.
Maximal mouth opening≈40–60 mm; below ≈35 mm usually called trismus Interincisal distance. Standard oral surgery texts.
Rate of secondary dentine depositionnot confidently sourced Deposition continues throughout life and progressively narrows the pulp chamber — this is well established — but published rates vary by tooth, site and method too much for a single figure. Chapter 4 shows the effect, not a rate.
Enamel lost per individual acid attacknot confidently sourced Sub-micrometre per challenge, and utterly dependent on the acid, the exposure time, the saliva and the surface. This guide describes the direction of the process rather than quoting a depth.
Go deeperhow to read a survival number

Three traps sit inside every restoration-survival figure. The first is the definition of failure: one study counts a replaced filling, another counts a repaired one, a third counts only extraction. Ranges that look like disagreement about materials are often disagreement about words.

The second is selection. Teeth that get crowned are chosen for crowning; teeth left uncrowned were often the ones the dentist judged sound enough to leave. A retrospective study comparing them is comparing two decisions as much as two treatments, which is why the six-fold figure for cuspal coverage should be read as a strong signal rather than a measured effect size.

The third is the operator. In the composite-longevity literature the spread between individual practitioners and practices is comparable to the spread between materials. The headline number is a property of a health system as much as of a resin.

A short history of getting this far

Most of what a modern dentist does was invented within the last hundred and fifty years, and several of the load-bearing ideas — bonding to enamel, integrating titanium into bone, cleaning a canal with a superelastic file — are within living memory. Before that there was two and a half thousand years of skilled, inventive, mostly hopeless craft.

Dentistry, in seventeen steps
WhenWhat happenedWhy it mattered
c. 700–500 BC Etruscan goldsmiths band replacement teeth into gold strips fitted to the surviving teeth The earliest known dental prosthetics. Nothing here treats disease — it is jewellery solving a functional and social problem, and it is genuinely good work.
Mediaeval Europe Extraction becomes the trade of barber-surgeons; in England the Company of Barber-Surgeons is chartered in 1540 For centuries the whole repertoire was pulling the tooth out. Pain relief was alcohol, and the "tooth worm" was still a serious explanation for decay.
1728 Pierre Fauchard publishes Le Chirurgien Dentiste in Paris The founding document of the profession. Fauchard described caries, fillings, dentures and tooth straightening in systematic detail, dismissed the tooth worm, and — crucially — published rather than guarded his methods.
1820s–1830s Amalgam reaches Europe and then America A material that could be packed into a cavity and set hard, cheaply. It provoked the "amalgam war": the first American dental society made its members pledge not to use it, and eventually broke apart over the argument.
1840 The Baltimore College of Dental Surgery opens The world's first dental school. Dentistry stops being an apprenticed craft and starts being a taught discipline with entry standards.
1844–1846 Horace Wells uses nitrous oxide; William Morton demonstrates ether at Massachusetts General Hospital General anaesthesia, introduced by two dentists. Surgery of every kind became thinkable — arguably dentistry's largest single contribution to medicine.
1884–1905 Koller uses cocaine as a local anaesthetic; nerve blocks follow within months; procaine is synthesised in 1905 The change that made everything else possible. Cutting a tooth is only tolerable if the tooth is numb, and until this point it was not.
1895–1896 Röntgen discovers X-rays in November 1895; the first dental radiographs follow within weeks The inside of a tooth and the bone around its roots became visible. Every diagnosis in Chapter 5 depends on this.
1895–1908 Greene Vardiman Black standardises the amalgam formula and publishes his cavity classification and Operative Dentistry Dentistry acquires a shared vocabulary, reproducible instruments and rules for cutting a cavity. His "extension for prevention" — cutting well beyond the disease into cleansable areas — governed practice for most of a century before being reversed.
1901–1942 Frederick McKay investigates "Colorado brown stain"; H. Trendley Dean maps mottling against decay across dozens of towns A forty-year detective story that identified fluoride as the agent behind both the staining and the striking absence of decay that came with it.
1945 Grand Rapids, Michigan, adds fluoride to its water supply on 25 January The first community water fluoridation scheme, and the start of the largest decline in caries ever recorded — alongside, and hard to disentangle from, fluoride toothpaste.
1955 Michael Buonocore publishes the acid-etch technique Etching enamel with phosphoric acid creates a microscopically rough surface that resin grips. This one page opened the adhesive era: fillings that stick to the tooth instead of being wedged into a shape cut to trap them.
1962 Rafael Bowen formulates the Bis-GMA resin behind modern composites Gave the adhesive technique something worth sticking down — a filled resin strong enough for a load-bearing restoration.
1952–1965 Per-Ingvar Brånemark observes that titanium fuses to bone, and places the first titanium dental implant in a patient in 1965 Osseointegration. A lost tooth could be replaced with something anchored in bone rather than resting on gum or hanging off neighbouring teeth. Acceptance took until the 1980s.
1988–1990s Nickel-titanium is proposed for root canal files; rotary NiTi systems reach general practice through the 1990s Superelastic files follow a curved canal instead of straightening it. Root canal treatment became faster, more predictable and far less dependent on hand skill alone.
1990s–2000s Minimal intervention dentistry: selective caries removal, sealed lesions, standardised detection scoring Black's rule is reversed. If decay can be arrested or sealed in, cutting it out is no longer automatically correct — the surviving tooth structure is now understood as the scarcest resource in the mouth.
Now Digital scanning and chairside milling, cone-beam CT, adhesive onlays instead of crowns, vital pulp therapy in adult teeth The direction of travel is consistent: cut less, keep the pulp alive longer, and treat the tooth as something to be conserved rather than progressively replaced.

Sources, and what this guide is not

This is a lay explainer built on standard textbooks and published reviews. It is not a systematic review, it was not peer reviewed, and it is not a clinical resource. Where the evidence is strong it says so; where clinicians genuinely disagree it says that too; and where a figure could not be traced to a source worth trusting, the table above says "not confidently sourced" rather than offering a plausible-sounding number.

The anatomy, histology and developmental biology throughout lean on Ten Cate's Oral Histology (Nanci) and Orban's Oral Histology and Embryology. The pulp chapters — diagnosis, pulpitis, necrosis, and everything about root canal treatment — lean on Cohen's Pathways of the Pulp and on the European Society of Endodontology's quality guidelines for endodontic treatment. Cariology follows Kidd and Fejerskov's Dental Caries: The Disease and its Clinical Management. Material properties come from Craig's Restorative Dental Materials.

Clinical practice and prevention are checked against NICE guidance and the Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme (SDCEP) guidance, both of which are freely published and are the better starting point for anyone who wants the actual standards of care rather than an explanation of them. Effectiveness claims come from Cochrane systematic reviews where one exists, and survival figures from the published longevity meta-analyses named row by row in the numbers table — principally Ng and colleagues on endodontic outcomes, Demarco and Opdam and colleagues on restoration longevity, Pjetursson and Sailer and colleagues on crowns, and Moraschini and Derks and Tomasi on implants.

Still not medical advice

Everything in these nine chapters describes how teeth and treatments work in general. None of it can tell you what is happening in your own mouth, and none of the ranges here predicts what a particular tooth will do. If something hurts, see a dentist. If there is facial swelling, fever, or any difficulty swallowing or breathing, that is an emergency and needs urgent care rather than an appointment.

That is the end of the guide. A tooth is the hardest thing your body builds, the only organ it cannot repair, and the one part of you that keeps a permanent, unforgiving record of everything that has ever happened to it. Everything in these nine chapters follows from those three facts.
