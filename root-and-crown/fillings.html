CHAPTER 6

The Restoration Cycle

Nothing a tooth has lost is ever rebuilt. Enamel cannot regrow, so the only available move is to cut the hole bigger, then pack it. That buys a decade or two, until the plug fails at its edge — and the next one must be larger than the last. Here are the materials, how each dies, and the one-way ratchet that follows.

The word "filling" does a lot of quiet work. It sounds like topping something up: a hole appeared, we filled it in. The operation is closer to a controlled excavation, and much of what comes out was never diseased.

A hole cut deliberately larger than the disease

Three things force the cavity wider than the lesion looked. Softened dentine has to go: you cannot bond to mush, or seal against an advancing front. Undermined enamel has to go too — enamel with no dentine beneath it chips at the first hard bite. And because decay spreads sideways along the junction between the two tissues, a pinhead of damage on the surface has usually hollowed out a far wider area below. Some materials must also be mechanically trapped: a shape wider at its base than its opening, cut whether the disease went there or not.

For most of the twentieth century that logic went further. Greene Vardiman Black, whose Operative Dentistry of 1908 gave the profession its cavity classification — Class I for pits and fissures through Class V for the necks — also gave it extension for prevention: push the margins out until they sit where a brush and a cheek keep them clean, because a margin in a stagnation groove decays again. With no fluoride, no adhesives and a material that had to be wedged in place, that was rational. It also meant a pinhead lesion could justify a preparation the width of the tooth.

The reaction was a return to first principles: caries is not something to excise to a clean margin, but a disease of the biofilm that happens to leave a hole. Soft dentine still comes off the walls, where a sealable margin matters. But in a deep lesion, firmer stained affected dentine is now deliberately left over the pulp rather than chased out at the risk of exposing the nerve — sealed off, it loses its supply line and goes quiet. The International Caries Consensus Collaboration set this out in Advances in Dental Research in 2016: selective removal to firm dentine, not to hard dentine everywhere.

The trade at the heart of it

Every millimetre you cut makes the restoration more retentive and easier to seal — and the tooth permanently weaker. No setting of that dial is free.

Four materials, four different bargains

Amalgam is an alloy of silver, tin and copper mixed with liquid mercury, roughly half the set material by weight. It is strong in bulk, forgiving of a damp field, and it has one elegant property: it corrodes, and its own corrosion products silt up the microscopic gap at its margin, so an amalgam's seal can improve over its first years. High-copper alloys, standard since the 1970s, removed the weak phase that made older amalgams crumble at the edges. But it sticks to nothing: retention is purely mechanical — undercuts, grooves, dovetails, and the sound tooth they consume.

On mercury, plainly

The major reviews — the European Commission's SCENIHR opinion of 2015, and assessments by the World Health Organization and the US Food and Drug Administration — have not found evidence that dental amalgam harms patients at the exposures involved. The FDA nonetheless advised in 2020 that some groups, including pregnant women and young children, might avoid new amalgam where an alternative exists: a precaution, not a finding of injury.

The phase-down is a different argument. It runs under the Minamata Convention on Mercury, a treaty about mercury as a persistent global pollutant, and lists amalgam for progressive reduction rather than prohibition. The European Union has since ended routine use. Environmental policy and patient toxicity are separate cases, and they get conflated constantly, in both directions.

Composite resin made minimal intervention possible, because it sticks. Buonocore showed in 1955 that etching enamel with acid — today about 35% phosphoric acid — leaves a porous surface that liquid resin locks into; on dentine the equivalent trick builds a hybrid layer, resin infiltrating demineralised collagen and setting inside it. A bonded restoration needs no trap, so the outline can follow the disease. But polymers occupy less space than their monomers — typically 1.5% to 3% by volume — and in a cavity bonded on every wall that contraction cannot flow away, so it becomes stress pulling on the bond and the cusps. Hence incremental placement, and hence post-operative sensitivity. Composite is unforgiving of moisture too: saliva on etched enamel at the wrong moment wrecks the bond, invisibly.

Glass ionomer runs on different chemistry: an acid–base reaction between a fluoroaluminosilicate glass and a polyalkenoic acid. It bonds chemically to the calcium in tooth mineral without etching, and releases fluoride. It is also weaker, more brittle and more soluble — which makes it excellent precisely where the others struggle: root-surface lesions, a child who cannot hold still, a field that will not stay dry. Resin-modified versions add resin for strength. How much that fluoride prevents decay at a real margin is a place where the evidence is thinner than the marketing.

Finally the indirect options: gold and ceramic inlays and onlays, made outside the mouth and cemented in. Because they cure before they arrive, no shrinkage happens inside the tooth, and the margin can be finished to tens of micrometres. Gold is the kindest material to the tooth biting against it; ceramic is harder than enamel, so it wears that tooth instead.

Miss Molaris, on material choice

"Patients ask which filling lasts longest, and it is the wrong question by about two orders of magnitude. The size of the hole and the mouth it lives in swamp the material every time. No meta-analysis will rescue you from that."

Everything fails at the edge

Fillings almost never fail in the middle; they fail at the join. A margin is the interface between two substances with different stiffnesses, thermal expansions and appetites for water, held together by a bond or by friction, in warm salt water, loaded a few hundred times a day and cycled between ice cream and coffee. A gap of a few micrometres is not a defect; it is the expected outcome.

Microleakage is what passes through that gap: fluid, ions and, once it is wide enough, bacteria. Older teaching treated the leak itself as the disease. The modern reading is sharper — secondary caries is no special affliction of restorations. It is ordinary caries, at the one spot on the tooth that holds plaque best and is hardest to clean and see. Same chemistry as Chapter 3; different geography.

Roughly in order of how often they send a restoration to be redone:

  • Secondary caries at the margin — consistently the most-cited reason for replacement in practice-based surveys.
  • Fracture — of the material, or of the tooth. In a wide restoration it is usually the cusp that goes, not the filling.
  • Marginal breakdown and wear — the edge chips and "ditches", building the plaque trap that produces the first item on this list.
  • Debonding — a restoration that comes loose while perfectly intact, its dentine bond quietly hydrolysed.
  • Post-operative sensitivity — the one that fails immediately rather than eventually: shrinkage stress, or an incomplete seal.

One more failure mode is not in the tooth. Shown the same restorations, different dentists disagree strikingly about which need replacing. Redoing a filling is a judgement under uncertainty — and it triggers everything that follows.

How long, honestly

The unit the literature uses is the annual failure rate: the share of surviving restorations that fail each year. The meta-analysis of posterior composites by Opdam and colleagues (Journal of Dental Research, 2014) pooled rates of roughly 1.8% over five years and 2.4% over ten. Reviews put both amalgam and composite in the low single digits, with individual studies scattered far wider.

Compound those and the picture is neither as good nor as bad as people assume. At 2% a year, 82 restorations in 100 survive ten years and 67 survive twenty. At 5% — a large one in a high-risk mouth — it is 60 and 36. Same material, two different worlds.

Go deeperwhere the numbers come from, and where they disagree

Two bodies of evidence point opposite ways. The Cochrane review of randomised trials (Rasines Alcaraz and colleagues, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2014) found direct composites carried roughly twice the failure risk of amalgam in posterior teeth, driven mainly by secondary caries — while grading the evidence low quality and noting the trials were largely in children's teeth. Practice-based cohorts of adults, notably from Opdam's group, found composite doing at least as well. Both can be true: they follow different patients, cavities and eras of material.

What survives every dataset is the ranking of predictors. Demarco and colleagues titled their 2012 review in Dental Materials "not only a matter of materials" for a reason: surfaces restored, caries risk, heavy grinding and the operator all outweigh the choice of material.

Note what "failure" means. These studies count re-intervention, folding the clinician's judgement into the outcome — a real limitation, and one reason the spread is so wide.

Set size to 1 surface and risk low, then move to 4 surfaces and high. That gap dwarfs the gap between any two materials.

The restoration cycle

Here is the part that reframes everything above. When a filling is replaced, the new one is necessarily larger. Not usually. Not on average. Necessarily.

Two forces guarantee it. The old material has to come out, and no instrument can tell material from tooth at their shared boundary, so removing the one takes a rim of the other. And the reason for replacing it — decay at a margin, a chipped edge — sits outside the old outline by definition, so the new outline must enclose it. Robert Elderton named this the restorative cycle; Brantley and colleagues found the same one-way widening in the Journal of the American Dental Association in 1995. How much larger the next one is varies with the tooth and the reason for redoing it. That it is larger does not.

The consequence is structural. Reeh, Messer and Douglas measured the stiffness of extracted teeth as they cut them (Journal of Endodontics, 1989): a conservative occlusal cavity cost roughly a fifth of the tooth's cuspal stiffness, while extending it into an MOD — a slot running right across the tooth — cost about 63%. The access cavity for a root canal, which patients dread, cost around 5%. It is the width of the preparation, not the depth of treatment, that turns a tooth into two cantilevered cusps.

The ratchet

Small filling → larger filling → larger still → cuspal coverage → crown → and, if the cutting or the caries reaches the pulp, root canal treatment and a crown after it → and finally too little structure above the gum to hold anything at all.

Nobody chooses this path. It is what emerges when a tissue that cannot regenerate meets a repair with a service life: subtraction, run at roughly a step a decade, inside a lifetime long enough to run it several times.

Which is why watching is sometimes the treatment

If replacement is a ratchet, the decision to replace is bigger than it feels in the chair. It is not "this filling for a fresh one". It is "this filling, plus a rim of tooth I will never get back, for one that will also fail". Two consequences follow.

The first is repair instead of replacement: if one corner has failed, cut out that corner and patch it. It preserves tissue, and reported outcomes compare well with wholesale replacement. The second is monitoring instead of intervention. Marginal staining is not caries; a slightly ditched amalgam edge is common and often stable for years. Telling an untidy margin from an active one — over time, with radiographs — is much of what an examination is for.

Not advice about your tooth

None of this is a rule anyone can apply to their own mouth from a web page: whether a margin is stable or active needs someone who can see it. The point here is narrower. When a dentist says "let us keep an eye on that", they may be making the more conservative call rather than the lazier one — because the alternative spends something that cannot be earned back.

The tooth that keeps its small filling for thirty years is not lucky. It is one on which somebody repeatedly declined to start the next turn. The tooth that runs the cycle all the way down arrives where the pulp is breached and the whole calculation changes — which is where Chapter 7 picks it up.
