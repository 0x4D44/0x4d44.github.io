CHAPTER 7

Root Canal

Almost everything people believe about root canal treatment is about the wrong organ. It is not really an operation on a nerve. It is a disinfection problem: a branching, invisible space inside the tooth has been colonised by bacteria the immune system can no longer reach, and the treatment empties it, cleans walls no instrument can touch and seals it so nothing can live there again.

The hollow core of a molar is narrower than the lead in a propelling pencil, curved, oval rather than round, and it branches near the bottom like a river delta. It has just lost its blood supply.

The target is a habitat, not a nerve

People call it "having the nerve taken out". That describes a side effect. By the time a tooth needs treatment the pulp is dying or dead — Chapter 5 follows that process — and dead tissue in a warm sealed chamber is a culture medium.

Then comes the fact that defines the specialty. Immune defences travel in blood, and so do antibiotics. A canal whose blood supply has died is, from the body's point of view, outside the body — white cells cannot patrol it, and antibiotics reach the tissue around the root tip but not the space inside. That is why antibiotics can settle a facial swelling and leave the tooth as infected as before. The bacteria have to be evicted by hand.

The aim, precisely stated

Remove the infected tissue. Disinfect the walls. Shape the space so it can be filled. Then seal it, top and bottom, so nothing survives inside and nothing new gets back in.

Notice what is not on that list: sterility.

Three stages that carry the weight

The whole sequence is in the figure above. Three parts of it are worth arguing about.

The rubber dam is not a nicety. A sheet of latex or nitrile over a clamp, isolating one tooth, does three jobs. It keeps saliva out — and saliva carries the very organisms being evicted, so without it every swallow re-inoculates the space just cleaned. It stops an instrument being swallowed or, far worse, inhaled. And it makes it safe to flood the tooth with sodium hypochlorite, which is bleach. The European Society of Endodontology and the American Association of Endodontists both treat it as a requirement, not a preference.

Working length has to be exact: short of the end leaves a reservoir of infected tissue, past it pushes debris into bone and hurts. The target is the apical constriction, the narrowest point, which sits typically half a millimetre to a millimetre short of the radiographic tip, and whose exit hole often opens on the side of the root rather than at its point — Kuttler's 1955 study established both. An apex locator finds it electrically; a radiograph confirms it.

Shaping is enabling work, not cleaning: the taper exists so irrigant can be delivered deep and exchanged there. Rotary files are nickel-titanium, superelastic at mouth temperature, so they bend around a curve and spring back rather than staying bent — which is what lets a rotating instrument follow a curve instead of straightening it.

Irrigation is the heart of it

Here is the fact that reorganises everything. Files touch only part of the wall. Scanning instrumented molars by micro-computed tomography, Peters, Schönenberger and Laib found that all four preparation techniques they tested left 35% or more of the canal surface unchanged, and later micro-CT work on the flattened mesial canals of lower molars reports untouched fractions above half. A canal is not a drilled hole — it is a ribbon, or two channels joined by a thin web called an isthmus. A rotating instrument is a circle, and a circle inside a ribbon leaves fins.

So the instruments shape and the irrigant cleans.

Sodium hypochlorite is the workhorse, used clinically at concentrations between roughly 0.5% and 6%. It is the only common irrigant that dissolves organic tissue: it does not merely kill what it can reach, it digests the pulp remnants and biofilm in the fins, isthmuses and lateral branches no file will ever enter. It is also why the dam is non-negotiable — hypochlorite pushed past the root tip causes a severe chemical injury.

EDTA, typically at 17%, does the other half. Files smear the wall with a paste of cut dentine, pulp fragments and bacteria — the smear layer — which plugs the dentinal tubules and shields whatever is inside. EDTA is a chelator: it takes up calcium and dissolves the mineral part of that sludge, which hypochlorite cannot touch.

Chlorhexidine, usually at 2%, is a recognised alternative or adjunct: broad-spectrum, and substantive — it binds to dentine and keeps working after the appointment. But it does not dissolve tissue, so swapping hypochlorite for it trades away the one property that reaches where instruments cannot.

Where the work runs over two visits, the canals are dressed between them with calcium hydroxide, an alkali at around pH 12.5 that is hostile to most of the organisms involved. Whether two visits beat one has a clear answer: a Cochrane review found no difference in radiological failure, with single-visit treatment associated with slightly more swelling and painkiller use.

Obturation fills the space. A gutta-percha cone is only about a fifth actual gutta-percha polymer, the rest mostly zinc oxide with radiopacifiers and waxes, and it seals nothing by itself. It is a core; the seal is made by the sealer, a thin cement that flows into the irregularities the cone cannot occupy, entombing survivors without nutrients.

Why the seal at the top matters as much as the one at the bottom

Last, the access cavity is closed with a definitive material rather than a temporary left for months — a step routinely treated as an afterthought. Endodontics spent decades on the apical end of the root filling before a 1995 study by Ray and Trope pointed at the other one. They graded the root filling and the restoration of a thousand teeth separately, then counted how often the bone around the root tip was healthy.

The order of those four numbers is the finding. A poor root filling under a good restoration did better than a good root filling under a poor one, because a leaking crown lets the mouth back down into the canal.

Go deeperthe argument about coronal leakage

The figures come from Ray HA & Trope M, International Endodontic Journal, 1995.

They have been challenged. Ricucci, Grøndahl and Bergenholtz (2000) examined root-filled teeth whose restorations had been lost or destroyed by decay, found their periapical status was no worse, and concluded that the root filling dominates. The defensible reading is that both seals matter, and that a well-obturated canal is more forgiving of leakage than the 1995 paper implied.

Why it is hard

Canal systems are not tubes. A cleared, dye-injected tooth looks like a root system: lateral canals branching sideways, most in the last few millimetres; apical deltas where one canal fans into several tiny exits; isthmuses joining two canals inside one root; oval cross-sections; and curvature in two planes that a flat radiograph flattens into one. Clearing studies find accessory canals in a quarter to a third of teeth.

Then there is the canal that is simply missed. In upper first molars the mesiobuccal root usually holds two canals rather than one; the second — universally MB2 — hides under a shelf of dentine a millimetre or so palatal to the obvious orifice. Prevalence rises the harder you look: pooled clinical series in Cleghorn, Christie and Dong's review put it near 55–60%, while laboratory sectioning and micro-CT studies commonly report 80–95%. Hoen and Pink found untreated canals in over 40% of teeth sent for retreatment. Leave one behind and you have done all the work and kept the habitat.

At the other extreme, canals close down. A pulp under long-term irritation lays down secondary and tertiary dentine — Chapter 4 covers it — until the chamber is a slit and the canals are threads, harder to find and harder to clean.

Sterility is not the goal, because it is not achievable

No realistic technique sterilises a root canal system. In a histobacteriological study by Nair and colleagues of mandibular molars treated in one visit, bacteria were still demonstrable in the apical canal system of 14 of the 16 roots examined — after technically satisfactory treatment.

The goal is to cut the load far enough, and starve the survivors thoroughly enough, that the tissues at the root tip win. Healing is done by the body.

Does it work?

Yes, usually — but two endpoints are in circulation and they do not measure the same thing. Healed means radiographic resolution: the dark patch of bone destruction has filled in. Survival means the tooth is still in the mouth doing its job, whatever the radiograph shows. Quoting one while implying the other is how "root canals are 95% successful" gets about.

Miss Molaris, on the actual job

"People think I am taking something out. Mostly I am putting something in — bleach, a few millilitres at a time, for most of an hour. Strep here does not care how elegant my files are. He cares whether there is anywhere left to live."

"The files are the plumbing. The irrigant is the eviction notice."

The brittleness myth

Root-filled teeth do fracture more than others, and the explanation everybody has heard is that the dentine has "died and dried out". The evidence does not support it.

Papa, Cain and Messer measured the moisture content of root-filled teeth against vital ones and found no meaningful difference. Sedgley and Messer compared root-treated teeth with their own contralateral vital partners in the same mouths and found no significant difference in the mechanical properties tested. Dentine that has lost its pulp is not measurably different dentine.

What has changed is how much of it is left. The decisive experiment is Reeh, Messer and Douglas (1989), who measured cuspal stiffness as structure was removed step by step. An endodontic access cavity cost about 5% of it. A two-surface cavity that removed both marginal ridges cost about 63%. The procedure is nearly irrelevant; the carpentry around it is everything.

The real mechanism

A molar resists splitting because its walls are joined all the way round, and the marginal ridges — the raised rims front and back — are the tie-beams. Decay took some, the old filling took more, the access cavity took the roof. What is left is two unsupported cusps hinged at the bottom, and every bite wedges them apart.

So the answer is cuspal coverage, which holds the cusps together rather than sitting between them. In Aquilino and Caplan's study, root-filled teeth that were not crowned were lost at roughly six times the rate of those that were — which is Chapter 8.

When it does not work

Failure announces itself as persistent tenderness to biting, a sinus tract on the gum that discharges and reseals, or a lesion that has not shrunk on a radiograph a year later. Bone refills over months, so the judgement is unhurried.

The usual next step is retreatment: dismantling the restoration, removing the old filling material, finding whatever was missed and redoing the disinfection. It succeeds a little less often than first-time treatment — the cases that come back are the difficult ones by definition.

Where that is impossible or has failed, the fallback is surgical: apicoectomy, or root-end resection. The root tip is exposed through the gum, the last few millimetres are cut off, and a small filling is placed into the cut end from outside. Meta-analyses by Setzer and colleagues comparing traditional root-end surgery with modern endodontic microsurgery — high magnification, ultrasonic preparation, biocompatible materials — report pooled success around 59% for the old approach against roughly 90–94% for the new.

The reputation problem

No other dental procedure is a punchline. "I'd rather have a root canal" is a fossil of an idiom: it preserves a treatment carried out before reliable local anaesthesia, before magnification, before nickel-titanium, before apex locators and before anybody understood that the job was bacteriological rather than mechanical.

The modern version is, for most people, an unremarkable long appointment. Most of the pain associated with root canal treatment is the pain that brought the person in, and it falls steeply afterwards. Among dental treatments it is unusual: more often than not it takes somebody out of severe pain rather than putting them into it.

A reminder

This chapter describes how the procedure works, not what anybody should do about a particular tooth. Every figure quoted is a population range from published studies, and none of them predicts an individual outcome.
