CHAPTER 5

Death of a Pulp

A tooth can hurt more than a broken bone, and the reason is architectural rather than poetic. This chapter follows an inflamed pulp from the first twinge to the abscess: why a rigid chamber turns ordinary swelling into agony, how clinicians sort the stages, why cold sometimes stops the pain of a dying nerve, why anaesthetic and antibiotics both disappoint, and why a mouthful of cold water can beat a full dose of painkillers. It ends with a real radiograph — and with the honest limits of what a radiograph can show.

Inside a tooth the size of your little fingernail sits a chamber of soft tissue about the volume of a grain of rice. Inflame it and it tries to do what inflamed tissue everywhere does: swell. It cannot. It is walled in on every side by dentine, and the pressure that follows is the whole story of this chapter.

Everywhere else, swelling is the relief valve. Sprain an ankle and the tissue puffs outward into loose connective space; the pressure inside stays near normal because the container gives. The pulp has no give. Its walls are mineralised dentine and its only exit is a hole at the root tip a fraction of a millimetre across. Engineers call that low-compliance: add volume and the pressure climbs at once.

That feedback is the mechanism. Inflammation raises tissue pressure; the pressure squeezes the very venules feeding the tissue; the starved tissue releases more mediators; the pressure climbs again. It is a compartment syndrome running inside a space you could balance on a fingertip.

Everything follows from the box

The severity: pressure has nowhere to go, so the nerve endings are loaded directly. The throbbing in time with your pulse: every heartbeat delivers another bolus of blood the chamber cannot take. Why it is worse lying down: going flat raises blood pressure at the head, and with it the pressure inside the pulp. And the tendency to run to completion: past a point, removing the original irritation no longer stops it.

Go deeperthe measured pressures, and where the classic story overstates itself

Resting pulpal tissue pressure is usually quoted around 10 mmHg, with published values spanning roughly 5–20 mmHg; Van Hassel's 1971 measurements in human pulp (Oral Surgery, Oral Medicine, Oral Pathology) are the classic source. Localised rises of the order of 35 mmHg have been recorded in acutely inflamed pulp. The exit is correspondingly tight: the apical constriction is commonly reported at about 0.2–0.4 mm across, after Kuttler's 1955 study of root apices.

Now the caveat, because the textbook cartoon is too tidy. Heyeraas and Berggreen's review of pulpal interstitial pressure (Critical Reviews in Oral Biology and Medicine, 1999) showed the rise is regional, not global: it stays local to the inflamed segment, and the pulp has partial defences, including increased lymphatic drainage. A pulp does not strangle itself all at once. It dies in patches — which is why partial necrosis is real. The compartment argument has the direction right; it overstates the speed.

Reversible, irreversible, and the words in between

Clinicians do not grade toothache by how much it hurts. They grade it by behaviour, because behaviour tracks the biology, and two questions do most of the work: did something set it off, and how long did it last afterwards?

Reversible pulpitis is provoked and brief: cold or sweet triggers a sharp jolt that stops a second or two after the stimulus goes. The pulp is irritated but its circulation is intact, so removing the cause can let it settle. Irreversible pulpitis is spontaneous and lingering. It arrives unprovoked, keeps going for minutes after the trigger has gone, worsens lying down, and wakes people at three in the morning. The name is a promise about the future rather than a description of the present: this pulp will not recover on its own.

Then comes necrosis, first partial and then total, and it can be deceptively peaceful — a dying pulp often stops hurting, and patients understandably read that as recovery. It is not. Bacteria work down the canal and out of the apical foramen into the ligament and bone beyond, which is apical periodontitis. From there the routes diverge: contained pus is an abscess; pus that finds a way out through the gum becomes a sinus tract, which relieves the pressure and, alarmingly, the pain; infection spreading through the soft tissue planes of the face is cellulitis, and that is the dangerous one.

Go deeperhow well do the labels match the tissue?

The names describe symptoms; the treatment decision depends on the histology; the two do not correspond perfectly. Seltzer and Bender's 1963 work compared clinical diagnoses with what the pulp looked like down a microscope and found the agreement poor. Ricucci and colleagues repeated it with modern criteria (Journal of Endodontics, 2014) and did much better: diagnosis matched histology in the region of 85% or more of teeth. Better — but a minority of pulps are not in the state their symptoms advertise.

The threshold is moving too. Trials of vital pulp therapy — removing only the diseased coronal pulp and dressing what remains — report encouraging medium-term results in mature teeth diagnosed as irreversibly inflamed. "Irreversible" describes untreated natural history, not a verdict on what can be rescued.

The cold paradox

Here is the strangest sign in dentistry, and the one that most often convinces a patient their tooth is not merely sore but seriously wrong. In a vital, inflamed pulp, cold provokes: the classic ice-water jolt that lingers. In a pulp that is necrosing, that can reverse. Cold begins to relieve the pain, sometimes dramatically, and heat sets it off. People arrive at surgeries holding a cup of iced water, sipping continuously and looking faintly embarrassed. It is not a quirk. It is a sign.

The usual explanation is mechanical rather than neurological. A partly necrotic pulp generates gas and inflammatory fluid inside a chamber that is now effectively sealed. Gases expand when warmed and contract when cooled, so heat raises the pressure in the chamber and cold lowers it — and pressure, in a low-compliance box, is what the surviving nerve endings report. Be precise about the status of that account: it is a long-standing, plausible explanation set out in the standard endodontic texts, not something proven in patients. The clinical association is not in doubt. Relief with cold is a recognised sign of a dying or partly dead pulp.

Why the tooth gets a name only at the end

Ask someone with early toothache which tooth it is and they are usually wrong, often by several teeth, sometimes by the wrong jaw. The pulp holds nociceptive fibres and essentially no proprioceptors, and many teeth funnel into shared branches of the trigeminal nerve — so the brain receives a loud signal with no return address. Chapter 2 covers the wiring.

The periodontal ligament is different. It is packed with mechanoreceptors, because its day job is telling you how hard you are biting and on what. So the moment inflammation crosses the apex into the ligament, the pain acquires an address: the tooth becomes tender to bite, tender to tap, and suddenly obvious. That transition is diagnostically enormous and clinically ironic. The tooth becomes identifiable at precisely the point the pulp inside it is beyond saving.

Why dentists ask about swelling and swallowing

Most dental infection stays local. A minority spreads through the fascial planes of the face and neck, and that is a different category of problem. Spreading facial swelling, fever, swelling in the floor of the mouth, difficulty swallowing, difficulty opening the mouth or any difficulty breathing are medical emergencies, not toothache. The worst has a name: Ludwig's angina, a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the floor of the mouth that lifts the tongue and can close the airway. Hence the triage questions that look disproportionate. They are not about the tooth.

Why the injection often fails

A "hot" pulp — an acutely inflamed one — is notoriously hard to numb. This is well documented; it is not the patient imagining things or the clinician missing the nerve. In mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, a standard inferior alveolar nerve block alone achieves profound pulpal anaesthesia in only a minority of cases in published trials — figures cluster broadly in the 15–50% range, against roughly 80–90% in uninflamed teeth. Supplementary intraosseous and intraligamentary injections raise it substantially, which is why they exist.

Two mechanisms are offered. The first is chemical: local anaesthetics cross the nerve membrane in their uncharged base form, and inflamed tissue is acidic, so less of each dose is in the form that can get in. The second, probably the more important, is neuronal: inflamed nociceptors sit at altered resting potentials and up-regulate tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels, markedly less sensitive to lidocaine than the ordinary sort. Hargreaves and Keiser's review of anaesthetic failure in endodontics sets out both, and flags the flaw in the pH story: a nerve block is deposited well away from the inflamed tooth, in tissue of normal pH. Acidity alone cannot explain the failure. The changed nerve can.

Why antibiotics do not cure it

This follows from a single fact. A necrotic pulp has no blood supply. Systemic drugs travel in blood; no circulation means no delivery, and the dead, infected tissue in the canal is beyond the reach of any dose. Antibiotics act on infection that has spread beyond the tooth, into tissue that is still perfused. They do nothing to the reservoir inside it.

The load-bearing sentence

Pulpitis and necrosis are treated by removing the diseased tissue — through the tooth, or with the tooth. No drug reaches a dead pulp, and a Cochrane review of antibiotics for irreversible pulpitis (Agnihotry and colleagues, 2019) found no evidence they reduce pain. The definitive treatment is mechanical.

Why the tablets fail and cold water does not

That argument has a consequence people meet at four in the morning and find genuinely baffling. A full dose of paracetamol, a full dose of ibuprofen and a couple of co-codamol on top can barely touch a dying pulp — while a mouthful of cold tap water, swilled round the tooth, works within seconds. It feels like the wrong way round. It is not. Both halves have the same explanation, and it is the one from the last section: nothing gets in.

Why the tablets lose

Three problems, in descending order of importance.

Delivery. This is nearly the whole story. An oral analgesic is carried to its site of action in blood. But the pressure that is causing the pain has already squeezed the pulp's own microcirculation shut, and once tissue is frankly necrotic there is no circulation at all. The drug is in the bloodstream, the bloodstream no longer reaches the tooth, and the tablet may as well have been swallowed by somebody else. It is precisely the argument that explains the antibiotics, applied to analgesia.

The wrong mechanism. Ibuprofen works by suppressing prostaglandin synthesis, which damps the chemical sensitisation of inflamed tissue. That is a real and powerful effect — it is why ibuprofen is genuinely excellent after an extraction. But the dominant driver here is not chemical sensitisation, it is mechanical: a rigid box whose contents have nowhere to go, pressing directly on nerve endings. No COX inhibitor decompresses a box.

Codeine, specifically. It is the drug people reach for when the first two fail, and it is close to the worst choice available. A 2021 review by CADTH, the Canadian drug-assessment agency, of codeine for acute dental pain concluded that codeine alone performs poorly, and — more pointedly — that adding codeine to paracetamol or ibuprofen did not add benefit in dental pain. There is a pharmacogenetic sting as well: codeine is a prodrug that must be converted to morphine by the liver enzyme CYP2D6, and a few per cent of people of northern European ancestry carry variants that make them poor converters. For them it does almost nothing at any dose.

The trials are not about this pain

Almost the entire analgesic evidence base in dentistry rests on one experimental model: pain after surgical removal of third molars. It is a good model — reproducible, and severe enough to show differences between drugs. The Oxford league table of analgesic efficacy, the source of most of the familiar numbers, is built largely on it.

But it is post-surgical pain, in soft tissue, with an intact blood supply. It is not a pulp in a sealed chamber with its circulation collapsing. The drugs that look strong in that model are being asked, at four in the morning, to do a completely different job in a compartment they cannot reach. Their reputation is earned; it is just earned somewhere else.

On top of all this sits sensitisation. Hours of intense nociceptive traffic lowers thresholds both at the nerve endings and at the second-order neurons in the trigeminal nucleus, so the system amplifies whatever signal survives. A standard dose is fighting an amplified system through a closed door.

Why cold water wins

It does not need the bloodstream. Cold arrives by conduction, through enamel and dentine, from the outside. That single fact is why it beats the tablets: it is the one intervention available at four in the morning that reaches a tissue with no blood supply.

Cooling contracts what heating expanded. The gas and fluid generated inside a partly necrotic chamber shrink as they cool, and intrapulpal pressure falls with them. This is the same mechanism as the cold paradox above, viewed from the other end: heat makes it worse because expansion raises the pressure, cold makes it better because contraction lowers it.

It blocks the nerve directly — and the numbers are unexpectedly apt. Cooling slows conduction in peripheral nerve, and in unmyelinated C fibres the threshold for a partial, reversible conduction block sits around 15 °C, with complete block below about 5 °C. Those are cold enough to sound irrelevant to tap water. But there is a twist: experimental work has shown that after a nerve has been warmed, that threshold rises markedly — partial block from about 25 °C rather than 15 °C. An acutely inflamed pulp is a warmed, sensitised nerve. It has moved into the range where quite modest cooling starts to shut conduction down. And C fibres are exactly the population carrying the deep, throbbing, lingering pain of pulpitis.

Go deeperthe cold-block numbers

The thermal-block figures come from work on mammalian peripheral nerve (Zhang and colleagues, Journal of Neurophysiology, 2020: "Thermal block of mammalian unmyelinated C fibers by local cooling to 15–25 °C after a brief heating at 45 °C"). Before heating, partial cold block of C fibres appeared at about 15 °C and complete block at or below 5 °C; after the nerve was warmed, the partial-block threshold rose to about 25 °C and complete block to about 15 °C.

Two caveats worth stating plainly. That is isolated nerve, not a human pulp, and the pulp never reaches the temperature of the water — enamel and dentine insulate, so a mouthful of water at 14 °C cools the pulp to somewhere well above 14 °C. The mechanism is a reasonable explanation for a real and repeatable clinical observation, not a measured quantity in a patient.

And there is counter-irritation on top. Cold thermoreceptor traffic inhibits nociceptive transmission centrally — the same reason rubbing a banged shin helps. It is a modest effect next to the other two, but it is free.

Why it only lasts a minute

Here is the cruel part, and the reason anybody in this situation ends up sipping continuously rather than once. Enamel and dentine are poor conductors of heat. That is excellent design — it is what stops a hot drink cooking the pulp — but insulation works in both directions. The tooth cools quickly under a mouthful of water and then, as soon as the water is gone, drifts back toward 37 °C over something like a minute. Every mechanism above runs in reverse: the gas re-expands, the pressure climbs, the C fibres come back on line. So the relief is real, immediate, and almost immediately spent.

How cold is a cold tap?

Colder than you would guess, and it varies by geography and season. Measured domestic mains water in southern England runs to 18–26 °C in July but sits nearer 9–12 °C in winter, and UK regulations require cold water to be stored and distributed below 20 °C. Scotland runs colder than the south — cooler climate, cooler sources — so a summer figure in the low-to-mid teens is a fair estimate for a Scottish cold tap, though no published measurements of Scottish mains temperature could be found to confirm it.

The number that matters is not the absolute temperature but the gap. Water at about 14 °C is roughly 23 °C below body temperature — a large gradient, and enough to drag a warmed nerve down into the band where conduction begins to fail. It has a practical advantage over iced water too: you can hold it against the gum indefinitely without damaging the soft tissue.

What the film can and cannot show

Below is a real intraoral periapical radiograph. Get your bearings: the crowns are at the bottom and the roots point up, so these are upper teeth. Brighter means denser — that is all an X-ray image is, a map of how much radiation each path absorbed. Dense restorations stop nearly everything and burn out white; enamel is denser than dentine and reads a shade brighter; the canal inside a root is soft tissue, so it reads as a thin dark line; bone shows its trabecular scaffolding as a fine mottle.

The honest teaching point

A radiograph records mineral density and nothing else. It cannot show whether a pulp is alive or dead — necrotic and healthy tissue have the same density. It cannot reliably show a crack, because a crack is a gap of essentially zero width. And it is slow to show infection: Bender and Seltzer's 1961 experiments showed that lesions confined to cancellous bone often cast no shadow until the destruction reaches the cortical plate — one reason cone-beam CT finds lesions a periapical film misses.

That is why the diagnosis here is made from the symptoms and the pulp tests, not the film. The film rules things out and shows the anatomy. The story comes from the patient.

The pulp tests have limits too, because "vitality test" is a misnomer. A cold or electric test measures whether nerve fibres respond, not whether the tissue still has a blood supply. The most-cited evaluation of them (Petersson and colleagues, Endodontics and Dental Traumatology, 1999) put the cold test's sensitivity at around 0.8 and its specificity around 0.9, with the electric pulp test broadly similar. Good, then, and nowhere near infallible. Methods that read blood flow directly, such as pulse oximetry, remain largely research tools.

One last thing about this film, said without identifying anyone. It was taken because of a tooth that hurt, at the point where the pain had finally acquired an address. The questions it raised — why the anaesthetic struggled, why cold helped, why nothing on the image could confirm what was already obvious in the chair — are the reason this guide exists.
