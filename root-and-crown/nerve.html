CHAPTER 2

The Nerve

Ice on a tooth hurts. Sugar on exposed dentine hurts. A puff of air hurts, and heat hurts slowest of all. Four unrelated stimuli, one sensation — because a tooth has essentially one sense, and that sense is pain. Here is how that single channel works, why cold beats heat to it, and why it is so vague about where the trouble is.

Your fingertip reports in several dimensions at once: pressure, texture, vibration, warmth, cool and — separately — damage. A tooth reports in one. Whatever you do to it, if it registers at all, it arrives as pain. No tooth has ever felt pleasantly chilled.

That is not a quirk but a consequence of how the tooth is wired, and the mechanism explains most of what follows.

What is actually inside

The pulp is the tooth's only soft tissue: loose connective tissue in a chamber at the heart of the crown, running down a canal in each root and out through a hole at the tip called the apical foramen. An artery, a vein or two, lymphatics and the nerve bundle all pass through that hole, and it is only a few tenths of a millimetre across. That narrow entrance drives all of Chapter 5.

Lining the chamber wall is a palisade of tall cells, the odontoblasts — the tooth's only builders. Each sits with its body in the pulp and sends a long thin process into a tube in the dentine it made: one cell, one tube, for life. Those tubes are the dentinal tubules, roughly 45,000 per square millimetre near the pulp and thinning to about 20,000 out at the junction with enamel. Every one is full of fluid.

Just beneath them lies a fine mesh of nerve endings, the subodontoblastic plexus. Some endings poke into the tubules, but only the innermost tenth of a millimetre or so, and only in a minority of tubules, concentrated under the pulp horns. The bulk of dentine — the part a drill spends most of its time in — has no nerve supply at all.

The problem

Dentine is exquisitely sensitive along its whole thickness. Dentine has almost no nerves along its whole thickness. Both statements are true, and reconciling them is the central puzzle of this chapter.

Brännström's answer: it is plumbing

In the 1960s the Swedish researcher Martin Brännström proposed that dentine senses nothing directly. It transmits. A stimulus at the surface makes the fluid inside the tubules move; that movement tugs the mechanically sensitive nerve endings waiting at the far, pulpal end; and they fire. The nerve never meets the cold or the sugar. It only ever feels a shove.

This is the hydrodynamic theory, still the working model sixty years on. It dissolves the puzzle — you do not need nerves along a tubule if the tubule is a hydraulic line — and it explains why four stimuli with nothing in common produce one identical sensation. They all do the same physical thing.

Go deeperthe evidence, and the fourth-power rule

Brännström's case rested on experiments hard to explain any other way. Local anaesthetic dripped onto exposed dentine does not stop it hurting — the drug cannot reach endings buried at the pulpal end — yet anaesthetising the pulp abolishes the sensation. Concentrated sugar or salt on exposed dentine causes sharp pain; the same solutions made isotonic do not, and the only difference is the osmotic pull on the tubule fluid. And an air jet is agony though it barely changes the temperature. (Brännström & Åström, The hydrodynamics of the dentine, International Dental Journal, 1972.)

The fourth-power rule is Poiseuille's law: flow down a pipe goes as the fourth power of the radius. Tubules run about 2.5 µm across near the pulp and about 0.9 µm at the dentino-enamel junction, at densities of roughly 45,000 and 20,000 per square millimetre (Garberoglio & Brännström, Archives of Oral Biology, 1976). Combine calibre and density and deep dentine should conduct fluid a hundred-odd times better — broadly what David Pashley's permeability measurements found. Hydrodynamics remains the dominant model rather than a closed case: odontoblasts carry temperature- and stretch-sensitive ion channels of their own, and whether the cell is itself a sensor is open.

Why cold wins

Cool a tooth and the tubule fluid contracts. It is drawn outward, away from the pulp, and fast, because the shrinkage starts at the surface where the cold is. Warm a tooth and the fluid expands and creeps inward — slowly, because dentine is a poor conductor and the thermal front takes seconds to arrive.

Outward flow is the more provocative direction and the faster one. That asymmetry is why cold is the reflex test at the chairside, why a sensitive tooth flinches at iced water long before it objects to tea, and why heat pain has a slow-burning quality quite unlike the electric jolt of cold. Hold the thought: in Chapter 5 the asymmetry inverts, and a dying tooth can be soothed by cold and set off by heat.

Set the stimulus to air and drag the cavity floor to 0.2 mm — a tooth mid-treatment, pulp a fraction of a millimetre away. Then drag tubules open to zero: same insult, nothing happens. Sealing tubules is what a desensitising toothpaste, a varnish or a layer of sclerotic dentine is for.

Two fibres, two kinds of suffering

The nerve bundle entering the apex is not one wire. It carries two functionally different fibre types, and every confusing thing about toothache comes from the difference between them.

A-delta fibres are myelinated — insulated — and fast, conducting a few to about thirty metres per second. They terminate mostly at the pulp-dentine border, respond to exactly the fluid movement Brännström described, and behave like a well-mannered sensor: they fire when the stimulus starts and stop when it stops. Their product is the sharp, stabbing, electric sensation everyone calls "sensitive teeth". Unpleasant, but honest.

C fibres are unmyelinated and slow — on the order of half a metre to two metres per second — and lie deeper in the body of the pulp. They are polymodal: their business is chemistry as much as mechanics. They answer to the inflammatory mediators released by damaged tissue, have a high threshold in a healthy pulp and a much lower one in an inflamed pulp, and once going they keep going. Their product is the deep, throbbing, badly localised ache that arrives late and outstays its cause. Axon counts put them in the clear majority of a mature tooth's nerve supply.

Go deeperwhy the slow fibre is not slow because it is slow

It is tempting to assume the C fibre's lag is transmission time. The arithmetic says otherwise. The path from a molar to the brainstem is on the order of fifteen centimetres: about 150 milliseconds at 1 m/s, about ten at 15 m/s. A tenth of a second — nothing like the several seconds the ache takes to build.

The delay is at the front end. A-δ endings transduce a mechanical event that happens the instant fluid moves. C fibres wait on chemistry: mediators must accumulate, diffuse and bind. That takes seconds, and it does not stop when the stimulus does, because the mediators are still there.

One asymmetry matters later. C fibres tolerate low oxygen better than myelinated fibres do, so as a pulp's blood supply fails the A-δ population goes quiet first. A tooth can stop responding to cold while still aching relentlessly — select losing its blood supply above to see that shape. It is the hinge of Chapter 5.

The one-modality tooth

Both fibre types are nociceptors — damage reporters. There is no touch fibre, no warmth fibre, no vibration fibre worth speaking of. The tooth's entire vocabulary is one word spoken in two accents: sharp and brief, or dull and lasting. Every symptom in this guide combines the two.

The honest caveat: under very weak electrical stimulation some people report a faint pre-pain tingle, so "purely nociceptive" may be a simplification. Nobody has ever described a tooth as feeling pleasantly cool.

Nerv meets Chill

Chill never touches Nerv. He sits outside a millimetre of solid dentine, cools it, and the fluid in forty-five thousand little pipes flinches backwards. Nerv, at the far end, feels the tug and screams. He has no idea what happened. He has no vocabulary for cold. All he can say is ow.

Not a bad design so much as an old one. A tooth's job is to survive eighty years of grinding. Fine discrimination was never on the specification. One loud alarm was.

Which tooth is it? Nobody knows

Ask someone in the middle of pulpal toothache which tooth hurts and there is a fair chance they will get it wrong. Usually right about the side of the face; often wrong about the tooth; not rarely wrong about whether it is an upper or a lower. That is anatomy, not inattention, with two causes.

The first is that the pulp has no position sense. Skin and muscle carry receptors reporting where they are and what is being done to them. The pulp carries almost nothing but nociceptors. There is no spatial map to consult.

The second is convergence. Tooth sensation travels in the trigeminal nerve, the fifth cranial nerve: its maxillary division (V2) supplies every upper tooth, its mandibular division (V3) every lower one. All of it funnels through the trigeminal ganglion into a column of grey matter in the brainstem, the trigeminal spinal nucleus, where many peripheral fibres converge onto far fewer second-order neurons — teeth, gum, sinus, jaw joint, ear and skin sharing wiring. Downstream, the brain can only guess which input opened the gate.

So pulpal pain refers — a lower molar to the ear, an upper molar to the temple — and it crosses arches: an upper tooth convincingly aching in the lower jaw is routine. It essentially never crosses the midline, one of the few reliable rules in the business.

Then something changes and the tooth becomes findable. The reason is the periodontal ligament, the suspension holding it in its socket (Chapter 1). Unlike the pulp it is packed with mechanoreceptors and does have position sense — enough to detect a foil a few tens of micrometres thick between the teeth. Once inflammation spreads out of the apical foramen into the ligament, that system is recruited: the tooth becomes tender to bite on, and the patient who was waving at half their face points straight at it.

A diagnostic irony

The moment the pain becomes precisely locatable is the moment the disease has left the tooth and entered the tissue around it. Vague pain means the trouble is still contained. Sharply localised tenderness on biting means it is not.

Testing a nerve you cannot see

Clinicians must decide whether the pulp inside a tooth is alive, and they cannot look. The two standard instruments are a cold test — a refrigerant-soaked pellet held against the tooth — and the electric pulp test, a probe passing a small current until the patient feels a tingle. Both ask the nerve a question and see whether it answers.

That framing contains the flaw. What keeps a tooth healthy is its blood supply, and these tests measure the nerve. The two come apart. After a knock to a front tooth the endings may be stunned for months while circulation is intact, so the tooth reads dead and is not. A molar can have one necrotic canal and one live one and answer normally. A thick crown insulates. And the tests report only present or absent — never how inflamed.

Go deeperhow good are the tests, really

The most-cited comparison is Petersson and colleagues (Endodontics & Dental Traumatology, 1999), who tested teeth and then opened them to see what was inside. On the cold test, roughly 8 in 10 genuinely necrotic pulps correctly failed to respond (sensitivity 0.83) and roughly 9 in 10 vital ones correctly did (specificity 0.93). The electric pulp test was weaker at catching dead pulps (0.72) and the same at confirming live ones (0.93); a heat test was far worse at confirming vitality (0.41). Respectable for a two-second bedside test — but one to three teeth in ten are still misread.

A systematic review by Mejàre and colleagues (International Endodontic Journal, 2012) was blunter: the evidence base for judging a pulp's condition is limited, and the studies behind those figures carry real risk of bias. Instruments that measure blood flow directly — laser Doppler flowmetry, pulse oximetry — are more logical, but have not displaced a cotton pellet and a can of refrigerant.

Which leaves diagnosis where medicine usually sits: a story, imperfect signs and a working hypothesis. The character of the pain does much of the work, and by now you can read it. A jolt on cold that vanishes with the stimulus is an A-δ report — a tooth complaining. An ache that builds by itself, throbs with the pulse, wakes you at night and answers to no stimulus is a C-fibre report, and something in the pulp has begun to fail.

Which is Chapter 5. But before a pulp can fail something has to reach it, and almost always that something is acid, made by bacteria a few hundred micrometres away. Chapter 3 is the clock they run on.
