CHAPTER 8

Crowns, Gaps and Implants

A root-treated molar usually does not fail because the root filling failed. It fails because the tooth splits. This is the last third of a tooth's clinical life: why a hollowed-out back tooth needs a lid, what a crown costs the tooth underneath it, and the decision nobody lays out honestly — keep it, or take it out and choose between an implant, a bridge, a denture and an empty space.

Chapter 7 left a molar disinfected and sealed, and still not safe. What threatens it now is its shape. A molar is not a solid lump: it is a ring of enamel and dentine, braced across the top by two raised rims — the marginal ridges — and floored by the roof of the pulp chamber. Those are the tie-beams. Take them away and the cusps stop being parts of a box and start being cantilevers, levered outwards a few micrometres every time you bite.

That is the argument for crowning a root-treated back tooth. It has nothing to do with the tooth being dead, and everything to do with how much of the tooth the decay and the drill took first.

The keystone problem

The cleanest evidence is a small experiment from 1989. Reeh, Messer and Douglas loaded extracted teeth, measured how far the cusps moved, then cut them a stage at a time and measured again. Cutting the endodontic access cavity — through the occlusal surface and off the roof of the pulp chamber, the part that sounds most destructive — cost about 5% of cuspal stiffness. An occlusal cavity cost about 20%. An MOD cavity, running across the tooth and taking out both marginal ridges, cost about 63%: two-thirds of the tooth's resistance to being splayed apart, lost not to the root canal but to the cavity that made it necessary.

Why the root canal takes the blame

A root canal does not weaken a tooth in any way that matters. Losing the marginal ridges does. By the time a molar needs root treatment it has usually already lost them — which is why the two things travel together and why the root canal takes the blame.

Now add load. Bite force in the molar region runs to hundreds of newtons, and it does not arrive straight down: the opposing cusp lands on the sloping inclines of yours, so a vertical force becomes a sideways wedge. An unbraced cusp flexes outwards by a few micrometres — invisible, and endlessly repeated — and a crack starts at the sharp internal corner of the cavity, where stress concentrates.

When it goes, it does not chip. The fracture follows the long axis and frequently runs below the gum, and a vertical fracture beneath the bone crest usually ends the tooth. That is what cuspal coverage prevents: a restoration over the cusps rather than between them, so biting compresses the tooth instead of prising it apart.

Follow-up studies agree with the mechanics. Sorensen and Martinoff (1984) found coronal coverage significantly improved survival in premolars and molars — and made no difference to front teeth. Aquilino and Caplan (2002) found uncrowned teeth were lost at roughly six times the rate of crowned ones. And the largest review of survival after root canal treatment — Ng, Mann and Gulabivala, 2010 — pooled tooth survival at about 86–93% at eight to ten years, naming a crown as one of the few factors that significantly improved it.

Go deeperhow good is that evidence, really?

Good, but not the best kind. Every study above is observational: someone looked back at teeth that had been crowned and teeth that had not, and compared them. The trouble with that design is that the two groups were never comparable to begin with. Teeth get crowned because a clinician judged them worth crowning, and they go uncrowned because the patient could not afford it, or did not return, or the tooth was already a write-off. That is a large, unmeasured bias sitting inside every hazard ratio.

The Cochrane review on exactly this question — Sequeira-Byron and colleagues, 2015, "Single crowns versus conventional fillings for the restoration of root-filled teeth" — found only one randomised trial in the whole literature and concluded there was insufficient evidence to support or refute the practice. That is not the same as saying crowns do not help. It means the trial that would settle it has never really been run, and the profession is relying on a strong mechanical rationale plus consistent observational data. Both point the same way. Neither is proof.

Front teeth are the honest exception, and the reason is geometry. Incisors have no cusps to wedge apart and take lower forces, closer to the long axis. A root-treated incisor with its walls standing is often restored by sealing the access hole and nothing more.

What a crown costs the tooth underneath

A crown is not added to a tooth; it replaces the outside of one, so the tooth must first be made smaller by exactly the crown's thickness. Edelhoff and Sorensen measured it in 2002 by preparing extracted teeth and weighing what came off: a full-coverage posterior preparation removed on the order of 60–75% of the coronal tooth by volume. A tooth that has already lost its ridges and its chamber roof has little to spare.

The second bill lands on the nerve. Cutting a millimetre or two of dentine severs several million tubules, each an open line to the pulp. Follow-up studies of vital teeth prepared for crowns report pulp death afterwards at rates from a few per cent to the mid-teens over ten years, bridge abutments worse than single crowns (Cheung and colleagues, 2005). A crown on a living tooth carries a small but real chance of causing the very problem it was meant to prevent.

What crowns are made of, and what each one is bad at

Every crown material compromises between three things you cannot have at once: strength, appearance and how little tooth has to go to make room for it.

How long they last depends on who is counting. Reviews of crowns followed in study conditions report five-year survival broadly in the 90–96% range depending on material (Pjetursson and colleagues 2007; Sailer and colleagues 2015). Practice-based data are less flattering: Burke and Lucarotti's analysis of crowns placed in the General Dental Services in England and Wales found roughly three-quarters still in service at ten years.

The conservative middle: onlays and adhesive cuspal coverage

The full crown was designed when a restoration was held on by shape alone: if a cap grips a tapered stump, the stump has to be cut, all of it, everywhere. Adhesion changed the premise. A bonded restoration can follow whatever tooth is still there — cover the cusps that need covering, leave the sound wall, stop.

That is an onlay, and its evidence is respectable: Morimoto and colleagues' 2016 meta-analysis pooled ceramic inlays, onlays and overlays at about 95% survival at five years and 91% at ten. It is not a head-to-head comparison with crowns, and an onlay needs a clean, dry bonded surface in a way a cemented crown does not.

The direction of travel

Cuspal coverage is the well-supported part. The full crown is one way to deliver it, and for a long time the only way. The modern instinct is to deliver the same protection while cutting the least tooth that will do the job — because whatever is left today has to carry the next restoration, and the one after that.

Crownie explains himself

Crownie: "I'm not a repair. I'm a splint. My whole job is stopping those two cusps waving at each other."

Molar: "And for that you took two-thirds of my crown."

Then the real decision: save it or take it out

Somewhere in every long dental history there is a tooth where the honest question stops being "can this be saved?" and becomes "what does saving it buy, at what cost?" Almost anything can be saved by someone determined enough; that was never the question. The routes are usually presented one at a time, by whoever is offering that route.

The survival figures cluster: over a decade most of these routes work most of the time. What they cost the teeth that were never the problem differs enormously.

What actually happens to a gap

The socket heals reliably, but the bone it heals into is not the bone that was there. Alveolar bone exists to hold teeth — that is its entire developmental purpose — so when the tooth goes, the bone reads it as redundancy and remodels away.

The dimensions are well measured. Schropp and colleagues followed single extraction sites for twelve months in 2003 and found the ridge lost about half its width, roughly twelve millimetres down to six, two-thirds of it inside the first three months. Tan and colleagues' 2012 review put horizontal bone loss at 29–63% and vertical loss at 11–22% by six months. It never comes back — which is why the timing of an implant is a real clinical question.

Then there is the rest of the mouth. The tooth that used to bite against the missing one now bites against nothing, and unopposed teeth tend to over-erupt. The teeth either side can tilt in. Tight contacts open and food packs into them, which is the complaint people actually notice. Craddock and colleagues, surveying adults who had lost posterior teeth, found these changes common but strikingly variable — the folklore version where the dentition collapses like a dropped bookshelf is not what the measurements show.

The shortened dental arch

Losing the last molar in an arch is, by a wide margin, the least consequential extraction in the mouth. Nothing sits behind it, so nothing can drift forward. The only thing that reliably moves is the tooth above, which has lost its partner and may over-erupt.

This is not a rationalisation invented to make extractions sound better. It is a formal position with forty years of evidence behind it. In 1981 Käyser published the shortened dental arch concept: about twenty teeth — front teeth and premolars intact, with four occluding pairs behind them — supply enough function for most people for life. The World Health Organization later adopted twenty functional teeth as an oral health goal. Witter and colleagues found the occlusion of shortened arches stable and chewing adequate, and a German multicentre randomised trial comparing a shortened arch against one restored with a partial denture found no advantage for the denture.

Worth saying out loud

"You do not need to replace that one" is a legitimate, evidence-supported position for a terminal molar, not a counsel of despair. It is also the only route on the list that costs nothing and can be reversed later by choosing one of the others.

None of which applies to a front tooth, or to a first molar with an intact arch behind it. Position is not a detail here. It is most of the answer.

Replacing it: four routes and what each really costs

The implant is a titanium screw placed in the ridge and left while bone grows into direct contact with its surface — osseointegration, which Brånemark stumbled on in 1952, when he could not get his titanium chambers back out of rabbit bone; the first titanium implant in a patient followed in 1965. The tooth is built on top weeks or months later. Reviews with at least ten years of follow-up put survival around 93–97%, and its central advantage is that it does nothing at all to the neighbouring teeth. The costs are real: surgery, the highest price by a distance, and enough bone in the right place. Smoking roughly doubles the failure rate in meta-analyses. The long-term threat is peri-implantitis — inflammatory bone loss around a working implant, found in around a fifth of patients in Derks and Tomasi's 2015 review. An implant cannot decay, but it can be lost to gum disease, and with no periodontal ligament it gives less warning.

The conventional bridge carries a false tooth — a pontic — on crowns cemented to the teeth either side. Quick, fixed and effective: Tan and colleagues' 2004 review reported about 89% survival at ten years. The bill is paid by two teeth that were often sound, both cut down, both now carrying the risk of pulp death and of decay under a margin. A failing bridge often takes an abutment with it.

The resin-bonded bridge does the same job with almost no cutting: a pontic on a thin wing bonded to one neighbour. Older two-winged designs debonded often — Pjetursson and colleagues' 2008 review put five-year survival around 88% — but modern single-wing cantilevers behave far better, with ten-year survival reported around 90–95% in specialist series, notably Kern's. Its failure mode is forgiving: it comes off, and is generally bonded back on.

The removable partial denture is the cheapest route by an order of magnitude and the only fully reversible one. Its characteristic failure is not mechanical: a denture fails by living in a drawer. Clasped teeth gather plaque too, so decay around them is a documented risk. Hence the comparator's refusal to draw it a survival curve — what matters is not whether the appliance survives but whether it is still worn.

On the money

Fees vary so much between countries and practices that quoting them would mislead, so the comparator shows relative order of magnitude only. The ranking is the stable part: a denture is cheapest by a wide margin, a crown or bonded bridge sits in the middle, and an implant with its crown is typically the dearest.

There is no universally right answer

Everything above trades four currencies that do not convert into one another: cost to the tooth in question, cost to the teeth either side, money and time. A route that is obviously right for an upper incisor at twenty-five is obviously wrong for a lower second molar at seventy.

What moves the decision, roughly in the order clinicians weigh it: how much sound tooth is left above the gum, whether the neighbours are already restored or untouched, which tooth in the arch it is, whether the person can maintain the result, and only then cost. The first item is the tooth's remaining substance — because every restoration is a bet placed on the tooth that is left, and that stock only ever goes down.

The alternative endings are not disasters: a well-chosen extraction, an implant, a bonded wing or a deliberately empty space are all real answers.
