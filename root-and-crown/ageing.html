CHAPTER 4

Eighty Years of Wear

Not one bacterium appears in this chapter. A tooth that never gets a single cavity still ends its life shorter, flatter, darker, tender at the neck and numb at the tip, wrapped around a pulp chamber a fraction of the size it started with. None of that is disease. It is the arithmetic of eight decades of contact, chemistry and habit — and it makes a seventy-year-old tooth a fundamentally different object from a twenty-year-old one.

The contact areas of your molars lose enamel at roughly 29 micrometres a year. Lambrechts and colleagues measured it directly in 1989, from replicas of real teeth over real time; premolars came in at about 15. These are absurd little numbers. A human hair is around 70 µm across, so a molar sheds less than half a hair's width of enamel per year.

Then run the multiplication. Sixty years at 29 µm is 1.7 millimetres, and the enamel over a molar cusp is at most about 2.5 mm thick to begin with. Perfectly ordinary chewing consumes most of the enamel on the parts of the tooth that do the chewing. That is why an old molar has flat, glossy, yellow-brown islands where its cusp tips used to be. Those islands are not decay. That is dentine, showing through a hole worn in the cap.

Four ways to lose a tooth without a single bacterium

Tooth surface loss is not one process but four. They look different, they have different causes, and telling them apart is the whole diagnostic job — the treatment of a grinding habit has nothing in common with the treatment of an acid habit.

Attrition is tooth against tooth. It produces flat facets with sharp boundaries, and its signature is that the facets match: they were cut by each other, so they mate perfectly when the jaw returns to the position that made them. It also ignores materials — an amalgam in an attrition facet is worn to precisely the level of the enamel beside it, because another tooth does not care what it is grinding.

Abrasion is a foreign object, and its pattern follows the habit rather than the bite. The classic is a notch at the gum line, worst on the canines and first premolars because they stand furthest out in the arch, and often worse on the side opposite the dominant hand. Historically it was pipe stems, hairgrips and dressmakers' thread; now it is mostly brushing. The honest nuance is that a brush alone barely marks enamel. It is the paste's abrasive, and above all exposed dentine, that lets brushing cut: dentine's Knoop hardness is about 60 against enamel's 300.

Erosion is chemistry. No bacteria, no plaque — the acid arrives ready-made and dissolves mineral straight off the surface. Enamel starts dissolving below about pH 5.5, root surfaces below about 6.2. Measure what people drink and the exposure is obvious: cola sits around pH 2.4, wine around 3.0 to 3.8, orange juice around 3.3 to 4.0. When Reddy and colleagues measured 379 commercial beverages in 2016, roughly 93% came in below pH 4. Gastric contents are more aggressive still, at roughly pH 1 to 3, which is why reflux and self-induced vomiting leave a pattern clinicians recognise on sight.

The single best tell

If a filling stands proud of the tooth around it, the cause is acid. Amalgam and composite do not dissolve in dietary or gastric acid; enamel and dentine do, so the tooth has retreated around a restoration that never moved. Mechanical wear does the opposite — it grinds tooth and filling down together, leaving them flush.

Work through the five cases below. In each one the giveaway is a single feature — usually not the wear itself but where it is or what happened to the fillings.

The mechanisms also compound, and one pairing matters most. Acid does not remove enamel cleanly; it first softens a surface layer a few micrometres deep, and that softened layer brushes away far more readily than sound enamel. Erosion and abrasion together therefore destroy more than either alone — the mechanistic reason clinicians care about the interval between an acidic drink and a toothbrush.

Abfraction: genuinely contested

Abfraction is the one entry in that table whose existence is disputed. Grippo named it in 1991 for a specific idea: sideways occlusal load bends the tooth, concentrating tensile stress at the neck where enamel is thinnest, and the surface breaks away in a sharp wedge. Finite-element models do show that stress concentration, so the physics is not fanciful.

What has never been shown is that the lesions track occlusal loading in real mouths. Bartlett and Shah (Journal of Dental Research, 2006) and Senna and colleagues (2012) both found the evidence weak, and every such lesion is equally compatible with erosion, abrasion or both. A plausible mechanism with an unproven clinical case — not an established diagnosis.

The grinding you cannot feel

Bruxism is the ordinary word for grinding and clenching, and the striking thing is how many people do it without knowing. Sleep bruxism happens while you are unconscious and is silent to the person doing it; unless someone else is in the room to hear it, the only evidence is what it leaves on the teeth. Definitive diagnosis needs an overnight sleep study with audio and video. Almost nobody gets one, so nearly every diagnosis in practice is a probable one, assembled from self-report plus clinical signs.

Its signature is a set of findings rather than any single one: broad flat facets that interdigitate with the opposing arch, cusps and restorations that fracture more often than they should, bulky masseter muscles, a raised white line along the inside of the cheek at the level of the bite, and scalloped indentations along the tongue's border where it is pressed against the teeth. Each is suggestive. None is specific.

Facets are a record, not a rate

This is the part that gets missed. Wear facets tell you a tooth has been ground. They cannot tell you whether it is being ground now. A sixty-year-old with spectacularly flat teeth may have stopped grinding thirty years ago — a historical scar rather than an active process, and a completely different clinical problem. It is why monitoring over time settles the question and a single dramatic photograph does not.

Go deeperhow common, and what it even is

Manfredini and colleagues' systematic review of adult bruxism epidemiology (2013) put awake bruxism at roughly 22–31% of adults and sleep bruxism at roughly 8–16%, with sleep bruxism declining with age. The wide ranges are honest: they reflect how much the answer depends on whether you ask people, examine them, or record them overnight.

An international consensus published in 2018 (Lobbezoo and colleagues) went further and redefined the term. Bruxism, it proposed, is best described as masticatory muscle activity rather than a disorder, and in otherwise healthy people it should not be regarded as pathological at all — it may even be protective in some contexts. The same paper introduced the grading now in general use: possible bruxism from self-report, probable from self-report plus examination, definite only with an instrumental recording.

For wear more broadly, van't Spijker and colleagues (2009) pooled the prevalence literature and found the proportion of adults with severe tooth wear rising from about 3% at age 20 to about 17% at age 70 — the clearest single statement of the fact that wear accumulates and does not reverse.

The crack you cannot photograph

Cracks are the other mechanical story, and they run on a ladder from trivial to terminal. The American Association of Endodontists' five-way classification is the standard map, and the rungs matter because the prognosis changes completely between them.

The crack ladder
TypeExtentWhat it means
Craze lineEnamel only Essentially universal in adult teeth. No symptoms, no consequence, no treatment.
Fractured cuspA cusp shears off, usually beside a filling Often the lucky outcome: the crack finds its way out to the surface and the fragment leaves.
Cracked toothA crack running from the biting surface downward, still in one piece The problematic one. Symptoms depend on how deep it has gone.
Split toothThe crack has separated the segments The tooth is in two pieces; at best one segment can be kept.
Vertical root fractureA crack originating in the root Usually the end of that root, and often of the tooth.

The classic presentation, described by Cameron in 1964 and unchanged since, is one of the strangest complaints in medicine: sharp pain on releasing a bite rather than on making it, in a tooth that looks entirely normal. The mechanism is the hydrodynamic one from Chapter 2, driven mechanically instead of thermally. Biting wedges the segments apart; releasing lets them snap back, and the sudden movement of fluid in the tubules bridging the crack fires the nerve.

Now the part that surprises almost everyone told their radiograph is clear.

Why the film shows nothing

A radiograph is a shadow. A feature appears only if it attenuates the beam differently from what sits beside it, along the beam's path. A crack is a gap of essentially no width, so to cast a shadow the beam must travel within its plane, making the path through the gap long.

In the mouth the beam runs from cheek to tongue, across the tooth. A cracked tooth's crack almost always runs the other way, front to back along the arch. The beam therefore crosses it at right angles, through a few micrometres of nothing, and the film records exactly that. To shoot along the plane of the crack you would have to fire down the arch, through the neighbouring teeth. You cannot.

So diagnosis is physical rather than photographic. A bite test loads one cusp at a time until the offending one reproduces the pain. Transillumination puts a fibre-optic light against the tooth: it travels happily through sound enamel and dentine and stops dead at the crack, so the far segment goes black — very sensitive, and so poorly specific that harmless craze lines light up just as brightly. Dye, magnification and, finally, taking the filling out and looking, do the rest. Cone-beam CT resolves better, but its voxels are still tens to hundreds of micrometres across and metal restorations throw artefacts precisely where the crack is.

What changes everything is whether the crack has passed the crest of the bone. Above it, a crack can be enclosed: a cuspal-coverage restoration binds the segments and the tooth may run for years. Below it, the crack opens a channel between mouth and periodontal ligament that cannot be cleaned, cannot be sealed and has no accessible margin to restore to. The classic finding is a single deep, narrow probing defect beside an otherwise healthy tooth, and it moves the tooth from "restorable" to a conversation about which segment, if any, stays.

The gum retreats

Meanwhile the soft tissue is moving. Gingival recession is extremely common with age: in NHANES III, Albandar and Kingman (1999) found at least one site with a millimetre or more of recession in about 58% of American adults aged 30 and over, and in the great majority of those over 65. Periodontal inflammation, thin tissue and mechanical trauma all contribute, but the consequence is uniform.

What gets exposed is not enamel. There is no enamel below the neck of the tooth and there never will be. The root is covered by cementum, a layer only tens of micrometres thick at the neck and about 45–50% mineral by weight against enamel's roughly 96%. It wears through quickly. Underneath is dentine, tubules running straight to the pulp — which is the whole reason receding gums and cold sensitivity arrive together.

A small number with large consequences

Enamel dissolves below pH 5.5; root surfaces below about 6.2. That looks like a trivial gap until you remember pH is logarithmic: attacking enamel takes about five times the hydrogen-ion concentration that attacking a root does. Since plaque sits at 6.8 at rest, a dip reaching only 6.0 is a non-event for the crown and a full acid attack on the root. An exposed root is not merely softer — it is dissolved by episodes the crown never registers.

The pulp walls itself in

The last change is the one you cannot see from outside, and it runs from the day the root finishes forming until the tooth is lost. The odontoblasts lining the pulp never stop working. They lay down secondary dentine throughout life, from the inside, so the chamber shrinks year on year — and in molars the deposition is heaviest on roof and floor, so it flattens as much as it narrows. Under a specific threat the pulp adds tertiary dentine locally: faster, less organised, exactly where needed.

Four consequences follow, and together they describe what an old tooth is.

It is less sensitive. There is more dentine between the surface and the nerve, and the tubules themselves narrow and occlude as peritubular dentine fills them in — sclerotic dentine. Fewer patent tubules means less fluid movement and so less signal, and the nerve supply thins too. An old tooth can lose a great deal of substance quietly.

It is darker. The dentine core thickens and yellows while the enamel over it thins and grows more translucent, so more dentine shows through less enamel. Accumulated surface stain finishes the job.

It cracks more readily. Laboratory work on extracted teeth — Arola and Reprogel (2005), Koester, Ager and Ritchie (2008) — shows dentine's fracture toughness and its resistance to fatigue crack growth both falling with age. A crack a young tooth would arrest, an old one propagates.

It is harder to treat. A shrunken chamber and sclerosed canals are the practical curse of endodontics in older patients: roof and floor converge, the orifices become hard to find, and the risk of perforating the tooth while hunting for them rises. Add pulp stones, calcified masses sitting directly in the path of access — radiographic surveys find them in a minority of teeth, histological sections in far more, because most are too small to cast a shadow. And an ageing pulp is more fibrous, less cellular and less well supplied with blood, so it can repair less.

The tooth as a clock

These changes are so orderly that forensic scientists read age off them. Gustafson (1950) scored six age changes in a sectioned tooth, of which root dentine translucency — the sclerosis described above — proved the most reliable single indicator. Kvaal and colleagues (1995) estimated adult age from the ratio of pulp to tooth on an ordinary radiograph, typically to within about a decade.

Remember that spread whenever a single erosion prevalence figure is quoted at you.

Put it together and the ageing tooth is doing something quietly remarkable: losing structure from the outside at a few tens of micrometres a year while rebuilding inward, thickening its walls, sealing its tubules and withdrawing its nerve. It is not repairing itself — nothing here restores what was lost. It is conceding ground in good order. Which works right up until something reaches the pulp anyway, and a chamber that has spent seventy years getting smaller turns out to be exactly the wrong shape for what happens next. That is Chapter 5.

Molar, at seventy

"Shorter, flatter, browner, and I can barely feel the cold any more. People say that like it is a loss. It is a truce."
