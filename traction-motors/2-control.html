Controlling it: resistance, grouping, and weakening the field.

A DC series motor on full line voltage from a standstill would draw a current that welds the rails. The whole art of pre-electronic control is getting voltage onto the motors gradually — and doing it without simply burning the surplus as heat.

The starting problem

At a standstill there is no back-EMF to hold the current down.

Recall N ∝ (V − IₐR) / Φ. The term IₐR is tiny because armature resistance R is deliberately small. So at the instant of starting, with the motor stationary and producing zero back-EMF, almost the entire line voltage tries to push current through that small resistance. The result is an inrush of many times the normal current — enough to flash the commutator, strip the gear teeth, and trip the supply.

You must limit the starting current until the motor turns fast enough to generate its own back-EMF. There are three ways to control the voltage the motors actually see, and a good controller uses all three in sequence.

Tool 1 — series resistance

Crude, reliable, and wasteful by design.

Put a bank of resistors in series with the motors and the surplus voltage drops across them instead of forcing current through the armature. As the train accelerates and back-EMF rises, you cut the resistors out a step at a time, keeping current — and so tractive effort — roughly constant. This is the sawtooth you feel as a series of jerks in an old electric train.

The cost

Every watt dropped across a starting resistor becomes heat in a roof-top or underfloor grid — pure loss. Accelerating a heavy train through resistance can waste a large fraction of the energy used, which is exactly why the resistors are only ever a transient measure and why engineers worked so hard to minimise time spent on them. Eliminating resistance control entirely was one of the prizes of power electronics (Ch. 04).

Resistance alone could in principle give full control — but it would waste enormous energy at the lower running speeds. So it is paired with a cleverer, loss-free trick: changing how the motors are connected.

Tool 2 — series / parallel grouping

Re-wiring the motors gives free voltage steps.

With four identical motors you can share the line voltage between them in three tidy ratios — without any resistor. Each gives a stable, efficient running speed; the resistors are only needed to bridge the gaps between these steps and during the switchover.

All series
¼ V

All four in one chain. Line volts split four ways — lowest running speed.

Series-parallel
½ V

Two pairs side by side. Each motor sees half the line — middle running speed.

All parallel
1 V

All four across the line. Full voltage each — highest plain-field speed.

The transition between groupings must happen without breaking tractive effort or open-circuiting the motors — handled by bridge or shunt-transition switching sequences buried in the camshaft controller. Three loss-free running speeds from four motors and a switch: a beautifully economical idea, and the backbone of DC traction control for a century.

Tool 3 — field weakening

Why weaken the field? Because you have run out of voltage.

At the top running notch the motors are across the full line, with no resistance left and the field at full strength. You are at base speed — and you have used up every voltage trick available. The supply cannot give more than line voltage. So how do you go faster?

Return to N ∝ (V − IₐR) / Φ. If V is pinned at maximum, the only remaining variable is the flux Φ in the denominator. Reduce the flux and the speed must rise. You do it by diverting some field current through a parallel "diverter" resistor, so the series field carries less current and produces less flux.

Limits

You cannot weaken indefinitely. Too little flux and the same torque demands too much armature current; commutation worsens and armature reaction can cause flashover. Practical weakening reaches perhaps 50–60% field, extending top speed by a useful margin — typically 30–50% above base speed.
