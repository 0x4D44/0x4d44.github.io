Modern drives: the brushless inverter age.

Power electronics finally severed the link between supply and motor. Once you can synthesise any voltage at any frequency on board, you can throw away the commutator, the brushes, the resistors and the tap-changer — and run the toughest, simplest motor ever made.

The core idea

A DC link in the middle, an inverter on the end.

Whatever the supply — 25 kV AC, 3 kV DC, third rail — the modern locomotive first turns it into a steady DC link voltage. From that link a bank of fast electronic switches, the inverter, builds a fresh three-phase AC output whose voltage and frequency it can set instant by instant. That output drives the motor.

This is the decisive move. The supply no longer dictates the motor's behaviour; the inverter does. Variable frequency means variable speed for an AC motor. Variable voltage means controlled torque with no wasteful resistors. And because the switches work both ways, energy can flow back from the motor to the line — regenerative braking becomes routine.

The motor that won

The squirrel-cage induction motor: nothing to wear out.

The rotor of an induction motor is just a cage of solid copper or aluminium bars shorted at each end, embedded in laminated iron. There are no windings to connect to, no commutator, no brushes, no slip rings — nothing on the rotor that touches anything. The stator's rotating magnetic field induces currents in the cage (hence "induction"), and those induced currents produce torque.

It runs slightly slower than the rotating field — the difference is called slip — and torque is controlled by controlling the slip, which the inverter does by adjusting output frequency relative to rotor speed. The payoff is enormous: the induction motor is lighter and more powerful for its size than a commutator motor, tolerates overload and weather, and asks for almost no maintenance for decades. It is the motor that the inverter set free.

How the inverter drives it: volts and hertz together.

Below base speed the inverter raises voltage and frequency in step, keeping the ratio V/f — and so the flux and the available torque — constant. At base speed the voltage hits the ceiling of the DC link. Beyond that, frequency keeps rising but voltage cannot, so flux falls: this is electronic field weakening — the same constant-power trade as Chapter 02, now done with software instead of a diverter resistor. The switches

The story is really a story about semiconductors.

Every advance here was unlocked by a better power switch — a device that can block thousands of volts and switch hundreds of amps thousands of times a second, without melting.

  • Thyristor / GTO (1970s–90s). The Gate Turn-Off thyristor made the first practical traction inverters. Big, slow-switching, and needing bulky snubber circuits — but they worked, and brushless three-phase traction became real.
  • IGBT (1990s–today). The Insulated-Gate Bipolar Transistor switches faster and is far easier to drive, allowing finer pulse-width modulation, quieter motors, smaller and lighter converters. It is the workhorse of virtually all modern traction.
  • SiC — silicon carbide (now). Wide-bandgap devices switch faster still with lower losses and run hotter, shrinking the converter and its cooling further, and squeezing out a few more percent of efficiency. The current frontier.

Why do these trains sound like they're changing gear?

Anyone who has stood on a platform knows the noise: an early inverter train pulls away with a series of rising, stepped tones — nyoom… nyoom… nyoom — exactly as if it were shifting through a gearbox. It isn't. A traction motor has a single fixed gear. The "gear changes" are the inverter switching its modulation pattern, and the sound comes straight out of the motor's iron.

Here is the mechanism. To build its sine wave, the inverter chops the DC link on and off — pulse-width modulation (PWM) — thousands of times a second. Each switching edge slams magnetic forces through the motor's laminations and windings, which flex by microns (magnetostriction); that vibration at the switching frequency is radiated as an audible tone. The pitch you hear is essentially the switching frequency.

Now the catch that creates the "gears". An old GTO/thyristor inverter could only switch a few hundred times a second — far too slow to keep a smooth high carrier across the whole speed range. So the controller runs a ladder of discrete PWM modes: it starts with asynchronous PWM at a fixed carrier (a steady tone while the train creeps), then switches to synchronous PWM and steps the pulse count down — 15 pulses per cycle, then 9, 7, 5, 3 — as output frequency climbs, finally dropping to one-pulse (a plain square wave) at speed. Every mode change is a sudden jump in tone: the gear-change sound. Drag the speed and hear it for yourself.

So, not gears at all

The takeaway: those "gear changes" are the audible signature of a slow switch working hard. As semiconductors got faster (GTO → IGBT → SiC), the steps merged into a smooth sweep and then rose out of earshot entirely. The sound is disappearing — a quirk of one technological moment, captured in noise.

The next motor

Induction vs permanent-magnet.

With the inverter doing the hard work, attention turned back to the motor itself. The permanent-magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) replaces the induction motor's induced rotor currents with powerful rare-earth magnets. Because the rotor no longer has to be magnetised by the stator, the PMSM is even more efficient and more compact — compact enough that motors can be built directly into the wheelset or bogie, sometimes doing away with the gearbox.

Induction — the default

  • Dead simple, dirt cheap rotor
  • Utterly robust; tolerant of heat and overload
  • No magnets to demagnetise or to source
  • Slightly lower efficiency; needs magnetising current

PMSM — the high-performer

  • Highest efficiency and power density
  • Enables small, gearless / direct-drive motors
  • Costly rare-earth magnets; supply-chain risk
  • Magnets can demagnetise if overheated; harder to make safe in a fault

High-speed trains and modern metros increasingly use PMSM where efficiency and packaging matter most; the rugged induction motor remains the pragmatic default everywhere else. Either way, the commutator and its brushes — the thing that defined traction for a century — are simply gone.
