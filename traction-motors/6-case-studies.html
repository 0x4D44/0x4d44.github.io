Featured · success

The Pennsylvania GG1 — the AC commutator motor at its zenith.

The GG1 is the proof that the awkward single-phase AC commutator motor, done with enough care, could be magnificent. It drew 11 kV, 25 Hz AC through a pantograph, stepped it down with a central transformer, and controlled speed with a 21-position tap-changer — no starting resistors. Twelve six-pole, 400 V, 25 Hz commutator motors, mounted in pairs over each of six axles, fed their torque through quill drives to the wheels.

Two decisions from earlier chapters meet here. The 25 Hz frequency (Ch. 03) was low enough to let an ordinary-style series commutator motor run on AC. And the frame-mounted quill drive (Ch. 05) carried the motors on the sprung frame, keeping unsprung mass low so a long, heavy locomotive could run smoothly at 100 mph. Built from 1934, GG1s ran into the 1980s — one of the longest careers of any locomotive design.

The cautionary footnote

In a 1958 blizzard, exceptionally fine snow blew straight through the traction-motor air filters; when it melted it short-circuited the windings and disabled nearly half the fleet. A vivid reminder of Chapter 05's lesson — a traction motor lives or dies by how it breathes, and forced-air cooling is also a path for the weather to get in.

The verdict of history

Four standards survived. The motor under them changed completely.

For all the variety, the industry converged on just four electrification standards — 1.5 kV DC, 3 kV DC, 15 kV 16⅔ Hz, and 25 kV 50/60 Hz — and they coexist to this day. What is striking is that the wire proved more permanent than the machine. A 16⅔ Hz line strung for AC commutator motors in 1912 now feeds brushless inverter trains; a 1.5 kV DC line built for resistance-controlled series motors now runs IGBT-driven induction motors.

That is the real throughline of this study. The supply, the substations and the catenary are civil engineering that lasts a century. The traction motor and its control are where the relentless progress happened — from burning energy in resistor grids, through the delicate compromise of low-frequency commutation, to silicon switches synthesising perfect three-phase power for a motor with nothing to wear out. The job never changed. The cleverness with which we did it changed beyond recognition.

One sentence

The history of the electric traction motor is the history of moving the cleverness out of the iron and into the electronics — until the motor could finally be the simplest, toughest thing in the system.
