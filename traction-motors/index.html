The machines that
move the railway.

From the brush-sparking DC series motor to the brushless inverter drive — how electric traction motors work, why they were built the way they were, and the compromises behind every design.

A traction motor must start a 1,000-tonne train from rest, haul it up a 1-in-37 bank, then spin to 200 km/h — all from a single shaft geared to an axle. No other motor is asked to do quite so much. This study takes the machine apart, electrically and mechanically.

How to read this study

One question, asked six ways.

Every chapter circles the same engineering problem: how do you get usable, controllable torque to a steel wheel on a steel rail, across a 10-to-1 speed range, reliably, for thirty years?

The answer changed completely between 1890 and today — not because the physics changed, but because what was buildable changed. Resistors gave way to tap-changers; mercury arcs to thyristors; commutators to silicon. We follow that thread, and stop often to look at the real locomotives where each idea succeeded or failed.

Diagrams are interactive where you see the orange signal colour — drag, toggle, and drive the models. All figures are schematic.
