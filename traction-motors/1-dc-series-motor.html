The DC series motor: born for the rail.

For the first eighty years of electric traction, one machine did almost all the work. Not because it was elegant — because its torque appeared exactly where a train needs it: at a standstill, under maximum load.

The problem

A train asks for the opposite of what a motor likes to give.

To accelerate a heavy train you need enormous force at zero speed — to overcome inertia, gradient and the high "breakaway" friction of a standing train. Once moving, the force required falls: at cruising speed you only fight rolling resistance and air. So the ideal traction machine produces huge torque at a standstill and progressively less as it speeds up, naturally tapering to a constant-power haul at line speed.

A motor whose torque rose with current — and whose speed fell off as you loaded it — would match this demand almost for free. That motor is the series-wound DC machine.

Why series-wound

Field and armature share one current. That one decision explains everything.

In any DC motor, torque is the product of the magnetic field flux Φ and the armature current Iₐ:

Torque
T = k · Φ · Iₐ

In a series motor the field winding is wired in series with the armature, so the same current makes the field. Below magnetic saturation Φ ∝ Iₐ, therefore T ∝ Iₐ². Double the current, quadruple the pull.

Speed
N ∝ (V − IₐR) / Φ

Speed follows the back-EMF divided by flux. As load (and so current) rises, Φ rises and the numerator falls — speed drops steeply. The motor automatically slows under load and races when light.

This gives the series motor its famously "soft" characteristic: a hyperbola of speed against torque. At a standstill — where back-EMF is zero and current is huge — torque is at its maximum. As the train accelerates, back-EMF builds, current falls, and torque eases off exactly as the demand does. The motor is, in effect, self-regulating: it shares load gracefully between motors on different-diameter wheels, and it cannot easily stall a supply.

Compare — the shunt motor

Wire the field across the line instead (a shunt motor) and Φ is fixed by line voltage. Torque becomes merely proportional to current, and speed stays nearly constant regardless of load — wonderful for a lathe, useless for starting a train, which is why shunt and compound motors stayed rare in traction.

The catch

Take the load away and it will tear itself apart.

Look at the left edge of the curve: as current approaches zero, flux approaches zero, and speed (N ∝ 1/Φ) shoots toward infinity. An unloaded series motor runs away — it can over-speed until centrifugal force throws the armature windings out of their slots.

Traction gets away with it because a motor geared to an axle is never truly unloaded: bearing drag, gear losses and the train itself always present a load. But it is why series motors are bolted to their gearing, never belt-driven, and why a snapped coupling or a wildly slipping wheel was historically a real hazard.

The hardest part

Commutation: reversing current in a moving coil, thousands of times a second.

The commutator's job is to keep reversing the current in each armature coil at the instant it passes between poles, so torque always pushes the same way. But reversing current in a coil that has inductance, while it is briefly short-circuited by the brush, is genuinely difficult — do it badly and you get sparking, burnt commutator bars, and ultimately a destructive flashover across the whole commutator.

Two refinements make it survivable, and both appear in the drawing above:

  • Interpoles (commutating poles). Small poles set between the main poles, carrying armature current. They induce just enough voltage in the commutating coil to cancel its self-inductance and drive the current cleanly to zero and back — automatically scaling with load.
  • Compensating windings. Conductors bedded into the main pole faces, again carrying armature current, that cancel the distorting effect of armature reaction — the way the armature's own magnetism skews the main field and pushes the "neutral" commutating zone out of position under heavy load.
Why this matters for everything that follows

Commutation is the Achilles' heel of the DC motor. It limits how much current you can push, demands constant brush and commutator maintenance, and — crucially — gets dramatically worse on alternating current. That single weakness drives the entire story of AC traction in Chapter 03.
