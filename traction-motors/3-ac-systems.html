AC systems: the transformer, and the frequency problem it created.

Alternating current brought one decisive gift — the transformer — and one stubborn curse: the commutator motor hates AC. Half a century of traction engineering is the story of reconciling the two.

The gift

A transformer can only work on AC — and that one fact reshaped the railway.

To send power down a catenary you want high voltage and low current: current is what heats and wastes in the wire (loss ∝ I²R), so for a given power, raising the voltage lets you use a thinner conductor, space the feeder substations far apart, and lose less along the way. Transmission engineers learned this lesson with the grid.

But a DC motor needs a few hundred volts, not tens of thousands. On DC there is no simple way to step voltage down, so DC railways are stuck at low catenary voltages (600–3000 V) with heavy conductors and substations every few kilometres. On AC, a transformer on the locomotive steps the catenary down to whatever the traction equipment needs — and a tap-changer on that transformer gives smooth, near-lossless voltage control, replacing the wasteful starting resistors of Chapter 02.

Hold the delivered power fixed and slide the catenary voltage. Current — and the copper, the losses, and the substation density it forces — fall away as voltage climbs. This is the whole economic case for AC.

The curse

Feed alternating current to a commutator motor and it fights back.

You can build an AC version of the series motor — laminate the whole magnetic circuit so the alternating flux doesn't cook it with eddy currents, and because field and armature current reverse together, torque stays one-way. Such AC series (commutator) motors were built in large numbers. But AC attacks the commutator in two ways that DC never did:

  • Transformer EMF. The coil briefly short-circuited by a brush as it commutates is wrapped around the pole — so it behaves like the secondary of a transformer linked to the alternating main flux. That induces a voltage in the shorted coil proportional to frequency, driving a heavy circulating current and vicious sparking at the brush.
  • Reactance & power factor. The windings are inductive; their reactance X = 2πfL rises with frequency. Higher frequency means a larger reactive voltage drop, a poorer power factor, and more volt-amps demanded from the supply for the same useful power.

Both problems scale with frequency, and both vanish at DC. So if you are committed to a commutator motor but want the transformer, the obvious move is to make the frequency as low as you practically can.

Why 16⅔ hertz?

Slide the supply frequency from DC up to industrial 50 Hz and watch what it does to the commutator. The sweet spot is a compromise: low enough that the commutator motor survives, but still AC so the transformer works — and a tidy one-third of 50 Hz, so it could be generated by rotary converters from the public grid.

The price of low frequency

It is not free. A 16⅔ Hz supply needs its own generation and distribution — separate power stations, or converter stations turning grid power into railway power — a parallel electricity network. And transformers and motors at low frequency are physically larger and heavier for the same power, because magnetic components scale inversely with frequency. Several countries (Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, Norway) judged it worth building anyway, and run 15 kV 16⅔ Hz to this day.

The road not taken

Three-phase: brushless decades early, and abandoned anyway.

There was always another way to use AC: the three-phase induction motor — rugged, brushless, no commutator at all. Early Italian and other mountain railways ran it, and it offered something remarkable for its day: easy regenerative braking down the long descents, feeding energy back into the supply.

But before power electronics it had crippling drawbacks. An induction motor's speed is set by the supply frequency, which was fixed — so you got only a few fixed speeds, changed clumsily by switching pole numbers or cascading machines. Worse, three phases down a railway needed two overhead wires plus the rail, with complicated crossings and junctions. The idea was sound but a century too early; it was abandoned — and then reborn triumphantly once the inverter could synthesise a variable frequency on board (Chapter 04).

The 50 Hz answer

If you can't beat the commutator's hatred of AC — convert the AC.

The breakthrough that opened up cheap, standard 50 Hz industrial frequency traction was to stop asking the motor to run on AC at all. Take 25 kV 50 Hz straight off the public grid, step it down with the on-board transformer and tap-changer, then rectify it to DC and feed a conventional, well-understood DC series motor. Pioneered in France in the 1950s, 25 kV 50 Hz became the global standard for new electrification.

The rectifier itself evolved through three generations:

1st

Mercury-arc / ignitron

Glass or steel tubes of mercury vapour. Effective but delicate, needing careful temperature control — sometimes water-cooled — and hazardous to maintain.

2nd

Silicon diode

Solid-state diodes: robust, compact, maintenance-free. They made the rectifier locomotive simple and reliable, and dominated through the 1960s–70s.

3rd

Thyristor

Controllable rectifiers let you vary the DC voltage electronically, finally retiring the tap-changer and resistors — the doorway to the inverter age.

The rectifier locomotive was a brilliant bridge: the transmission economics of high-voltage AC married to the proven, well-loved DC traction motor. But it still carried that motor's commutator and brushes. The last barrier — getting rid of the commutator entirely — would fall only when electronics could build a variable-frequency supply for a brushless motor.
