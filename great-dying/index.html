The end of the Palaeozoic

The day the world nearly ended for good

There have been five great mass extinctions in the last half-billion years. The one everybody knows — the asteroid that ended the dinosaurs — is only the most recent. It is not the worst. The worst happened 186 million years earlier, at the close of the Permian period, and it came so close to sterilising the planet that biologists call it, without much exaggeration, the Great Dying.

Around 251.9 million years ago, something erased roughly 96% of all marine species and about 70% of land vertebrates. Whole branches of the tree of life that had thrived for a quarter of a billion years — the trilobites, the sea scorpions, the great rugose corals — simply stopped. It is the only event in the entire fossil record that came close to killing off insects. Nothing else in Earth's history erased so much.

And there was no asteroid. No single moment. The killing took tens of thousands of years, and the murder weapon was the planet's own chemistry, turned against everything living in it. This page is about how a volcano in Siberia cooked the atmosphere, boiled the tropics, suffocated the oceans, and left a scar in the rock you can still put a finger on today.

The murder weapon · a continent of fire

The Siberian Traps

The trigger was the largest volcanic event of the last 500 million years: the Siberian Traps, a flood basalt province that buried an area the size of Australia under sheet after sheet of lava. Not a single eruption — a million years of them. Here is that volume against eruptions you may have heard of. Watch the familiar ones vanish.

But the lava itself was almost a side-effect. The real catastrophe was what the magma passed through on its way up. The Traps erupted through the Tunguska Basin — thousands of metres of coal, oil shale, limestone and salt. The rising heat cooked those rocks like a kiln, baking out carbon dioxide, methane and toxic halocarbons on a scale no ordinary volcano could manage. The eruption didn't just add lava to the world. It added carbon — and that is what did the killing.

The killing floor · a sea with no air

The ocean stopped breathing

Warm water holds less oxygen — every diver and aquarium keeper knows it. Heat the whole ocean by ten degrees, stop the currents that stir it, and the deep sea goes stale. Then bacteria that breathe sulfur instead of oxygen take over, filling the water with poisonous hydrogen sulfide. This is where most of the 96% actually died. What grew back

The empty world, and the door it opened

For millions of years afterwards the world stayed broken. There is a coal gap in the rock — no coal forms anywhere on Earth for some five million years, because the peat-forming forests were simply gone. There is a reef gap, a chert gap. In the earliest Triassic, a single stubby, tusked, burrowing herbivore called Lystrosaurus — pig-sized, unglamorous, tough — accounted for as much as nine in ten of all land vertebrate fossils. The world had been sorted down to the survivors, and for a while the survivors had it almost entirely to themselves.

But an emptied world is an opportunity. Among the scattered reptile lineages that scraped through were the archosaurs — and in the recovering Triassic, freed from the competitors the Permian had piled up, one branch of them grew larger, faster and stranger until it became the dinosaurs. The Great Dying did not just end the Palaeozoic. By clearing the board, it wrote the opening move of the age of dinosaurs.

Which is the strange symmetry of this story. The catastrophe that made the dinosaurs possible was a slow one — carbon, heat and a suffocating sea, over tens of thousands of years. The catastrophe that ended them, 186 million years later, was the opposite: a single rock from space, and an afternoon.
