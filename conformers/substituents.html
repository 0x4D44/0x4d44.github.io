Chapter 5 · Substituted cyclohexanes

Room for big groups

In plain cyclohexane the two chairs are identical, so the ring flip changes nothing. Put a single methyl group on the ring and suddenly the two chairs are different molecules-in-waiting: in one the methyl is axial, in the other equatorial. One of them is noticeably more comfortable, and working out which — and by how much — is what conformational analysis is for.

1Axial is crowded

Here is methylcyclohexane with the methyl group axial. An axial bond points straight up, parallel to the ring's axis — and so do the two other axial bonds on the same face, two carbons along in each direction. All three are packed together on the same side of the ring, uncomfortably close. Flip the ring and the methyl swings out to an equatorial position, pointing away from everything.

When the methyl is axial, the two hydrogens it collides with glow red. These are 1,3-diaxial interactions: the methyl is on carbon 1, the hydrogens on carbons 3 and 5 — "1,3" relative to it in both directions.

Each 1,3-diaxial CH3/H interaction costs about 3.8 kJ/mol, and an axial methyl has two of them, so the axial chair is 7.6 kJ/mol higher in energy than the equatorial chair. That number might ring a bell.

2Gauche butane in disguise

Look at axial methylcyclohexane along the C1–C2 bond, and the ring carbon C3 is sitting 60° from the methyl — exactly the arrangement of gauche butane. Look at the equatorial conformer the same way and C3 is 180° from the methyl: anti butane.

Axial methyl: gauche to C3

Equatorial methyl: anti to C3

Looking from C1 (front) to C2 (back). The ring continues from C2 to C3 behind the circle, and from C1 to C6 in front. The same is true along C1–C6, with C5 in the role of C3.

An axial methyl is gauche to two ring carbons (C3, via the C1–C2 bond, and C5, via the C1–C6 bond). An equatorial methyl is anti to both. So the penalty is two gauche-butane interactions: 2 × 3.8 = 7.6 kJ/mol. The "1,3-diaxial interaction" and the "gauche interaction" are two descriptions of the same crowding — one counting the hydrogens that get bumped, the other counting the carbons they're attached to. Use either, but don't count both.

Rule of thumb

Substituents prefer to be equatorial. The bigger the group, the stronger the preference.

3How strong is the preference?

Both chairs are in rapid equilibrium, so the energy gap ΔG° sets the ratio between them through the familiar relationship:

K = [equatorial] / [axial] = eΔG°/RT

Values are typical textbook figures (twice the 1,3-diaxial cost per H). Measured A-values vary a little with solvent and method.

Three patterns are worth understanding rather than memorising:

  • Ethyl ≈ methyl. An axial ethyl group can rotate so that its CH3 points away from the ring, leaving only a hydrogen facing the axial hydrogens. Isopropyl can still turn its single H inwards, so it's only a little worse.
  • tert-Butyl is enormous. It has no hydrogen to turn inward: whatever way it rotates, one of its methyls points straight at the axial hydrogens. The axial conformer is so bad (~23 kJ/mol) that a t-butyl group effectively locks the ring in the chair that puts it equatorial — a trick chemists use to freeze a ring's conformation.
  • Halogens are small. Bromine is a big atom, but the C–Br bond is long (1.93 Å against 1.54 for C–C), which holds it further from the axial hydrogens. Size and distance roughly cancel.

4Two substituents: cis and trans

With two groups on the ring there's a new question: are they on the same face (cis) or opposite faces (trans)? That's configuration — fixed for good, because flipping never moves a group to the other face. But within each isomer the ring can still flip, and each chair puts the groups in different axial/equatorial positions.

Build a dimethylcyclohexane and the page will draw both chairs and do the bookkeeping.

Run through all six and a neat pattern falls out:

cistrans
1,2a,e ⇌ e,ae,e ⇌ a,a
1,3e,e ⇌ a,aa,e ⇌ e,a
1,4a,e ⇌ e,ae,e ⇌ a,a

Whichever isomer can put both groups equatorial at once is the more stable one: trans-1,2, cis-1,3 and trans-1,4. You don't need to memorise the table — just draw one chair, put the groups in, and flip it.

What if the two groups are different?

When one chair has group X axial and the other has group Y axial, compare their A-values: the chair with the smaller group axial wins. Take cis-1-tert-butyl-4-methylcyclohexane: one chair has methyl axial (7.6 kJ/mol), the other has t-butyl axial (~23 kJ/mol). The molecule sits almost entirely in the first.

✓Quick check

Question 1

In the more stable chair of trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane, the methyls are…

On C1 and C4, "up" and "down" on opposite faces correspond to the same kind of position. trans-1,4 can be e,e (0 kJ/mol of methyl strain) or a,a (15.2 kJ/mol); the e,e chair dominates.

Question 2

Why does a tert-butyl group have such a large A-value compared with isopropyl?

Isopropyl can turn so that only its small hydrogen points into the ring. t-Butyl has three methyls and no hydrogen, so it can't dodge.

Question 3

Cyclohexanol's OH has an A-value of about 4.2 kJ/mol. Roughly what fraction is equatorial at 25 °C?

K = e4.2/2.48 ≈ 5.4, so the equatorial fraction is 5.4/6.4 ≈ 84%.
