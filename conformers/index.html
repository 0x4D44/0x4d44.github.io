Chapter 1 · Overview

Molecules that won't sit still

The butane in a lighter isn't one shape. Every molecule in it is twisting about its middle bond billions of times a second, flickering between a handful of favourite poses. Those poses are called conformations, and learning to see them is one of the most useful habits in organic chemistry.

1Single bonds are axles

Draw butane the way you first learned to — CH3–CH2–CH2–CH3 — and it looks like a fixed, flat zig-zag. It isn't. Each carbon–carbon single bond behaves like an axle: the two ends can spin relative to each other without the bond breaking.

The reason is the shape of the bond itself. A single bond is a σ (sigma) bond: the electron density sits on the line between the two nuclei, and it is cylindrically symmetric around that line, like a sausage. Spin one end and the overlap between the two orbitals doesn't change at all — so nothing is lost by turning.

A double bond is different. On top of its σ bond it has a π (pi) bond, made from two p orbitals that overlap side-by-side, above and below the bond. Twist one end and those p orbitals stop pointing the same way; at 90° they don't overlap at all and the π bond is gone. Breaking it costs roughly 270 kJ/mol — far more than everyday thermal jostling can supply — so double bonds are, in practice, locked.

The key idea

Rotation about single bonds is (nearly) free. Rotation about double bonds is not. Everything in this guide follows from that one asymmetry.

2Conformation or configuration?

Two words that sound alike and mean very different things. It's worth getting them straight now, because exam questions love to blur them.

A conformer (short for conformational isomer) is a conformation that sits at an energy minimum — one of the poses a molecule actually lingers in, rather than one it merely passes through. Butane has three conformers (one anti, two gauche); it passes through infinitely many other conformations on the way between them.

A useful test: could I get from one structure to the other by twisting single bonds, without lifting a bond off the page? If yes, they're conformations of the same molecule. If you'd have to break something, they're different configurations — genuinely different compounds.

3Not all poses are equal

"Free" rotation isn't quite free. As a molecule twists, its atoms and bonds drift closer together and further apart, and the energy rises and falls. Chemists call any energy penalty for a bad geometry strain, and in this guide you'll meet three kinds:

Torsional strain

The cost of eclipsing: bonds on neighbouring carbons lined up behind one another, their electron clouds pushing apart.

Steric strain

The cost of crowding: two groups pushed closer than their sizes allow, so their electron clouds collide.

Angle strain

The cost of bending: forcing a bond angle away from the 109.5° that an sp³ carbon wants. Mostly a ring problem.

Every conformational question in this course is the same question in disguise: add up the strains in each pose, and the lowest total wins. Mostly wins, anyway — which brings us to temperature.

4How big is "small"?

Molecules in a liquid or gas are constantly being hit by their neighbours. At room temperature the typical energy of that jostling is about RT ≈ 2.5 kJ/mol. Occasionally a lucky run of collisions delivers much more, and the rate at which a molecule climbs over an energy barrier Ea is set by how rare those lucky runs are:

rate ≈ (6 × 1012 s−1) × e−Ea / RT

That exponential is ferocious. Every extra 5.7 kJ/mol of barrier makes the process ten times slower. Slide the barrier and watch the timescale fly past.

Everything below about 60–80 kJ/mol happens faster than you can blink, so those shapes are conformers that swap freely. Somewhere around 100 kJ/mol, swaps start taking hours to years, and the shapes behave like separate compounds you could put in different bottles. That fuzzy boundary is exactly the line between conformations and configurations.

Where does the 6 × 10¹² come from?

It's kBT/h — Boltzmann's constant times temperature divided by Planck's constant — the "attempt frequency" from transition-state theory, roughly how often a molecular vibration tries its luck at the barrier. At 298 K it comes to 6.2 × 1012 per second. You don't need the derivation this year; what matters is the exponential.

5Drawing 3D on flat paper

Half of conformational analysis is just drawing the shapes so you can see the strains. Chemists use a handful of conventions, each good at one job:

  • Wedge-and-dash — a solid wedge comes towards you, a hashed wedge goes away. Good for showing a single carbon's 3D arrangement.
  • Sawhorse — the C–C bond drawn diagonally, in perspective. Good for seeing both carbons at once.
  • Newman projection — looking straight down one bond. The best tool for judging staggered vs eclipsed. Chapter 2.
  • The chair — a stylised drawing of cyclohexane that encodes every up, down, axial and equatorial position. Chapter 4.

✓Quick check

Question 1

cis-But-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene are…

Getting from one to the other means twisting the C=C bond through 90°, which breaks the π bond (~270 kJ/mol). At room temperature that never happens, so they are separate, bottleable compounds.

Question 2

A barrier rises from 12 to 24 kJ/mol. Roughly how much slower does the molecule cross it at 25 °C?

Each 5.7 kJ/mol is a factor of ten, and 12 kJ/mol is about two of those: e−12/2.48 ≈ 1/126. Doubling a barrier does far more than double the wait.
