Chapter 4 · Cyclohexane

The chair

Cyclohexane manages something no other small ring can: every bond angle comfortable, every bond perfectly staggered, no strain at all. It does it by folding into a zig-zag that looks, with a little imagination, like a deckchair. Learn this one shape properly and a large chunk of organic chemistry opens up.

1Meet the chair

Take a flat hexagon of carbons. Lift alternate corners up and push the others down, so the ring zig-zags round its edge: up, down, up, down, up, down. That's the chair. Two things come out of it, both exactly right:

  • The angles. Every C–C–C angle is about 111°, a whisker from the ideal 109.5°. No angle strain.
  • The twists. Look along any C–C bond and the substituents are perfectly staggered. No torsional strain.

2Axial and equatorial

Each carbon in the chair carries two hydrogens, and they're not equivalent. Every carbon has exactly one of each kind:

  • Axial bonds point straight up or straight down, parallel to the ring's axis. They alternate round the ring: up, down, up, down. Three point up, three point down.
  • Equatorial bonds stick out sideways, around the ring's "equator". They tilt slightly too — down where the axial bond goes up, and up where it goes down.

So on every carbon, one hydrogen is on the top face of the ring and one is on the bottom face, and one is axial while the other is equatorial. Switch the colouring above to "up / down" and you'll see that up and down don't line up with axial and equatorial: on an "up" carbon the axial bond is the up one, on a "down" carbon the equatorial bond is the up one. Keeping those two ideas separate is the whole game in the next chapter.

Remember

Axial = parallel to the axis, straight up or down. Equatorial = roughly in the plane, pointing outwards. Each carbon: one axial, one equatorial; one up, one down.

Common mistakes to look out for
  • Equatorial bonds drawn horizontal. They're never horizontal — each one is parallel to a ring bond.
  • Axial bonds slanted. They're always vertical: up at the corners that point up, down at the corners that point down.
  • Equatorial bond drawn inside the ring. Equatorial bonds always point away from the ring.
  • The two ends of the chair at the same height. One tip goes up, the other goes down; that's what makes it a chair rather than a boat.

4The ring flip

The chair isn't rigid. By twisting about its C–C bonds — no bonds broken — it can turn itself inside out into a second chair. The headrest swings down, the footrest swings up, and the molecule passes through a series of less comfortable shapes on the way.

Watch the red hydrogens — axial in the starting chair. After the flip, every one of them is equatorial, and every blue one is axial. But a red hydrogen that pointed up still points up. The energy profile is schematic: the real pathway wanders through two twist-boats, and the true boat is a small hump between them.

The stops along the way:

  • Half-chair (≈45 kJ/mol) — five carbons nearly in a plane, so lots of eclipsing and squashed angles. This is the top of the barrier.
  • Twist-boat (≈23 kJ/mol) — a genuine but shallow energy minimum; a twisted boat that relieves some of the boat's problems.
  • Boat (≈30 kJ/mol) — both ends up. Four bonds are eclipsed, and the two "flagpole" hydrogens at the bow and stern point at each other, only about 1.8 Å apart: steric strain.

A 45 kJ/mol barrier is small. At room temperature each cyclohexane molecule flips around 105 times a second, so you can't isolate the two chairs of cyclohexane any more than you can isolate the gauche and anti forms of butane. Cool it to −100 °C, though, and the flipping slows enough that NMR spectroscopy can see axial and equatorial hydrogens as separate signals.

Why doesn't the boat count as a conformer?

Look at the boat along its two side bonds and you'll find them fully eclipsed, and its flagpole hydrogens bumping. Twisting slightly relieves both at once, so the boat slides downhill into a twist-boat. That makes the boat a hilltop (a transition state between two twist-boats) and the twist-boat the real local minimum. At room temperature only about one molecule in 104 is in a twist-boat at any moment; for practical purposes cyclohexane is all chairs.

5Up stays up

The flip is the reason it's so important to separate the two vocabularies:

Changes in a ring flip?Describes
axial / equatorialyes — every axial becomes equatorial and vice versathe conformation (which chair you're in)
up / down (wedge / dash)no — a group on the top face stays on the top facethe configuration (which molecule you have)

Flipping turns the ring inside out, not upside down. Since no bond is broken, nothing can swap faces. That's why cis and trans ring compounds stay cis and trans through any number of flips — and it's the key to the next chapter.

✓Quick check

Question 1

How many axial hydrogens does chair cyclohexane have, and how many point up?

Every carbon has one axial bond, so there are six. Their direction alternates round the ring: up on the carbons that pucker up, down on the ones that pucker down.

Question 2

A hydrogen is axial and pointing down. After a ring flip it is…

A flip swaps axial and equatorial, but never moves a group to the other face of the ring. Down stays down.

Question 3

Which shape is the highest-energy point on the ring-flip pathway?

The half-chair (≈45 kJ/mol) has five carbons nearly coplanar, with heavy eclipsing and angle strain. The boat (≈30) and twist-boat (≈23) are both lower.
