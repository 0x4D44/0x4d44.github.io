Chapter 3 · Ring strain

The trouble with rings

Tie the two ends of an alkane chain together and you lose a freedom: the bonds can no longer turn however they like. Now the ring has to make compromises — between the angles it would like, the twists it would like, and the room its atoms need. The bill for those compromises is called ring strain.

1Baeyer's flat rings

In 1885 Adolf von Baeyer reasoned like this. A saturated carbon is tetrahedral and wants bond angles of 109.5°. Rings, he assumed, are flat — regular polygons. And the interior angle of a flat polygon is fixed by its number of corners. So every ring except one of exactly the right size must have its angles bent away from 109.5°, and the bigger the bend, the more strained the ring.

Baeyer's theory predicts that cyclopentane (108°) should be the happiest ring, and that rings get steadily more strained from cyclohexane upwards, as the flat angles open out past 109.5°. Half of that is right. To find out which half, we need to measure strain.

2Measuring strain by setting fire to it

Strain is stored energy — a compressed spring. The cleanest way to measure stored energy is to release all of it. So chemists burn the rings and measure the heat:

(CH2)n + 1.5n O2 → n CO2 + n H2O

Every cycloalkane is just a loop of CH2 groups, and every CH2 ends up as the same CO2 and H2O. In a long, relaxed alkane chain, each CH2 gives out 658.6 kJ/mol when it burns. If a ring gives out more than that per CH2, the extra was stored in the ring as strain.

Strain = n × (heat per CH₂ − 658.6). Cyclopropane releases 38.5 kJ/mol extra for each of its three CH2 groups: 115 kJ/mol of strain. Cyclohexane releases exactly the unstrained value.

Baeyer was right about small rings: three- and four-membered rings are badly strained. But he was wrong about the rest. Cyclohexane, which "should" have 10.5° of bend, has no strain at all, and rings bigger than six are only mildly strained. Baeyer's mistake was the assumption that rings are flat. Real rings with four or more carbons pucker.

3The three bills a ring pays

Ring strain is the total of the same three penalties you met in chains:

  • Angle strain (Baeyer strain) — ring angles squeezed or stretched away from 109.5°.
  • Torsional strain — bonds on neighbouring ring carbons eclipsed. In a flat ring, every single C–C bond is fully eclipsed, because a flat ring forces every dihedral to 0°.
  • Steric strain — atoms across the ring bumping into each other. In rings this is called transannular strain ("across the ring"), and it matters most in 7- to 11-membered rings, whose inward-pointing hydrogens get in each other's way.

Here's the tension. Flattening a ring is good for some angles but forces every bond to eclipse. Puckering staggers the bonds but usually squeezes the angles. A real ring settles wherever the sum is smallest — and you can find that point yourself.

5Ring by ring

Cyclopropane — the coiled spring

Three points always lie in a plane, so cyclopropane is flat whether it likes it or not. Its C–C–C angles are 60°, a staggering 49.5° below ideal, and all six of its C–H bonds are eclipsed with their neighbours. Strain: 115 kJ/mol.

Cyclopropane copes with the impossible angle by cheating. The carbon orbitals can't point along the 60° lines between the nuclei, so they point outside the triangle, at about 104° to each other, and overlap at an angle. The result is the bent bond or "banana bond": weaker than a normal C–C bond, because the overlap is poor, with electron density bulging outside the ring.

The orbitals (purple) point about 22° outside the line between the nuclei. They still overlap — but sideways, and less well. That weak, strained bond is why cyclopropane rings snap open in reactions that leave other alkanes untouched; with 115 kJ/mol to release, it behaves a little like an alkene.

Cyclobutane — the butterfly

Flat cyclobutane would have 90° angles and eight eclipsed C–H pairs. Instead it folds about one diagonal by roughly 25°, like a butterfly closing its wings. That makes the angles slightly worse (about 88°) but relieves a good chunk of the eclipsing. It's a narrow win, and the butterfly flips its wings back and forth constantly. Strain: 110 kJ/mol, almost as bad as cyclopropane.

Cyclopentane — the envelope

Flat cyclopentane would have angles of 108°, almost perfect. Baeyer loved it. But a flat cyclopentane would have ten eclipsed C–H pairs, about 40 kJ/mol of torsional strain. So it lifts one carbon out of the plane, like the flap of an envelope. The angles get a little worse; the eclipsing gets a lot better. Strain: 26 kJ/mol. The flap doesn't stay on one carbon, either: it ripples round the ring continuously, a motion called pseudorotation.

Cyclohexane — the one that gets it right

Pucker a six-membered ring the right way and something lovely happens: every angle comes out at about 111° and every bond is perfectly staggered — all at once. No angle strain, no torsional strain, no strain at all. That shape is the chair, and it gets the whole next chapter.

And bigger rings?

Seven- to eleven-membered "medium" rings can pucker their way out of angle and torsional strain, but they pay instead in transannular strain: hydrogens on opposite sides of the ring point inwards and collide. Around twelve carbons and up, rings are big and floppy enough to behave like ordinary chains again, nearly strain-free.

The pattern

Small rings (3, 4) are strained because they can't reach good angles. Rings of 4 and more pucker to stagger their bonds. Six is the sweet spot where puckering fixes everything at once — which is why six-membered rings are everywhere in nature, from sugars to steroids.

✓Quick check

Question 1

Cyclopentane isn't flat, even though flat cyclopentane would have nearly ideal 108° angles. Why?

In any flat ring every dihedral is 0°, so every bond is eclipsed. For cyclopentane that's about 40 kJ/mol of torsional strain, which is worth trading a few degrees of angle for.

Question 2

Cyclobutane's heat of combustion is 2744 kJ/mol. What is its ring strain?

An unstrained (CH2)4 would release 4 × 658.6 = 2634 kJ/mol. 2744 − 2634 = 110 kJ/mol extra — that's the stored strain.

Question 3

Which ring has essentially zero ring strain?

The chair gives cyclohexane ~111° angles and fully staggered bonds simultaneously. Its heat of combustion per CH2 is exactly the unstrained 658.6 kJ/mol.
