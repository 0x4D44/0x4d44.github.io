Chapter 2 · Newman projections

Looking down the bond

To judge how a molecule feels about a twist, you need to see the twist. The trick, introduced by Melvin Newman in the 1950s, is almost embarrassingly simple: stop looking at the bond from the side, and look straight along it.

1Turn the molecule end-on

Here is ethane, CH3–CH3, drawn from the side. Two carbons, each carrying three hydrogens. Press the button and the camera swings round until you're looking straight down the carbon–carbon bond.

End-on, the front carbon hides the back one completely. The Newman projection keeps what matters: the front carbon becomes a point where three bonds meet, and the back carbon becomes a circle, with its three bonds poking out from behind the rim.

How to draw one, every time

  1. Pick the bond you want to look along, and decide which carbon is in front.
  2. Front carbon: draw three bonds meeting at a point, 120° apart (a "Y" or an upside-down "Y").
  3. Back carbon: draw a circle centred on that point. Its three bonds start at the edge of the circle, never at the centre.
  4. Add the groups at the ends of each bond. Check each carbon has four things attached: three drawn groups plus the C–C bond you're looking along.

The angle between a front bond and a back bond, measured around the circle, is the dihedral angle (or torsion angle), written θ or φ. It's the single number that describes a twist.

2Ethane: staggered and eclipsed

Now grab the back carbon and turn it. You'll find two special poses repeating every 120°:

  • Staggered — each back bond sits neatly between two front bonds (dihedral 60°, 180°, 300°). Every hydrogen is as far as possible from its neighbours.
  • Eclipsed — each back bond hides directly behind a front bond (dihedral 0°, 120°, 240°). Three pairs of C–H bonds are lined up, nose to nose.

The energy difference is small but real: eclipsed ethane sits 12 kJ/mol above staggered. Because the eclipsed pose has three identical H/H eclipsings, each one costs about 4.0 kJ/mol. That penalty is torsional strain.

Notice what kind of thing the eclipsed pose is. It's not a conformer — it's the top of a hill, a transition state the molecule passes over on its way from one staggered well to the next. With a 12 kJ/mol hill and 2.5 kJ/mol of typical thermal energy, ethane makes that crossing around 1010 times every second. At any instant, nearly every ethane molecule in a sample is staggered or close to it.

Why does eclipsing cost energy at all? Hydrogens are tiny.

They are — the hydrogens in eclipsed ethane are about 2.3 Å apart, roughly their comfortable contact distance, so plain bumping (steric strain) is only a minor part of it. Two effects do most of the work. First, the bonding electron pairs in eclipsed C–H bonds are closer together and repel each other. Second, in the staggered pose each filled C–H bonding orbital on one carbon lines up perfectly with an empty C–H antibonding orbital (σ*) on the other, and a little electron density leaks across, stabilising the molecule. This is called hyperconjugation. Chemists still argue about the exact split between the two; for this course, "eclipsed bonds repel, staggered bonds don't" is the right picture.

One step up: propane

Swap one hydrogen for a methyl group and you have propane. Its barrier is about 14 kJ/mol. Bookkeeping explains it: the eclipsed pose now has two H/H eclipsings (4.0 each) and one H/CH3 eclipsing, which costs a bit more — about 6.0 kJ/mol, because the methyl is bigger. 4.0 + 4.0 + 6.0 = 14. Conformational energies really are this additive, and that's what makes them easy to estimate.

3Butane: the rollercoaster

Butane, CH3CH2–CH2CH3, is the first molecule where the poses stop being equal. Look down the central C2–C3 bond and each carbon carries one methyl group and two hydrogens. Now the dihedral angle that matters is the one between the two methyls.

What's costing energy

Walk round the circle from the best pose to the worst:

  • Anti (180°) — the methyls point in opposite directions. Everything is staggered and nothing is crowded. This is the global minimum: 0 kJ/mol.
  • Gauche (60° and 300°) — still staggered, but the two methyls are now neighbours, 60° apart. Their hydrogens jostle: steric strain of about 3.8 kJ/mol. A local minimum — a genuine conformer, and there are two of them (mirror images).
  • Eclipsed (120° and 240°) — each methyl eclipses a hydrogen: two H/CH3 eclipsings (6.0 each) plus one H/H (4.0) = 16 kJ/mol. A hilltop.
  • Fully eclipsed (0°) — methyl directly behind methyl. CH3/CH3 eclipsing (≈11) plus two H/H (4.0 each) ≈ 19 kJ/mol. The highest point on the curve.

Minimum or maximum?

Staggered poses (anti, gauche) are the conformers — the energy minima. Eclipsed poses are maxima — the barriers between them. If an exam asks you to "draw the most stable conformation", it's always a staggered one.

4So which pose wins?

"Anti is lowest" doesn't mean every butane molecule is anti. Thermal energy keeps kicking molecules up and over the barriers, and the population of each conformer is set by a Boltzmann distribution: a pose that's ΔE higher in energy is less populated by a factor e−ΔE/RT.

Bars: where the molecule has actually been. Gold line: the Boltzmann prediction, ∝ e−E/RT. Cool it down and the molecule gets trapped in whichever well it's in; heat it up and the gauche wells fill in.

At 25 °C the sums come out at about 70% anti and 30% gauche (15% in each gauche well). Gauche is a minority, but hardly rare — and since there are two gauche wells to one anti, entropy partly offsets the energy cost.

That's why long alkane chains are mostly zig-zags. Each C–C bond along the chain prefers to be anti, so a chain left alone stretches out into the familiar zig-zag. Every gauche bond puts a kink in it. Straight, all-anti chains pack together neatly, which is part of why long-chain waxes and saturated fats are solids at room temperature.

✓Quick check

Question 1

In a Newman projection, the bonds to the back carbon are drawn…

The circle is the back carbon, seen from in front. Its bonds emerge from behind the rim; only the front carbon's bonds meet at the centre.

Question 2

Using the table, estimate the energy of the eclipsed conformation of propane relative to staggered.

Two H/H eclipsings (2 × 4.0) plus one H/CH3 eclipsing (6.0) = 14 kJ/mol.

Question 3

Gauche butane is higher in energy than anti butane mainly because of…

Gauche is fully staggered, so there's no torsional strain at all. The 3.8 kJ/mol comes purely from the two methyl groups bumping into each other.
