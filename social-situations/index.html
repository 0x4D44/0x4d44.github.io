A practical framework · not a personality test

Social situations
as learnable systems

A way to separate how you feel about a person, how much interaction the situation requires, and how predictable the situation is — then replace an amorphous social threat with a finite set of recognizable states and responses.

Start with the three axes Open the situation maps
Core ideaThe goal is not “be more social.” It is “know what this situation asks of me, and make it as predictable as possible.”

01 · Why a map helps

“People” is too large a problem.

A lecture hall, a hotel reception desk, a laboratory and a party can all contain dozens of people. Yet they can feel completely different. Headcount alone is a poor predictor of social demand.

The useful question is not simply how many people are there? It is: what interaction is expected, how well defined are the rules, and what happens if something unexpected occurs?

This is a working model

It is deliberately simple. It is not a diagnostic instrument, a clinical scale, or a claim that every social relationship fits neatly into coordinates. Its job is to make planning easier.

The origin is crowded.

Most of the world is approximately neutral + no interaction. That is normal. A lecture can contain hundreds of people without requiring a relationship with any of them.

People move. A lab partner might go from (0,0) to (+0.5,2) during a term, then drift back toward the origin after the course ends. Coordinates are states, not identities.

04 · Situation maps

Turn a setting into a protocol.

Pick a map. Each one separates what is required from what is merely optional, then lists the handful of states most likely to occur.

05 · The predictability trick

Convert ambiguity into a state machine.

2Classify

3Respond

4Return

Go back to the task.

A laboratory feels open-ended because the brain has to generate possibilities continuously: Who starts? What if I am wrong? What if they disagree? What if nobody speaks? What if I need to leave?

But the number of states that actually occur is usually small. If ten common states cover ninety percent of a session, the situation is no longer “anything could happen.” It is “one of these things will probably happen, and I already know my default move.”

07 · Build a new map

Five questions are enough to start.

  1. 01
    What is the setting?

    Where, when, how long, roughly how many people?

  2. 02
    What interaction level is actually required?

    Separate requirements from optional sociability.

  3. 03
    What are the most likely states?

    Arrival, beginning, being asked something, being unsure, disagreement, needing a break, finishing.

  4. 04
    What is the default move for each?

    One sentence or action. Add a Plan B only where needed.

  5. 05
    What changed after trying it?

    Update the map from evidence. Delete states that never occur; add the one surprise that did.

The map belongs to the person using it.

It should increase autonomy, not create another performance standard. A protocol is useful only if it makes the situation easier to navigate.

08 · Evidence & limits

What this borrows from — and what it does not claim.

This framework is an original practical synthesis, not a validated psychological model. Its emphasis on explicit structure, predictable routines, advance information and concrete coping strategies is consistent with established autism-support guidance. It also reflects research linking intolerance of uncertainty with anxiety in autistic people.

That does not mean every difficult interaction should be scripted, every unexpected event can be removed, or anxiety should be treated as a failure. Some contexts are genuinely difficult, sensory load matters, and formal accommodations can be more appropriate than asking an individual to compensate harder.
