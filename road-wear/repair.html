Chapter 6 — Looking After Roads

A pound of prevention

Here is the counter-intuitive heart of road maintenance: the cheapest time to treat a road is while it still looks fine. A road declines slowly for years and then falls off a cliff, and money spent before the cliff buys many times what the same money buys after it. Understand the deterioration curve and you understand why your council seals a road that "didn't need doing" — and why the one that clearly did will cost ten times as much.

A road does not wear out at a steady rate. It holds up well for most of its life, then collapses quickly at the end — and that shape changes everything about how we look after it.

Plot a road's condition against time and you get the famous deterioration curve: a long, gentle decline, then a sharp knee where distress accelerates and the road unravels. The single most important idea in pavement management is where on that curve you intervene.

The rule that runs a highways budget

A widely-cited finding from US pavement studies (and the intuition behind maintenance policy everywhere) is that every £1 spent on preventive maintenance at the right time can save £6–£10 of future reconstruction. The reason is the curve: a road typically drops the first 40% of its quality over ~75% of its life, then the next 40% in a small fraction of the time. Treat it in the gentle zone and you slide back up cheaply; miss the window and you are buying a rebuild. Deferring maintenance to "save money" is one of the most expensive things a road authority can do.

The maintenance ladder

Treatments form a ladder from cheap-and-preventive to dear-and-corrective. You climb it as the road declines — and the whole art is to stay as low on the ladder as possible for as long as possible.

Surface dressing deserves a special mention because it is the workhorse of cost-effective road care and the one people most often misunderstand. It looks slapdash — spray tar, throw down chippings, roll, let the traffic bed them in, sweep up the loose stone a few days later — and it briefly makes a fine road look like a building site. But it does two vital jobs for a fraction of the cost of resurfacing: it seals the surface against water (the enemy of Chapter 3) and it restores skid resistance lost to polishing (Chapter 4). It adds no structural strength — but a sound road doesn't need strength, it needs to be kept sound. That is prevention, working.

Rolla & Tarmac

Tarmac: "People grumble when you dress me — 'that road was fine!'"

Rolla: "That's the whole point. Seal you while you're fine and you stay fine for another decade. Wait till you're full of holes and it's not me you'll need — it's the diggers, and a road closure, and a bill with a lot more noughts."

Patching, and why the patch never matches

The pothole gets filled, and everyone has an opinion about the result. A proper permanent patch is real work: saw-cut a clean rectangle around the failure, dig out the loose and failed material down to something sound, tack the edges with binder, fill with fresh hot asphalt in compacted layers, and roll it. Done well it lasts. The ubiquitous quick patch — cold-mix shovelled into a wet hole and tamped by a passing boot — is triage: it keeps the road usable and stops water getting deeper, but it is expected to fail, because the hole's cause (a failing structure, a live crack, standing water) is still there. That is why patches look different (new material, different age and binder), why they often sink or ravel at the edges, and why a road full of patches is a road that has climbed too high up the ladder and is really asking to be resurfaced.

Managing a whole network

Scale all this up to thousands of miles and you get a Pavement Management System: survey the network's condition (the PCI and IRI of Chapter 5), predict each road's path down its deterioration curve, and then optimise — with a limited budget — which roads to treat, when, and with what, to keep the whole network's average condition as high as possible for the money. Counter-intuitively, that often means spending on good roads to keep them good rather than pouring it all into the worst ones, because a pound holds a good road near the top far more effectively than it lifts a failed one off the bottom. The politics of that — fixing roads that "look fine" while a visibly terrible one waits — is the permanent tension of every highways department.

Go deeperwhole-life cost and the timing of treatments

The formal version of "prevention beats cure" is whole-life costing: instead of minimising the cost of the next treatment, you minimise the total discounted cost of keeping a road serviceable over decades. Because the deterioration curve steepens, the maths almost always favours a series of cheap, well-timed preventive treatments over a single expensive reconstruction — provided each is applied in the window where the road is still structurally sound. The model in the interactive is a deliberately simple version of this: a sigmoid-shaped condition curve, a set of treatments that each reset condition by different amounts at different costs, and a "value" that rewards buying the most road-life per pound. Real systems add traffic growth, seasonal effects, the fourth-power damage of Chapter 2, and the risk that deferring a treatment drops the road past the point where the cheap option still works. But the shape of the answer — treat early, treat cheaply, never let a sound road fail — is exactly what the curve here shows.
