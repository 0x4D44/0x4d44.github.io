Chapter 4 — Ageing

The tar grows old

Even a road that never saw a heavy lorry would eventually fail, because the 5% of it that matters — the bitumen binder — is chemically alive. In sunlight and air it slowly oxidises, stiffening and losing its stretch, until one cold day it cracks under a load it would once have flexed around. Meanwhile the stone polishes smooth beneath the tyres. Age alone can ruin a road; it just takes its time.

Bitumen is the strangest material in civil engineering: solid, liquid and glue at once, and never quite the same from one year to the next.

Recall from Chapter 1 that asphalt is ~95% stone and ~5% bitumen, and that the bitumen is the glue holding the stone skeleton together. Fresh from the refinery it is ductile — pull it and it stretches like toffee, which is exactly what a road needs when a wheel bends it or the cold shrinks it. But bitumen does not stay young.

What "ageing" actually is

Bitumen ages in two phases, driven mostly by reaction with atmospheric oxygen:

  • Short-term ageing happens fast, in the few hours during mixing and laying, when the binder is hot (~150–180 °C) and thinly coated over hot stone with lots of air. The lightest oil fractions boil off (volatilisation) and rapid oxidation stiffens it before it even reaches the road. Laboratories simulate this with the "rolling thin-film oven" test.
  • Long-term ageing is the slow work of years in service: continued oxidation deep in the mat, accelerated at the surface by ultraviolet light and heat. The chemistry shifts the binder toward larger, stiffer molecules (asphaltenes), so it grows harder, more viscous and less able to stretch — exactly the properties you don't want.
Why old roads craze

An aged, brittle binder is why a road that is otherwise structurally fine can still fail from the top down. With too little stretch left, the surface cracks under ordinary thermal movement and traffic flexing, appearing first as a mosaic of interconnected cracks over large areas — block cracking — unrelated to the wheelpaths. And as the binder loses its grip on the stone, individual aggregate particles work loose and are flung out by tyres: the surface roughens, greys and sheds grit — ravelling — which is both a cause of that greyed look of an old road and a feeder of loose stone and open texture for water to exploit.

Meanwhile, the stone polishes

The other half of ageing happens to the aggregate, and it matters for a reason that has nothing to do with strength: skid resistance. A new surface is microscopically rough — sharp stone edges and fine texture that grip a tyre, especially in the wet. But every tyre that passes polishes those stones a little smoother, exactly as a pebble is rounded by a stream. Over years the surface can become dangerously slick even while it looks perfectly sound. Engineers fight this by choosing hard, angular aggregates with a high Polished Stone Value (PSV) for busy or high-risk sites, and by restoring texture with a surface dressing (Chapter 6) — one reason a road may be resurfaced for grip long before it is structurally worn out.

1
Year 0. Fresh and ductile — the binder stretches around every load and cold snap.
2
Oxidising. Sun, air and UV slowly stiffen the tar. It hardens; its stretch quietly disappears.
3
Brittle. Now it can't flex. Thermal movement and traffic craze the surface into block cracking.
4
Ravelling. The binder loses its grip; stones fly out, texture opens, and water gets its invitation.

The redeeming trick: asphalt heals

There is one piece of good news in the bitumen story. Because the binder is viscoelastic and can still flow a little when warm, asphalt has a modest capacity to self-heal: microcracks can knit back together during warm rest periods when the road isn't being loaded. It is a real, measurable effect that engineers are learning to exploit (even trialling induction heating and encapsulated rejuvenators to boost it). It is also why fatigue life depends not just on how many loads a road sees but on how they are spaced — a road allowed to rest and warm recovers a little between batterings. Age steals that capacity too: the stiffer and more oxidised the binder, the less it heals.

Professor Camber

"We spend a fortune choosing a bitumen with the right stiffness for the local climate — soft enough not to crack in the winter cold, stiff enough not to rut in the summer heat. The trouble is the road won't hold still: it keeps ageing toward the brittle end, whatever we picked. Half of maintenance is really about slowing that clock."

Go deeperviscoelasticity and why we grade binders

Bitumen's behaviour depends on both temperature and loading time, because it is viscoelastic. Under a fast load (a car at speed) or in the cold it responds elastically — stiff and springy; under a slow load (a parked lorry) or in the heat it responds viscously — it flows. This is why the same road ruts in a summer heatwave and cracks in a winter freeze, and why axle speed matters as much as axle weight. Modern practice grades binders by performance across the temperature range a site will see — the American Superpave "PG" grades, e.g. PG 64-22, name the high and low pavement temperatures the binder must survive. Ageing shifts a binder's real behaviour away from its design grade toward stiffer, more brittle territory, which is why long-term-ageing tests (the pressure-ageing vessel) are part of qualifying a binder at all. The interactive's numbers — penetration falling from roughly 50 to under 20 tenths-of-a-mm, softening point climbing past 60 °C, ductility collapsing — track the direction of a real paving-grade binder's decline, if not any one exact product.
