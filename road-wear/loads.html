Chapter 2 — The Weight of Traffic

Why one lorry beats ten thousand cars

If you remember one thing from this entire guide, make it this: road damage does not rise in step with weight. It rises with roughly the fourth power of axle load. That single, well-measured fact means the wear on a road is almost entirely the work of heavy goods vehicles, that cars are nearly irrelevant to structural decay, and that a little overloading is startlingly destructive.

In 1958 the American road authorities spent two years and a fortune deliberately destroying roads to answer one question: how much damage does a given load actually do?

The AASHO Road Test in Ottawa, Illinois built special loops of pavement and ran fleets of trucks around them, axle after identical axle, until the roads failed — recording exactly how many passes of each weight it took. The result was one of the most consequential findings in civil engineering, and it was not what anyone expected. Damage per pass rose not linearly with load, not with its square, but with something close to its fourth power.

The arithmetic that runs the roads

Engineers turn this into a single accounting unit: the ESAL, or Equivalent Single Axle Load. Every axle that passes is converted, via its fourth-power load equivalency factor, into some number of "standard 8-tonne axles," and a road is designed to survive a certain total number of ESALs before it needs rebuilding. Count the ESALs and you have counted the damage. Here is what that does to a mixed stream of traffic:

Cars do not wear out roads

Run the numbers and the conclusion is stark. A single loaded-lorry axle can do the structural damage of roughly 40,000 car axles. On a typical road, heavy goods vehicles are a small minority of the traffic but cause the great majority of the structural wear — often cited as HGVs being a few per cent of vehicles yet ~80–90% of the damage. The potholes you swerve round were, to a first approximation, not made by cars. They were made by lorries — and by the water and frost that finish the job (Chapter 3).

The overloading trap

The fourth power has a nasty corollary for anyone tempted to squeeze an extra tonne onto a truck. Because damage goes as L⁴, overloading an axle by just 20% raises its damage by 1.2⁴ ≈ 2.07 — it does more than double the harm. A 25% overload roughly 2.4×-es it. This is why weighbridges, axle-load limits and enforcement matter so much to a highways budget: a handful of illegally overloaded lorries can quietly consume a road's design life years early. It is also why buses and bin lorries, forever running the same routes at high axle loads, chew visible ruts and cracks into bus lanes and turning circles.

Hauler & Tarmac

Hauler: "Forty-four tonnes, coming through! I pay road tax same as everyone."

Tarmac: "Sure — but every one of your axles does what forty thousand cars do to me. The bloke in the hatchback behind you? I barely feel him."

Where the damage actually happens

The fourth-power law is about how many passes it takes to fatigue the pavement — to crack it from the bottom up, as we saw in Chapter 1. But heavy repeated loads do two distinct kinds of harm, and it helps to keep them apart:

  • Fatigue cracking. Each wheel bends the asphalt; each bend stretches the underside a little. Like flexing a paperclip, enough cycles snap it. This is what the ESAL count is really tracking.
  • Rutting. The two parallel troughs worn into each lane's wheelpaths. Part of it is the asphalt itself creeping sideways under hot, heavy, slow loads (plastic flow of the bitumen); part is the layers below being pushed down and densified. Ruts are ugly and dangerous — they hold water, causing spray and aquaplaning.

Speed and temperature matter too. A slow or stationary heavy wheel (a bus at a stop, a lorry in a jam) rests its load on the viscoelastic bitumen for longer, letting it flow — which is why rutting concentrates at junctions, bus stops and climbing lanes, and why a heatwave, softening the tar, is a rutting event. The load is only half the story; the material's mood on the day is the other half (Chapter 4).

Go deeperwhere the exponent comes from

The load equivalency factor between an axle of load L and the standard axle Lstd is modelled as a power law:

LEF = ( L / Lstd )n

The AASHO Road Test data are fitted well by n ≈ 4 for flexible pavements failing by fatigue, and this "fourth power law" became the design convention. But n is not a law of nature: it depends on the pavement's structure, the distress mode (rutting fits a lower power, fatigue a higher one), the layer that governs failure, and even how "failure" is defined. Reported values span roughly 3 to 5, with some thin pavements and specific modes falling outside even that. The headline conclusion is robust to all of this: because the exponent is large, damage is dominated by the heaviest axles, and light vehicles contribute negligibly. When you double a load, you multiply its damage by 2n — between 8 and 32 times. Nothing about a car comes close.
