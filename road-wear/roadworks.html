Chapter 7 — Roadworks

The paving train

A resurfacing is a moving factory. In one continuous pass a convoy grinds off the worn-out surface, sweeps and tacks the bared layer, lays a fresh ribbon of asphalt at around 150 °C, and rolls it dense and smooth — all against a clock that starts the moment the hot mat hits the road, because asphalt can only be compacted while it is hot. Here is the choreography, and the race against cooling that governs it.

The orange cones and the queue hide a genuinely elegant piece of industrial choreography — a train of machines each doing one job, in sequence, on the move.

The convoy, front to back

  1. The planer (milling machine). A large, water-cooled drum studded with hundreds of tungsten-carbide teeth spins against the road and grinds the old surface off to a set depth, throwing the millings up a conveyor into a waiting lorry. It leaves a rough, grooved, clean surface ready to receive new asphalt — and the removed millings are almost all recycled (see below).
  2. Sweep and tack. The milled surface is swept clean and sprayed with a thin tack coat of bitumen emulsion, the glue that bonds the new layer to the old so they act as one and don't slip apart under traffic.
  3. The paver (finisher). Fed hot mix from tipper lorries (or a separate material-transfer vehicle), the paver spreads it through a floating, heated screed that strikes off the mat to a precise thickness and profile, giving it its initial shape and a first light compaction. The screed "floats" on the fresh mat, which is why paving evenness depends on a steady, unbroken supply — every time a paver stops and starts, it risks a bump.
  4. The rollers. One or more steel-drum and rubber-tyred rollers follow closely, compacting the mat to remove air voids and lock the stone skeleton together. This is the step the whole operation is timed around, because it must happen while the asphalt is hot.
Why the rollers are always hurrying

Compaction is not cosmetic — it is the single biggest control on how long the new surface lasts. Fresh asphalt from the paver is full of air voids; the rollers squeeze them out, and every extra 1% of air left in the mat can cut its life by roughly 10%. Under-compacted asphalt is permeable (water gets in — Chapter 3), weak and quick to ravel and rut. But you can only compact it while the bitumen is soft enough to let the stones move — above roughly 85 °C for a typical mix. Below that the mat has effectively set, and rolling it does nothing but polish a surface that is now the wrong density forever. So the rollers race the cooling front, and on a cold day with a thin mat they can lose.

Rolla

"Everybody sees the paver and thinks that's the clever bit. But a badly-rolled road is a short road. I've got maybe ten minutes from when that mat lands before it's too cold to move the stones — so no, I can't slow down, and no, you can't drive on it yet. Give me my window and I'll give you twenty years."

The road that eats itself: recycling

One of the quiet triumphs of modern road engineering is that asphalt is almost endlessly recyclable. The millings ground off an old surface — reclaimed asphalt pavement, or RAP — are still made of perfectly good stone and bitumen; they can be reheated, topped up with a little fresh binder and rejuvenator to undo some of the ageing, and laid again. Asphalt is one of the most-recycled materials by weight in the world, with high proportions of RAP now routine and some processes recycling the existing road in place (cold in-situ recycling), pulverising and re-stabilising it without ever carting it away. It saves the stone, the bitumen (an oil product), the haulage and the landfill all at once.

Why roadworks take so long

The convoy itself moves at a walking pace, and that is rarely the delay. Roadworks are slow because of everything around the paving: the road must be closed and made safe (the single biggest cause of works taking "forever" is traffic management, not laying); each layer must be laid, rolled and allowed to cool before the next; the weather has to cooperate (you cannot lay asphalt onto a wet or frozen surface, or in heavy rain that chills the mat); utility diversions and drainage often have to be done first; and the new surface needs time to cool and cure before traffic returns. A resurfacing you pass in ten seconds at 50 mph took a crew a shift, a tight temperature window, and a lot of standing about waiting for the right conditions — which is also why it is done at night in summer and not at all in a hard winter.

Go deeperthe cooling model behind the window

The interactive models the mat cooling roughly as Newton's law of cooling — an exponential decay from the laydown temperature toward the air temperature:

T(t) = Tair + ( Tlay − Tair ) · e−t/τ

The time constant τ depends chiefly on the mat's thickness (a thick mat holds heat far longer — surface area to volume falls), and on the air temperature, wind and the temperature of the surface it is laid on. Real cooling is more complex — radiation, convection and conduction into the base each dominate at different moments, and practitioners use charts or software (the classic being the "PaveCool" model) rather than a single exponential — but the essential lesson holds: available compaction time collapses for thin mats in cold, windy conditions. That is why minimum laying temperatures, minimum layer thicknesses and material-transfer vehicles (which keep the mix hot and uniform) all exist, and why the humble question "will it be warm enough on Tuesday?" quietly governs the whole national resurfacing programme.
