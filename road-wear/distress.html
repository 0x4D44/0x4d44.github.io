Chapter 5 — The Distress Catalogue

Reading a broken road

Every mark on a failing road is a clue. To the trained eye the pattern of cracks and deformations names the cause — a fatigue failure from below, a thermal crack from the cold, a rut from heavy slow wheels, a brittle mosaic from an aged binder. Learn the vocabulary of distress and a stretch of road tells you its whole history, and what it needs next.

Highway engineers catalogue road distress the way a doctor catalogues symptoms: by type, by severity, and by extent. Get the diagnosis right and the treatment (next chapter) follows.

Use the identifier below to walk through the main distresses of a flexible pavement. Each has a characteristic look, a specific cause traceable to the earlier chapters, and a severity that decides whether it wants a cheap seal or a dig-out.

The main distresses, and what they tell you

Cracking — the biggest family

  • Fatigue ("crocodile" / "alligator") cracking. Interconnected cracks breaking the surface into small many-sided pieces, concentrated in the wheelpaths. The signature of structural fatigue: too many heavy loads flexing the asphalt until it cracks from the bottom up. A structural failure — it usually means the pavement is under-strength or the layers below have weakened (often from water).
  • Longitudinal cracking. Cracks running along the road. In the wheelpath they can be early fatigue; at a lane joint or the paving-mat seam they are a construction weakness opening up; at the edge they are the unsupported edge breaking away.
  • Transverse cracking. Cracks running across the road, roughly evenly spaced. Usually thermal: the surface shrinks as it cools and, restrained by the material around it, tears itself apart — worsened by an aged, brittle binder. Little to do with traffic.
  • Block cracking. A mosaic of interconnected cracks dividing the surface into large rectangular blocks, over the whole area rather than just the wheelpaths. The mark of an aged, shrunken, brittle binder — an ageing failure, not a load one.
  • Reflection cracking. Cracks that copy a pattern from an underlying layer — most often the joints of an old concrete slab showing through a new asphalt overlay laid on top of it, "reflecting" upward as the slab beneath moves.

Deformation — the road changes shape

  • Rutting. Longitudinal depressions in the wheelpaths. Either the asphalt itself creeping sideways under hot, heavy, slow loads (a mix or binder too soft), or the layers below being pushed down (a structural rut). Dangerous because ruts pond water — aquaplaning and spray.
  • Shoving & corrugation. Ripples or a bow-wave of displaced asphalt, where braking, accelerating or turning traffic shoves an unstable, too-soft mix — classic at bus stops, junctions and approaches to traffic lights.
  • Depressions & settlement. Localised low spots where the foundation has consolidated or a trench reinstatement (a utility dig) has settled unevenly.

Surface & disintegration — the road comes apart

  • Ravelling. Progressive loss of aggregate from the surface — it roughens, greys and sheds grit. An ageing/binder failure or poor compaction, and a gateway for water.
  • Bleeding (flushing). A shiny, black film of excess bitumen risen to the surface in the wheelpaths, from too much binder or too little air-void space; slick when hot and wet.
  • Potholes. The bowl-shaped cavity — the end state of neglected fatigue cracking plus water and frost, once traffic has ejected the loosened pieces (Chapter 3).
Type × severity × extent

A single crack is not an emergency; a wheelpath of high-severity crocodile cracking over half a mile is. Engineers therefore score every distress on three axes — type (what it is), severity (low / medium / high, by crack width, rut depth, spalling) and extent (how much of the area it covers) — because the right treatment depends on all three. A low-severity crack wants a cheap seal; the same crack at high severity, spalled and interconnected, wants the surface milled off and replaced.

Putting a number on it: condition indices

To manage thousands of miles of road you need to reduce all this to comparable numbers. Several indices do it. The Pavement Condition Index (PCI) surveys the distresses on a section and combines their type, severity and extent into a single score from 100 (perfect) down to 0 (failed). Ride-quality indices like the IRI (International Roughness Index) instead measure how rough the road feels to a vehicle — the bumps you can hear in your coffee. These scores are what feed the deterioration curves and budgets of Chapter 6: a network is triaged by condition so the limited money goes where it does the most good.

Go deeperhow a PCI score is actually built

The Pavement Condition Index (ASTM D6433) is not a judgement call — it is a procedure. A section is divided into sample units; a surveyor walks each one and records, for every distress present, its type, its severity (low/medium/high, against published thresholds — crack width, rut depth, degree of spalling) and its density: the percentage of the sample unit's area it affects.

Each (type, severity, density) triple is then looked up in a set of curves to give a deduct value — how many points that particular problem costs. Deducts are not simply added: several distresses on one section overlap in what they signify, so a correction is applied to the total to avoid double-counting, and the corrected total is subtracted from 100:

PCI = 100 − CDV

where CDV is the corrected deduct value. The weighting is deliberately not even-handed: a small area of high-severity fatigue cracking costs far more than a large area of low-severity ravelling, because the first says the structure is failing and the second says only the surface is. That is the whole reason to score type and severity separately rather than just measuring how bad a road looks — the index is trying to predict what the road will need, not to describe how it photographs.
