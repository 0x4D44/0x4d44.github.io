Chapter 1 — Anatomy of a Road

A load-spreading sandwich

The black ribbon you drive on is the thinnest slice of a much deeper structure — a stack of engineered layers whose entire job is to take the fierce, concentrated pressure under a tyre and spread it, layer by layer, until it is gentle enough for ordinary soil to bear without rutting. Understand the sandwich and you understand almost everything about how a road fails.

A road does not "hold up" traffic the way a bridge does. It has no beams and no foundations reaching down to rock. It works by distributing load: taking the half-tonne pressing down through one tyre and passing it down through progressively stiffer, cheaper, thicker layers until, at the bottom, it is a soft, wide push that the ground can take.

Get this idea and the rest of the guide falls into place. Every distress in Chapter 5 is the load-spreading system failing somewhere: too much stress at the bottom of the asphalt (fatigue cracking), too much at the top of the soil (rutting), water destroying a layer's stiffness, or the tar that binds it all growing brittle with age.

The five layers, top to bottom

From the tyre down, a typical flexible (asphalt) pavement is built like this. Thicknesses vary hugely with traffic — a farm lane and a motorway share the anatomy but not the scale.

A typical flexible pavement, surface to soil

Subgradethe ground itself Native soil, sometimes improved The foundation. Everything above exists to protect this from overload.

The guiding principle is stiff-over-soft, expensive-over-cheap: strong, costly, thin layers on top; weak, cheap, thick layers below. Each layer only has to be strong enough for the (already spread-out) stress that reaches it, so the dear asphalt can be thin and the cheap stone can do the bulk of the spreading. It is the most economical way yet found to carry a wheel over soft ground.

Professor Camber

"Run the numbers on the figure above and you'll see the trick. A lorry tyre presses at about 800 kilopascals — eight atmospheres, concentrated on a patch the size of a dinner plate. Ordinary clay subgrade starts rutting somewhere around a tenth of that."

"So the entire job of half a metre of stone is to take that eight-atmosphere stamp and hand the ground below a gentle push it can live with. Every layer is just a device for spreading a number until it stops being dangerous."

Where roads crack from

Because the bound layers bend under each wheel, the most-stretched fibre is at the bottom of the asphalt, not the top. Fatigue cracks therefore usually begin unseen at the base of the asphalt and grow upward, only reaching daylight as the "crocodile" cracking of a tired road. By the time you can see fatigue cracking, the damage is old. (Some cracking is top-down too — from ageing and thermal stress — which is why Chapter 5 is careful about telling them apart.)

What is asphalt, really?

The black material is a deliberate mix of two things:

  • Aggregate — crushed rock, gravel and sand, carefully graded from coarse stones down to fine dust so the particles interlock and pack tightly. This is about 95% of the mix by weight and carries the load through stone-on-stone contact.
  • Bitumen — the thick, black, sticky residue left at the bottom of the crude-oil barrel after the petrol, diesel and everything lighter has been distilled off. It is the glue: roughly 5% by weight, it coats every stone and holds the skeleton together. (In American English the whole mix is "asphalt" and the glue is "asphalt cement"; in British English the glue is "bitumen" and the mix is "asphalt". Same stuff.)

Bitumen is what makes a road a road and not just gravel, and it is the source of nearly all the interesting failure. It is viscoelastic: on a hot day, or under a slow, heavy, standing load, it flows like a very thick liquid (roads rut in heatwaves and at bus stops); on a cold day, or under a fast wheel, it behaves like a brittle solid (roads crack in frost). It is also chemically alive — it oxidises and hardens over years, the subject of Chapter 4. Hold that thought: most of this guide is really about what happens to 5% of the road.

Tarmac says

"People think I'm solid rock. I'm mostly stone I'll grant you — but the bit that makes me me is a five-percent skin of oil-barrel tar gluing it together. Warm me up and I'll spread; freeze me and I'll snap. Everything that goes wrong with me starts there."

The other kind: rigid concrete

A minority of heavy-duty roads — some motorways, ports, bus lanes and airfield pavements — are built rigid, from Portland-cement concrete slabs. A rigid pavement doesn't spread load by bending through layers; it is stiff enough to act as a beam, bridging over the ground and carrying the wheel almost regardless of the soil beneath. That makes it long-lived (30–40+ years) but unforgiving: concrete can't flex, so it is cast in slabs with joints to let it expand, and it fails at those joints and in cracked slabs rather than by rutting. It is dearer to lay and much dearer to repair, which is why the world mostly chose flexible asphalt. Concrete's failure modes get their own entries in the Lexicon.

Go deeperthe Boussinesq bulb and why it matters

The interactive uses Boussinesq's 1885 solution for the stress in an elastic half-space under a uniform circular load of pressure p over a circle of radius a. On the centre-line, the vertical stress at depth z is exactly:

σz = p · [ 1 − ( 1 / (1 + (a/z)²) )3/2 ]

The contact radius comes from the load and the tyre pressure — a wheel carrying force Q at inflation pressure p presses over area Q/p, so a = √(Q/πp). Because σz depends on the ratio a/z, a bigger contact patch (heavier wheel) pushes the same fraction of pressure to a greater depth — which is why the surface pressure hardly moves with load while the deep, subgrade-level stress keeps rising. Deep enough that z ≫ a, the expression collapses to σz ≈ 1.5 Q / (π z²): at depth the stress depends only on the total load Q, not on how it is spread — so it grows linearly with wheel load, and no faster. Keep that firmly separate from the fourth-power law: stress is linear in load; damage goes as the fourth power, because fatigue life falls very steeply with the strain each pass imposes. Real pavements are layered, not a uniform half-space, so engineers use multi-layer elastic theory (Burmister) or finite elements; but the single-bulb picture captures the essential economics of why we build in layers at all.
