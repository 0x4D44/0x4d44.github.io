Chapter 8 — The Lexicon

The working vocabulary

Every field builds a private language, and roads are no exception. Here is the vocabulary of wear, the numbers worth remembering, the materials in the sandwich, and the long history of a technology that keeps being reinvented — a reference to send you back into the chapters, and out onto the road, able to read what you see.

Glossary A–Z

Aggregate
The graded stone, gravel and sand that makes up ~95% of an asphalt mix and carries load by stone-on-stone contact. Chosen for hardness, shape (angular interlocks better) and, for grip, a high Polished Stone Value.
Alligator / crocodile cracking fatigue cracking
Interconnected cracks breaking the surface into small polygons in the wheelpaths; the signature of structural fatigue, cracking from the bottom of the asphalt upward.
Asphalt
In British usage, the mix of aggregate and bitumen. In American usage, "asphalt" is both the mix and the binder ("asphalt cement"). Either way, the black stuff roads are surfaced with.
Binder bitumen · asphalt cement
The thick, black, viscoelastic residue of crude-oil refining that glues the aggregate together. About 5% of the mix and the source of most of a road's interesting behaviour.
Bleeding flushing
Excess bitumen risen to the surface as a shiny black film in the wheelpaths; slick when hot and wet.
Block cracking
A mosaic of cracks dividing the whole surface into large rectangular blocks; the mark of an aged, shrunken, brittle binder rather than of traffic.
Boussinesq solution
The 1885 elastic-theory result for how a surface load spreads as stress with depth — the "pressure bulb" of Chapter 1, and the reason we build roads in layers.
Camber crown
The slight arching of a road's cross-section that sheds rainwater to the edges. Simple, cheap and one of the most important defences a road has against water.
Compaction
Rolling the fresh mat to squeeze out air voids and lock the stone together. Must be done while the asphalt is hot; under-compaction is a leading cause of premature failure.
ESAL equivalent single axle load
The accounting unit of traffic damage: every axle converted, via the fourth-power law, into a number of standard 8-tonne axles. A road is designed to survive so many ESALs.
Fatigue
Cracking caused by many repeated flexing loads, each too small to break the material alone — like snapping a paperclip by bending it back and forth. The failure the ESAL count tracks.
Flexible pavement
An asphalt road that carries load by bending and spreading it through its layers to the soil. The great majority of roads; fails by rutting, fatigue cracking and potholes.
Freeze–thaw cycling
Repeated freezing and thawing of water trapped in the pavement. Each freeze expands ~9% and levers cracks wider; the engine of pothole formation in temperate climates.
Frost heave
The lifting of a road as ice lenses grow in a frost-susceptible soil, drawing up water from below. Followed by the spring thaw, when the melting leaves the subgrade briefly very weak.
IRI international roughness index
A standardised measure of how rough a road feels to a vehicle — the ride-quality counterpart to the structural condition score.
Macadam
John McAdam's early-1800s method: a compacted layer of small, clean, angular broken stone, well drained. Sprayed with tar to lay the dust, it became tarmacadam — "tarmac".
Mill & overlay
Grinding off the worn surface and laying fresh asphalt on top. A corrective treatment that restores ride, grip and some strength once cheaper preventive options have run out.
PCI pavement condition index
A survey-based score from 100 (perfect) to 0 (failed) combining distress type, severity and extent. Feeds the deterioration curves and budgets of pavement management.
Polished Stone Value PSV
A measure of how well an aggregate resists polishing under traffic — i.e. how long it keeps its skid resistance. High-PSV stone is specified for busy and high-risk sites.
Pothole
A bowl-shaped cavity: the end state of neglected fatigue cracking once water, frost and traffic have prised and ejected the loosened surface.
Pumping
The ejection of water and fines from under a slab or through a crack as a wheel passes, acting as a piston. Erodes the layers and undermines concrete slabs.
Ravelling
Progressive loss of aggregate from the surface — it roughens, greys and sheds grit. A binder ageing or compaction failure, and a doorway for water.
Reflection cracking
Cracks that copy a pattern — usually old concrete-slab joints — from a layer beneath, working their way up through a new overlay laid on top.
Rigid pavement
A concrete road that carries load as a stiff beam, bridging over the ground. Long-lived but dear to build and repair; fails at joints and by cracked slabs.
Rutting
Longitudinal troughs in the wheelpaths, from the asphalt creeping sideways under hot heavy slow loads, or from deformation of the layers below. Ponds water; a hazard.
Skid resistance
The grip between tyre and surface, especially wet. Falls as traffic polishes the stone; restored by surface dressing or resurfacing, sometimes before a road is structurally worn out.
Stripping
Water breaking the bond between bitumen and aggregate, stripping the tar off the stone so particles pluck out. A moisture-damage failure that feeds ravelling and potholes.
Subgrade
The natural (or improved) soil beneath the built layers — the foundation. Everything above it exists to keep the stress reaching it low enough that it doesn't rut.
Superpave PG grades
A modern US system for designing asphalt mixes and grading binders by the performance temperature range they must survive (e.g. PG 64-22). Ties the binder to the local climate.
Surface dressing chip seal
Spraying binder and rolling in stone chippings to reseal a surface and restore grip, without adding strength. The workhorse of cost-effective preventive maintenance.
Tack coat
A thin spray of bitumen emulsion between layers, gluing a new layer to the old so they act as one and don't slip.
Viscoelastic
Behaving partly like an elastic solid and partly like a viscous liquid, depending on temperature and how fast it is loaded. Bitumen's defining property: it ruts in slow summer heat and cracks in fast winter cold.

A timeline of the road

Road-building, reinvented across the ages
WhenMilestone
c. 4000 BCEarliest known paved and timber "corduroy" roads and stone-paved streets.
c. 300 BC+The Roman viae — deep, layered, cambered stone roads, superbly drained; some still traceable.
1750s–1820sTrésaguet (France) and Telford (Britain) refine layered stone construction with engineered foundations and drainage.
c. 1820John Loudon McAdam: shallow, well-drained layers of small clean angular stone — the "macadam" road, cheap and effective.
1901–1902Tar sprayed onto macadam to bind dust — tarmacadam ("tarmac"), patented by Edgar Hooley.
Early 1900sHot-mix asphalt: mixing bitumen and aggregate before laying, industrialised through the century.
1958–1960The AASHO Road Test deliberately loads roads to destruction — yields the fourth-power damage law and the ESAL.
1980s–90sPavement Management Systems and the US SHRP programme; Superpave binder grading ties mixes to climate.
2000s–nowHigh-RAP recycling, warm-mix asphalt (lower laying temperatures), in-situ recycling, self-healing and rejuvenator research.

Tarmac signs off

"So that's me: a five-percent skin of ageing tar over a sandwich of stone, bent by every lorry, soaked and frozen and baked, slowly heading back to gravel. Look after me early and cheaply and I'll carry you for decades. Ignore me and I'll have your front wheel by spring. Mind how you go."
