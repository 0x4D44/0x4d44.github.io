Chapter 3 — Water & Frost

How a pothole is born

Traffic cracks a road; water kills it. A dry, well-drained pavement can outlast its design life. Let water into the structure and everything accelerates — the layers soften, the tar lets go of the stone, and where it freezes, the water levers the surface apart from inside a hairline crack. The pothole that swallows your wheel every spring is the signature of that partnership between weight, water and frost.

Ask a highways engineer for the three biggest threats to a road and the answer is often "drainage, drainage and drainage."

Everything else — heavy axles, ageing tar, hot summers — is made dramatically worse by the presence of water, and dramatically better by its absence. Here is the chain of destruction, and it starts with a crack that traffic and time have already made.

Step one: water gets in

A sound asphalt surface is nearly waterproof. But once traffic and ageing have opened even a hairline crack, rain finds it, and capillary action and the pressure of passing tyres drive the water down into the pavement. There it does three separate kinds of harm before frost is even involved:

  • It weakens the unbound layers. The granular sub-base and the soil subgrade lose much of their strength when saturated — think how a gravel path turns to a rutted mess in the wet. A load that the dry structure spread safely now overloads a softened layer.
  • Stripping. Water attacks the bond between the bitumen and the stone, literally stripping the tar off the aggregate. Once a stone is no longer glued in, it is plucked out by the next tyre — the surface starts shedding grains (ravelling) and losing its integrity from within.
  • Pumping. A wheel passing over a water-filled crack acts like a piston, driving pressurised water down and then sucking it back up — eroding the layers, washing out fines, and (on concrete roads) undermining the slabs. You can often see the grey slurry it ejects streaking a failing joint.

Step two: the frost lever

Now add a freezing night. Water has a rare and, for roads, catastrophic property: it expands as it freezes, by about 9% in volume. Trapped in a crack with nowhere to go, freezing water pushes outward with enormous force — far more than enough to overcome asphalt's feeble tensile strength. Each freeze widens the crack and breaks the bond a little more; each thaw lets the meltwater trickle deeper into the freshly opened gap. Repeat this freeze–thaw cycling a few dozen times over a winter and a hairline crack becomes a network of loosened, debonded fragments of surface.

The 9% that breaks roads

When water freezes, its molecules lock into an open crystal lattice that takes up more room than the liquid — so ice is less dense than water (which is why it floats), and a given volume of water becomes about 9% larger as ice. Confined in a crack, that expansion generates pressures of tens of megapascals, comfortably exceeding the roughly 1–3 MPa tensile strength of cold asphalt. The road cannot win: either it gives way, or the ice finds room. It gives way. Countries with many crossings of 0 °C each winter — not the coldest countries, but the ones that hover around freezing — suffer the worst pothole seasons for exactly this reason.

Step three: traffic ejects the pieces

Frost loosens the surface, but a pothole is not a hole until something removes the loosened material — and that something is traffic. A wheel striking the debonded patch punches down on the fragments; the trapped water, unable to compress, blasts sideways and pops the pieces out; and once a shallow depression exists it holds a puddle, so the next wheel delivers a pressurised water hammer to its floor and walls. The hole now grows itself: deeper, wider, faster, edges crumbling with every axle. This is why potholes appear so suddenly in late winter — the frost did its quiet work in December and January, and the first busy, wet, thawing week reaps the whole crop at once.

Drip & Percy

Drip: "All I did was trickle into a little crack and wait for a cold night. I expand nine per cent, you know. Nine!"

Percy the Pothole: "And I take it from there. A few lorries, a puddle to sit in, and I'll have your front wheel by March. Lovely."

The cure is drainage

Because water is the accelerant behind almost every failure in this chapter, the single most cost-effective thing a road can have is good drainage: a cambered (crowned) surface that sheds rain to the sides, clear gullies and channels, permeable sub-bases that let trapped water escape, and edges that don't pond. A great deal of expensive road failure is really a blocked drain or a missing camber. Keep the water out and moving, and the fourth-power loads of Chapter 2 take far longer to do their worst.

Go deeperfrost heave and the spring thaw

Beyond cracking the surface, frost attacks the structure in two deeper ways. In frost-susceptible (silty) soils, freezing draws up liquid water from below to feed growing ice lenses, which swell the ground and lift the road unevenly — "frost heave." Then, during the spring thaw, all that ice melts at once and the trapped meltwater cannot drain through the still-frozen soil beneath, leaving the subgrade briefly saturated and extraordinarily weak — its stiffness can fall by half or more. This is why some regions impose spring load restrictions, banning heavy lorries for a few weeks each thaw: the roads are temporarily too soft to carry them, and a fourth-power axle on a thaw-weakened subgrade does years of damage in days. Water, weight and temperature are not three separate problems — they are one problem with three variables.
