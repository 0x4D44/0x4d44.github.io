Chapter 4

Temperament & Tuning

Now the twist in the tale. In Chapter 1 the harmonic series handed us the pure, consonant intervals as simple frequency ratios — the octave 2:1, the fifth 3:2. You would think a keyboard should just be tuned to those. It can't be. The pure intervals, stacked up, do not fit into the octave — not by a little, but by an amount you can hear. Every fixed-pitch instrument in Western music is a negotiated settlement of this problem, and the piano's settlement is the most ruthless of all.

Two ways up the same hill that should meet at the top — and miss.

Start on a note and go up by pure fifths (ratio 3:2), twelve times: C → G → D → A… all the way around through every sharp and back to a C, seven octaves higher. Now start on the same note and go up by pure octaves (ratio 2:1), seven times, to reach — the same C. Two routes to the same note. They should arrive at the identical frequency. They do not. The twelve-fifths route lands about a quarter of a semitone sharp of the seven-octaves route. That stubborn little gap is the Pythagorean comma, and it is the crack in the universe that all tuning has to paper over.

The comma, in numbers

Twelve pure fifths multiply to (3/2)12 = 129.746…, while seven octaves give 27 = 128. Their ratio is

(3/2)1227 = 531 441524 288 = 1.01364… ≈ 23.46 CENTS

You cannot wish this away; it is arithmetic. Since 2 and 3 are different primes, no number of 3:2s will ever exactly equal a number of 2:1s. Something, somewhere, has to give.

§ 4.1Watch the circle refuse to close

Here is the comma made visible. Stack pure fifths one at a time and follow where each new note lands on the pitch circle (one full turn = one octave). With pure fifths the twelfth note overshoots the starting point — the "circle" of fifths is really a spiral. Switch to equal fifths, each shaved a hair flat, and it closes exactly.

§ 4.2Three ways to spend the comma

Every tuning system is a decision about where to hide that 23.5-cent error. You have to put it somewhere; the question is which intervals you are willing to spoil.

How the great temperaments spend the comma
SystemKeeps pureRuinsVerdict
Just intonationFifths and thirds — in one key Every other key; a "wolf" fifth appears Heavenly in C, unusable in F♯. Fine for a fixed choir, hopeless for a keyboard that modulates.
MeantoneThe major thirds (pure 5:4) One fifth becomes a howling "wolf" Ruled ~1500–1700. Glorious thirds, but a few keys are off-limits.
Well temperamentNothing pure, but all keys usable Each key coloured differently Bach's world (Das Wohltemperirte Clavier, 1722): every key playable, each with its own character.
Equal temperamentOnly the octave Every fifth (−2¢) and third (+14¢) — but the same in all keys The modern default. No key better or worse than any other; nothing pure but the octave.

Modern pianos use the last row: twelve-tone equal temperament. The octave is split into twelve identical steps, each a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of two. Every fifth is about 2 cents flat of pure, every major third a noticeable 14 cents sharp — but every key is exactly as slightly-wrong as every other, so you can play in all 24 keys and modulate freely between them. It is a democracy of evenly-shared error — each interval mistuned by a fixed amount, the same in every key. The cost is that nothing except the octave is truly in tune; the gain is everything Chopin and jazz ever did with distant keys.

Cents, and the twelfth root of twothe measuring stick of tuning

Because pitch is multiplicative, we measure intervals in cents: 1200 to the octave, 100 to an equal-tempered semitone. The cents between two frequencies is

cents = 1200 · log2(f2 / f1)

In equal temperament every semitone is the same ratio 21/12 ≈ 1.05946, so the fifth (7 semitones) is 27/12 ≈ 1.4983 — against the pure 1.5000, which is 701.96¢. The equal fifth is 700¢: flat by 1.96¢. Twelve of those shavings sum to 12 × 1.96 = 23.5¢ — the Pythagorean comma, now spread invisibly around the whole keyboard.

§ 4.3Hear the difference — beats

How can a tuner, by ear, set a fifth exactly 2 cents flat? By listening for beats. When two tones are almost in tune, they slip in and out of step, and the loudness pulses — a slow wah… wah… wah. The pulse rate equals the frequency difference between the tones (or between the harmonics that nearly coincide). A pure interval has no beats; a tempered one beats at a steady, countable rate. This is the craft under the science. An aural tuner sets one octave in the middle of the keyboard by counting beats — so many per second for a tempered fifth, a bit more for a third — then tunes outward from it, widening the octaves as Chapter 3's inharmonicity demands. What looks like a mystical "good ear" is, at bottom, arithmetic done by listening: spreading one 23.5-cent comma so evenly that no single interval ever offends. The well-tempered machine is well-tempered precisely because it is, everywhere, a little out of tune.

We have the string, the throw, the tone and the tuning. What remains is the thing that holds it all — the twenty-ton frame and the century of design that shaped it. That is the anatomy.
