The Well-Tempered Machine

A piano is the most complicated machine most people keep in their living room: some ten thousand moving parts whose only job is to throw a felt-covered hammer at a steel string and then instantly get out of its way. This guide takes one apart — the physics of the string, the mechanism of the throw, the wood that turns a faint buzz into a concert, and the two-thousand-year argument about tuning that the keyboard finally had to settle. Play it as you read.

There are three ways to get a taut string to sing, and pianos are the third — the strangest and the youngest of them.

You can pluck it, as a harpsichord does with a quill, or a guitar with a fingertip: a clean, bright attack, but always the same loudness however hard you press the key. You can bow it, as a violin does, feeding energy in continuously so the note sustains as long as you like. Or you can strike it with a hammer — and here is the trick that took until about the year 1700 to solve. A hammer that stays pressed against the string, as a clavichord's little brass tangent does, deadens the very note it just made. To get a loud, ringing tone you must hit the string and let the hammer bounce free in the same thousandth of a second, so the string is left to vibrate on its own.

Building a mechanism that does that — reliably, 88 times over, ten notes a second under a virtuoso's hands, hard when you play hard and soft when you play soft — is the whole engineering story of the piano. Its inventor was the Florentine harpsichord-keeper Bartolomeo Cristofori; his instrument was written up in 1711 as a gravicembalo col piano e forte, a harpsichord with soft and loud. We kept the last two words.

How this guide is builtplain story, optional maths

The main text assumes no physics and no music theory — every symbol and term is explained the first time it appears. Where the honest mathematics earns its place (the frequency of a string, the cents of a comma, the curve of a tuning), it lives in a fold-away panel like this one; open it or skip it, the story reads either way.

And everything is playable. This is a static web page — vanilla JavaScript, canvas and SVG, and the Web Audio synthesiser built into your browser, with no libraries and no build step — so the diagrams are live instruments, not pictures of instruments. Pull a slider and the sound changes with it.

What actually is a piano?

Here is the shortest honest definition. A piano is a keyboard that converts the speed of a finger into the speed of a hammer, throws that hammer at a tuned string, lets it fall away so the string can ring, and pours the result into a large thin panel of wood that couples the string's tiny vibration to the air. Everything else — the black lacquer, the three pedals, the gold-framed harp of strings — is in service of those four steps.

Strip the cabinet away and you are left with a surprisingly small set of physical ideas, and this guide is organised around them:

  • A pitch is a frequency, and a string's frequency is fixed by just three things — how long it is, how tight it is, and how heavy it is (Chapter 1).
  • Loudness is hammer speed, and delivering a controllable, free-flying blow is a genuinely hard mechanical problem — the one Cristofori solved and Sébastien Érard perfected (Chapter 2).
  • Timbre is which overtones survive — set by the softness of the felt, where along the string it strikes, and the resonance of the board (Chapter 3).
  • Tuning is a compromise forced by arithmetic: the pure intervals simply do not fit into an octave, so the piano spreads the error evenly across all twelve keys (Chapter 4).
The one number to remember

Pitch doubles every octave, and the octave is split into twelve equal steps, so each semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of two:

f(n) = 440 × 2(n − 69)/12 Hz

Here n is the key's MIDI number (the A above middle C is 69, and sounds at the reference 440 Hz). That single formula tunes the entire keyboard, from the bottom A at 27.5 Hz to the top C at about 4,186 Hz — a range of a little over seven octaves. It is also, as Chapter 4 shows, a two-thousand-year-old surrender.

A machine of ten thousand parts

The numbers are worth pausing on. A modern concert grand contains on the order of ten thousand individual parts, most of them in the 88 near-identical actions — a little tower of levers, felts, springs and pivots repeated once per key. It carries about 230 strings (most notes have three, the tenor two, the deepest bass one apiece), and their combined pull on the frame is on the order of twenty tons — as much as thirty on a full concert grand — which is why, since the 1820s, that frame has been a single casting of iron. The soundboard behind them is a panel of quarter-sawn spruce only about 8 millimetres thick and nearly two metres across, deliberately built with a slight crown so it can push against the air.

None of it is arbitrary. Every measurement — the length of each string, the mass of its winding, the exact point the hammer strikes, the thickness of the board — is the settled answer to a physical question. Over the next chapters we ask those questions one at a time, and let you turn the answers up and down yourself.

A very short history

Cristofori built the first pianos in Florence around 1700; three of his instruments survive. For a century it was a delicate salon curiosity, its light wooden frame able to hold only slack strings and a thin tone — this is the fortepiano that Haydn, Mozart and the young Beethoven wrote for. The nineteenth century then rebuilt it into a machine: Érard's double-escapement action (1821) let a note repeat before the key had fully risen; the American full cast-iron plate (Alpheus Babcock's 1825 patent, perfected by Steinway) let the strings be pulled to enormous tension; and cross-stringing fanned the long bass strings over the treble for a richer, more even voice. By about 1880 the instrument had reached essentially the form it keeps today.

How to read this guide

Start here and go in order — each chapter leans gently on the one before — or jump to whatever pulled you in. Play every interactive; the sliders explain what sentences cannot. And a promise about honesty: writing about pianos tends to drift into either mystique ("the soul of the wood") or dry cataloguing of model numbers. This guide keeps to the physics, names the real people and dates, and flags clearly the few places where taste, not acoustics, has the final say.
