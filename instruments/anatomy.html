Chapter 5

Anatomy & Construction

Everything so far — the string, the throw, the tone, the tuning — has to be held together against a ferocious load. Two hundred-odd strings pulled tight add up to something like the weight of a dozen cars, all trying to fold the instrument in half. The story of how the piano is built is largely the story of holding back that force without choking the sound it is there to make.

A concert grand is a controlled explosion, frozen. Cut a string and you would hear the crack across the room.

Each string on a grand is tuned to a tension of roughly 70 to 90 kilograms — the pull of a hanging adult. There are more than two hundred of them. Add it all up and the frame carries on the order of twenty tons of steadily applied force — as much as thirty on a full nine-foot concert grand — forever. The single most important structural fact about the modern piano is the thing invented to take that load: a one-piece cast-iron plate (the "frame" or "harp"), poured to shape and bolted into a heavy wooden case. Before it existed, string tension had to stay low, and the tone stayed thin. String the frame up yourself and watch the load climb:

Why a piano is so heavy

A concert grand weighs around 480 kg (half a tonne), and most of that is structure fighting the strings: the cast-iron plate alone is well over a hundred kilograms. The rest of the case — the bent laminated rim of hard rock maple, the heavy wooden beams under the soundboard, the pinblock of cross-laminated hardwood that grips the tuning pins — all exists to keep the geometry from creeping under that permanent twenty-ton pull. A piano that goes out of tune quickly is usually one whose structure is quietly losing that fight.

§ 5.1The harp, laid bare

Lift the lid of a grand and look straight down and you see the whole system at once. Here is that plan view, labelled — trace the force from the front, where the tuning pins grip the strings, to the back, where the strings hitch onto the iron plate.

The parts, front to back:

  • Tuning pins & pinblock. At the keyboard end, each string is wound round a steel tuning pin driven into a block of cross-laminated hardwood. Turning the pin a hair changes the tension — and hence, by Chapter 1, the pitch. The block's grip is what holds the tuning.
  • The cast-iron plate. The gold-painted skeleton taking the full string load; struts run down its length to resist the pull.
  • The strings & the scale. Graduated from short, thin, plain-steel trebles to long, copper-wound bass — Mersenne's laws made into a design (Chapter 1).
  • The bridges. Wooden ridges — a long treble bridge and a separate bass bridge — glued to the soundboard, over which the strings press. They pass the vibration into the board.
  • The soundboard. The great spruce panel beneath the strings, ribbed and slightly crowned, that actually makes the sound (Chapter 3).
  • Hitch pins. At the tail, the far ends of the strings loop onto pins anchored in the plate.

§ 5.2Why exactly 88 keys?

The modern keyboard runs from A0 at the bottom to C8 at the top — 88 keys, a little over seven octaves. It is a surprisingly recent standard. Cristofori's pianos had about four octaves; Mozart wrote for five; by Beethoven's late years makers had pushed to six and beyond as the iron frame allowed ever-longer, higher-tension strings. Around 1880 Steinway and others settled on 88, and it stuck — for a reason rooted in Chapter 1 and Chapter 3.

The two ends of hearing

The bottom A0 vibrates at 27.5 Hz — down near the floor of clearly-pitched hearing, where a note is felt as much as heard. Below it, extra keys would give more rumble than melody. The top C8 sits at about 4,186 Hz; higher still, and inharmonicity (Chapter 3) and our own fading pitch sense make the notes hard to tune and hard to tell apart. Eighty-eight keys is, roughly, as wide as the ear finds musically useful. A few makers go further — the Bösendorfer Imperial adds extra bass keys down to C0 for a full 97 — but those low strings are there as much to resonate with the notes above as to be played.

§ 5.3The three pedals

The feet get a say too. On a modern grand, from right to left:

  • Damper (sustain) pedal — the important one. It lifts all the dampers at once, so every string is free to ring and, crucially, to resonate sympathetically with the notes you play. It is what makes the piano bloom.
  • Sostenuto pedal (middle, a later addition) — a clever selective sustain: it holds up only the dampers of the notes already down when you press it, letting you sustain a chord while playing staccato above it.
  • Una corda (soft) pedal — shifts the action sideways so the hammers strike fewer strings and with softer felt, changing the colour as much as the volume (Chapter 3).

That is the instrument, whole: string, action, tone, tuning, and the frame that survives them all. It did not arrive fully formed — it was argued into shape over three centuries, by makers competing to be louder, more even and more reliable. That argument is the last chapter: the family.
