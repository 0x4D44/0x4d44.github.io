Chapter 7

The Lexicon

A reference to keep. The vocabulary of the instrument, defined in one line each; the constants worth remembering; the frequency of every one of the 88 keys, computed live from the equal-tempered formula of Chapter 1; and a timeline of the whole three-hundred-year argument.

A–ZThe working vocabulary

Action
The whole mechanism, per key, that turns a finger's push into a hammer's free blow — key, wippen, jack, repetition lever, hammer, back-check and damper. See Chapter 2.
Back-check
A felted block that catches the hammer as it rebounds off the string, stopping it bouncing back for an unwanted second strike.
Bridge
A wooden ridge glued to the soundboard, over which the strings press; it carries their vibration into the board. A grand has a long treble bridge and a separate bass bridge.
Cents
A logarithmic unit of pitch interval: 1200 to the octave, 100 to an equal-tempered semitone. cents = 1200·log₂(f₂/f₁). The measuring stick of tuning.
Damper
A felt block resting on a string to silence it; lifted by its key (or by the sustain pedal) so the string can ring, and dropped again on release.
Equal temperament
The modern tuning: the octave split into twelve identical ratios of 2^(1/12). Only the octave is pure; every key is equally, slightly out of tune. Chapter 4.
Escapement let-off
The instant the jack is tripped out from under the hammer, leaving it to fly the last few millimetres to the string under its own momentum. Cristofori's key invention.
Fortepiano
The early piano (c.1700–1830): light wooden frame, low string tension, a clear but slender tone. The instrument of Mozart and the young Beethoven.
Fundamental
The lowest, strongest mode of a vibrating string — the pitch you name. Everything above it is a harmonic. Chapter 1.
Hammer
A wooden core wrapped in graded wool felt, thrown at the string by the action. Its softness and strike point set the tone; a technician adjusts (voices) the felt. Chapter 3.
Harmonic series
The ladder of overtones at 1×, 2×, 3×… the fundamental frequency, present in every string tone. The source of both timbre and the intervals of harmony. Chapter 1.
Hertz Hz
Cycles per second — the unit of frequency, and therefore of pitch. A4 = 440 Hz.
Inharmonicity
The slight sharpening of a real string's upper partials caused by its stiffness: the n-th partial sits at n·f·√(1+Bn²). It forces the stretched tuning of Chapter 3.
Jack hopper
The spring-loaded lever on the wippen that drives the hammer up and then escapes from under it at let-off.
Just intonation
Tuning by pure whole-number frequency ratios (3:2, 5:4…). Beautiful in one key, unusable across all — the problem equal temperament solves.
MIDI number
An integer naming each semitone; A4 = 69. Frequency is 440·2^((n−69)/12). Used throughout this guide.
Node
A point on a vibrating string that stays still. Striking the string at a mode's node fails to excite that mode — the basis of the strike-point trick. Chapter 3.
Overstringing cross-stringing
Fanning the long bass strings diagonally over the treble, so they can be longer and load the soundboard centrally. Steinway, 1859.
Pinblock wrest plank
The block of cross-laminated hardwood that grips the tuning pins. Its hold is what keeps a piano in tune.
Plate frame, harp
The one-piece cast-iron casting that carries the ~20-ton string load. The enabling invention of the modern piano. Chapter 5.
Pythagorean comma
The gap by which twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves: 531441/524288 ≈ 23.46¢. The reason tuning is a compromise. Chapter 4.
Railsback curve
The characteristic stretch of a well-tuned piano — sharp at the top, flat at the bottom — measured by O. Railsback in 1938. A response to inharmonicity, not an error.
Repetition lever
The sprung lever of Érard's 1821 double-escapement action that catches the falling hammer part-way, letting a note repeat before the key has fully risen.
Soundboard
The large, thin, crowned spruce panel beneath the strings that actually radiates the sound. Chapter 3.
Sostenuto
The middle pedal on a grand: a selective sustain that holds only the notes already down when it is pressed.
Temperament
Any systematic adjustment of pure intervals to make a fixed keyboard usable in many keys. Chapter 4.
Timbre tone colour
What distinguishes two instruments playing the same pitch — the mix and decay of their harmonics.
Una corda
The soft pedal: on a grand it shifts the action so hammers strike fewer strings and softer felt, changing colour as much as volume.
Unison choir
The two or three strings tuned to one note and struck together; their slight mistuning gives the piano its shimmer and two-stage decay.
Wippen
The rocking sub-assembly the key's capstan lifts, carrying the jack and repetition lever.
Wolf
A badly out-of-tune interval (howling like a wolf) that appears in unequal temperaments when the comma is dumped in one place.

§A timeline

Three centuries of the well-tempered machine
YearEvent
c. 1700Bartolomeo Cristofori builds the first pianos in Florence — a harpsichord "with soft and loud," the escapement action born.
1722J. S. Bach completes Book I of Das Wohltemperirte Clavier, music for a keyboard tuned so every key is playable.
1730sGottfried Silbermann builds the piano in Germany; his later instruments reportedly win Bach's approval.
c. 1780The light Viennese fortepiano (Stein, Walter) reaches its classic five-octave form — Mozart's instrument.
1821Sébastien Érard patents the double-escapement repetition action, still used in every grand.
1825Alpheus Babcock patents a one-piece cast-iron frame (for square pianos) in Boston.
1853Steinway & Sons founded in New York.
1859Steinway patents the overstrung scale with a full iron plate in a grand — the modern grand's decisive form.
c. 1880The 88-key compass (A0–C8) settles as the standard.
1900The pneumatic player piano arrives; Yamaha begins building pianos in Japan.
1938Railsback measures the stretched tuning of real pianos; John Cage begins writing for the prepared piano.
1955A440 fixed as the international pitch standard (ISO 16, reaffirmed 1975).
1980sThe weighted-key digital piano arrives; sampled and, later, physically-modelled tone follow.
