Chapter 2

The Action

Here is the invention. Everything else about the piano — the strings, the wood, the frame — the harpsichord already had in some form. What Cristofori added, and what took a further century to perfect, was a chain of little levers that converts the gentle push of a finger into the fast, free blow of a hammer, and then snatches that hammer back before it can spoil the note. It is called the action, and it is the reason the instrument can play both soft and loud.

The problem sounds trivial and is not: hit a string with a hammer, but don't leave the hammer touching it.

A string only rings if it is free to vibrate. Rest anything against it — a finger, a felt, the very hammer that struck it — and the sound dies at once. So the hammer must strike and rebound in the same instant, flying back off the string under its own momentum. But it also has to be driven by the key right up to the last moment, or you would have no control over how hard it hits. Driven, then suddenly free, then caught again before it bounces back up for a second, unwanted blow. The device that manages this hand-off is the escapement, and it is the beating heart of every piano ever built.

§ 2.1Drive it yourself

Below is a schematic grand action — one key's worth — seen from the side, with the string at the top and the player's finger at the left. Drag the key slider to press the key slowly and watch the chain of levers move: the key tips on its balance pin, its tail lifts the wippen, the wippen's jack pushes the hammer up toward the string, and the damper lifts off so the string will be free to sound. Keep pressing, and at about four-fifths of the way down something decisive happens.

The moment at four-fifths depth is let-off (or escapement). Until then the jack sits under a rounded knuckle on the hammer shank and drives it upward. At let-off, the jack's toe meets a fixed regulating button and is tipped out from under the knuckle — so for the final few millimetres the hammer coasts, unpowered, onto the string, and rebounds into a padded back-check that stops it bouncing. Press slowly and you prove the point the animation makes: with the jack escaped, the hammer simply parks below the string. It has to be thrown.

Mallet

"For the first four-fifths of the trip I'm a passenger — the jack carries me. Then the jack ducks out and I'm on my own for the last three millimetres. I hit the string, bounce straight off, and land in the back-check. If I didn't bounce off, there'd be no note at all — I'd be a mute sitting on the wire."

§ 2.2Escapement, and then escapement twice

Cristofori's genius was the first escapement: a hopper that drove the hammer and then let it go. But his action, and the simpler single-escapement actions that followed, had a limitation that mattered enormously to players. After a note, the jack could only slide back under the knuckle once the key had risen almost all the way back to rest. Until it did, you could not play the note again. On a fast trill or a repeated chord, that meant letting the key come fully up between every strike — a real ceiling on how fast and how quietly you could repeat a note.

In 1821 the Parisian maker Sébastien Érard patented the fix that every grand piano still uses: the double escapement, or repetition action. He added a sprung repetition lever that catches the falling hammer and holds it partway up, so the jack can slip back underneath while the key is still half-depressed. The note can be re-struck from halfway, again and again, faster and softer than before.

Why grands feel different

This is the concrete reason a good grand "repeats" so effortlessly and an upright feels stiffer for fast passages. Uprights use a simpler action (the hammers travel horizontally and are returned partly by springs and gravity across their travel); most cannot reset from a half-lifted key the way Érard's repetition action can. It is not snobbery — it is a different linkage doing a genuinely harder job. Concert repertoire from Liszt onward quietly assumes the double escapement exists.

The parts, nameda field glossary of one key

Following the force from finger to string, a modern grand action has, per key, roughly: the key itself (a first-class lever on a balance pin); the capstan screw on its tail; the wippen, the rocking sub-assembly the capstan lifts; the jack (or hopper), the spring-loaded lever that drives the hammer and then escapes; the repetition lever of Érard's design; the knuckle (a leather-covered roller under the hammer shank the jack pushes on); the hammer — shank, and a wooden core wrapped in graded wool felt; the back-check that catches the rebounding hammer; the let-off button and drop screw that a technician adjusts to set where escapement happens; and the damper with its own lever, lifted by the key so the string can ring and dropped again on release. Multiply by 88. A regulator's job is to set the clearances of all of them, to thousandths of an inch, so every key feels and speaks the same.

§ 2.3Three ways to make a string speak

It is worth seeing the piano's method against its two ancestors, because the difference in mechanism is exactly the difference you hear. All three put a keyboard in front of a set of strings; what the key does at the far end is entirely different.

a · harpsichord
Plucked. A quill on a jack plucks the string as the key goes down. Bright and clear — but the pluck is the same strength however you play, so there is no loud and soft from the fingers.
b · clavichord
Struck & held. A brass tangent strikes the string and stays pressed to it, setting the pitch and staying in contact. Touch-sensitive and even bendable in pitch — but so quiet it barely carries across a room.
c · piano
Struck & freed. A hammer is thrown at the string and rebounds instantly. Loudness follows hammer speed, so the fingers control dynamics — and the note rings freely. The best of both, at the cost of a far cleverer mechanism.

That third column is the whole point of the escapement. It buys touch-sensitivity (like the clavichord) and a big free-ringing tone (better than the harpsichord) in one instrument, by throwing the hammer rather than holding it. The price is complexity: those ten thousand parts. What the hammer then does to the string when it lands — soft or hard, and where it lands — decides the actual sound. That is tone.
