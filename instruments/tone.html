Chapter 3

Tone & the Soundboard

Two pianos can play the identical note — the same frequency, from Chapter 1 — and sound completely different. The difference is timbre: which of the note's overtones are loud, which are missing, and how they fade. A piano's makers control that recipe with three quiet decisions — how soft the hammer is, exactly where it hits the string, and what the board behind it does with the vibration. This chapter is those three decisions.

A bare string, plucked in mid-air, is almost silent. Nearly everything you hear is the wood.

A vibrating string is a poor loudspeaker: it is thin, so it slices through the air instead of pushing it. Left to itself it would ring for a long time but barely be audible. The piano's job is to steal that energy and hand it to something broad enough to move real air — the soundboard, a panel of quarter-sawn spruce only about 8 millimetres thick and nearly two metres wide. The strings press down on a wooden bridge glued to that board; every time they vibrate, they rock the bridge, and the bridge flexes the whole board, which shoves the air and becomes the sound. The board is why a piano is loud, and its size and stiffness colour everything you hear. Trade a little of that loudness away — the string gives up its energy faster — and you get the piano's signature: a bright attack that decays, because unlike a bowed or blown instrument, nothing keeps feeding it.

§ 3.1Where the hammer lands

Recall from Chapter 1 that a string vibrates in many modes at once — whole, halves, thirds — and that a mode has still points, nodes, where the string doesn't move. Here is a lovely consequence: if you strike the string exactly at a point that is a node for some harmonic, you cannot set that harmonic going at all. Strike at the midpoint and the 2nd, 4th, 6th… harmonics (all of which have a node in the middle) vanish, leaving a hollow, clarinet-ish tone of odd harmonics only. Strike near one end and you get everything, harsh and bright.

Piano makers exploit this deliberately. The hammer is set to strike at roughly one-seventh to one-ninth of the speaking length from the end — the exact fraction shifting from note to note — which drops a node of the 7th harmonic (and its multiples, the 14th and 21st) right under the hammer and mutes it. Because the felt has real width, the cancellation is strong rather than total. That matters because the 7th harmonic is badly out of tune with the scale (a flat, sour minor seventh — you saw it flagged red in Chapter 1), and the very high partials just sound like clang. Killing them is how the piano avoids a metallic edge. Move the hammer yourself and watch the spectrum, and the tone, change:

"I'm not a hard mallet — I'm graded wool felt over a wooden core, deliberately soft. Hit gently and I stay soft and mellow; hit hard and I compress on impact and turn bright. That's why a piano doesn't just get louder as you dig in — it changes colour. A technician can 'voice' me sharper with a needle, or softer with a little ironing."

The mode that dies at the nodewhy 1/n mutes harmonic n

Mode n of a string has the shape sin(nπx/L), with nodes wherever that is zero — at x = L/n, 2L/n, …. Striking the string at position a·L excites each mode in proportion to how much that mode is moving there, roughly |sin(nπa)| (times a factor for the hammer's softness and width). Set a = 1/7 and the 7th mode's factor is sin(π) = 0 — dead — along with the 14th, 21st and so on. That is the whole reason for the strike point. A softer, wider hammer additionally rolls off the high partials (it can't turn on a wavelength shorter than itself), which is the second slider.

§ 3.2Choirs, and the two-part decay

Look inside a piano and you'll see that most notes have not one string but two or three, tuned to the same pitch and struck together by one wide hammer. This is not for loudness so much as for tone and sustain. The strings of a unison are never tuned to a perfect match; a hair's-width difference makes them drift very slowly in and out of step, giving the note a gentle shimmer — a natural chorus. It also produces the piano's famous two-stage decay: at first the three strings, moving together, dump their energy into the bridge quickly and loudly (the immediate sound); then, as they fall out of phase, they start to fight each other at the bridge, the energy transfer slows, and a quieter, longer aftersound takes over. It is why a held piano note seems to bloom and then sing on.

What the soft pedal really does

The left pedal on a grand — una corda, "one string" — physically shifts the entire action sideways by a few millimetres so the hammers strike only two of each three strings (and hit them with a less-used part of the felt). The result isn't just quieter: the untouched string still resonates sympathetically, and the tone goes softer and more veiled — a genuine change of colour, which is why composers write una corda as an expressive mark, not a volume knob. On most uprights the same pedal merely moves the hammers closer to the strings, which only softens the blow.

§ 3.3The honest lie: inharmonicity and the stretched scale

Chapter 1 told a small idealisation. A real piano string is not perfectly floppy — it is a stiff steel wire, and stiffness makes the higher modes vibrate slightly faster than the perfect 2×, 3×, 4× of the ideal. The overtones are stretched sharp. The effect is tiny for any one partial but it grows with the harmonic number and with the string's stiffness, so it is worst in the short, stiff treble strings — the long bass strings are kept comparatively tame precisely because they are wound over a thin, flexible core rather than being solid rods. The consequence is one of the loveliest facts about the instrument. A piano tuner does not tune the keyboard to the mathematically exact frequencies of Chapter 1. To make octaves sound clean — beat-free — they must widen them slightly, so the top of the keyboard ends up as much as 30 cents sharp and the bottom comparably flat of "textbook" pitch. Inharmonicity is not a defect to be engineered away; a completely inharmonic-free piano sounds thin and lifeless. The slight stretch is part of what we mean by a rich piano tone. Which brings us to the question the stretch is a response to: what does "in tune" even mean, when the numbers refuse to cooperate? That is temperament.
