Chapter 1

Strings & Frequency

Before there is a piano there is a stretched string, and before there is music there is a number: the frequency of that string's vibration, in wobbles per second. Almost everything the instrument does is a way of controlling one string's frequency and then hitting it cleanly. So start there — with what a pitch is, and the three humble measurements that set it.

Sound is pressure, arriving in waves. A pitch is how often those waves arrive.

When a piano string vibrates, it shoves the air beside it back and forth, and that shove races outward as a pressure wave. If the string swings back and forth 440 times a second, it sends 440 pressure crests a second into your ear, and you hear the note we call A above middle C. Double the rate to 880 and you hear the A an octave higher; halve it to 220 and you hear the A an octave lower. That rate — cycles per second — has a unit, the hertz (Hz), and it is the single most important quantity in this whole guide. Pitch is frequency.

The piano's job is to produce a chosen frequency, on demand, 88 times over. And a stretched string is a beautifully controllable frequency machine, because its rate of vibration is set by just three things you can measure with a ruler and a scale.

§ 1.1The three dials on a string

Pin a string down at both ends, pull it tight, and pluck it. It springs back, overshoots, springs back again — vibrating at a rate that depends on how long it is, how tight it is, and how heavy it is. Nothing else. Not how hard you pluck (that sets loudness), not what it's made of except through its weight. Three dials, one frequency. The lab below is a real string with those three dials exposed.

Play with it and you rediscover, in a minute, what the friar-mathematician Marin Mersenne worked out and published in 1636. There are three laws, one per dial:

Mersenne's laws — the frequency of a string

1. Shorter is higher. Halve the length and you double the frequency (up an octave). Frequency is inversely proportional to length. This is the one your finger uses on a guitar.

2. Tighter is higher. Pull four times as hard and the frequency doubles. Frequency rises with the square root of tension — which is why tuning is a matter of tiny turns of the pin.

3. Heavier is lower. Make the string four times as massive per unit length and the frequency halves. Frequency falls with the square root of the mass per length — which is why bass strings are fat and wound with copper.

The one equationMersenne–Taylor, and why bass strings are wound

All three laws are the same formula. For an ideal flexible string of length L (metres), under tension T (newtons), with mass per unit length μ (kg/m), the fundamental frequency is

f = 12L √ T / μ  MERSENNE–TAYLOR

Read the three laws straight off it: f goes as 1/L (law 1), as √T (law 2), and as 1/√μ (law 3).

Now the bass problem. To reach the bottom A at 27.5 Hz with a plain steel wire you would need a string several metres long — no cabinet could hold it. You cannot just make it thicker either: a thick solid wire becomes stiff and rings sharp and dull. The fix is to add mass without adding stiffness — wrap a thin, flexible steel core with one or two layers of heavy copper. That raises μ, drops the pitch, and keeps the core supple. It is Mersenne's third law, solved with a wire winder.

Miss Wire

"Three dials, and only tension is easy to turn once I'm built — a quarter-turn of my pin and I'm in tune or I'm not. My length and my weight were decided the day I was strung. That's why the tuner visits and the string-maker doesn't."

§ 1.2One note, many notes: the harmonic series

Freeze the lab into "mode 2" and you'll see the string split into two humps with a still point — a node — in the middle. Mode 3 gives three humps and two nodes, and so on. Here is the crucial fact: a real plucked string does all of these at once. It vibrates as a whole (giving the fundamental, the pitch you name), and simultaneously in halves, in thirds, in quarters — each faster subdivision producing a fainter, higher tone called a harmonic or overtone.

Because the halves vibrate exactly twice as fast, the thirds three times, the quarters four times, the overtones form a perfectly regular ladder: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5… times the fundamental frequency. This ladder is the harmonic series, and it is the reason a note has a colour and not just a pitch — and, as Chapter 4 will show, the hidden source of every problem in tuning. Build one and listen:

Switch the partials on one at a time and you're not changing the pitch — the fundamental stays put — you're changing the timbre, the tone colour. Fundamental alone is a pure, hollow flute-like tone. Add the low harmonics and it warms and rounds. Add only the high ones and it thins and buzzes. A piano, a violin and a human voice singing the very same pitch differ entirely in which harmonics they emphasise and how those fade over time. Chapter 3 is about how the piano shapes exactly this recipe.

Where the intervals come from

The first few rungs of the harmonic series are the basic musical intervals, and this is no coincidence — our ears learned them from natural vibrating things. Partial 2 is one octave above the fundamental (frequency ratio 2:1). Partial 3 above partial 2 is a perfect fifth (3:2). Then a fourth (4:3), a major third (5:4), and on up. Two notes whose frequencies form a simple ratio share many harmonics and sound consonant; complicated ratios clash. The whole of harmony is downstream of this ladder.

§ 1.3The keyboard as a frequency chart

Put the two ideas together and the 88 keys become a chart of frequencies. Each key is tuned so that going up twelve keys (an octave) exactly doubles the frequency, and the twelve steps in between are spaced equally in the way the ear judges spacing — that is, by equal ratio, not equal difference. Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of two, about 1.0595. Tap along the keyboard and watch the numbers climb geometrically:

The lowest note on the piano, A0, vibrates at 27.5 Hz — slow enough that you half-feel the individual pulses as much as hear a pitch. The highest, C8, sits at 4,186 Hz, up where the tone is more glitter than melody. Between them lies every frequency the instrument can name. The next question is the one Cristofori actually had to solve: given a string tuned to the pitch you want, how do you strike it — hard or soft, at will — and then get out of its way? That is the action.
