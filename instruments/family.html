Chapter 6

The Piano Family

The instrument in the last five chapters did not spring into being. It was argued into shape over three hundred years, by rival makers each chasing a bit more volume, a bit more evenness, a wider range, a repeating action. Line the family up and you can watch the modern grand assemble itself — and see how much of "how a piano works" is really a snapshot of a machine still, quietly, evolving.

Every piano is a fortepiano that grew a cast-iron skeleton and never stopped eating octaves.

The most visible way the piano changed is its compass — how many keys it has. Cristofori's first instruments barely spanned four octaves; composers promptly wrote to the very edges, and makers answered by extending the range, which meant longer, higher-tension strings, which meant a stronger frame, which was the iron plate of Chapter 5. Pick an era and see how much keyboard a composer actually had under their hands:

§ 6.2Grand, or upright — and how big?

Among acoustic pianos the great divide is grand (strings horizontal, hammers falling back under gravity, Érard's repetition action) versus upright (strings vertical, hammers returned by springs, a simpler action — Chapter 2). Within the grands, size is destiny: a longer case means longer bass strings, a bigger soundboard, and — by Mersenne and by Chapter 3 — a deeper, more even, more powerful tone. That is the entire reason a concert hall uses a nine-foot instrument and not a baby grand. Compare the footprints:

Maestro

"People ask which piano has the 'best' bass. It's mostly length — a longer string can be tuned low without so much winding, so it's less inharmonic and sings truer (Chapter 3). That's why a nine-foot concert grand rumbles cleanly where a spinet just thuds. Physics, not magic. Though a great voicing never hurt."

§ 6.3The edges of the family

Beyond the mainstream sit the instrument's odder cousins, each of which tells you something about how a piano works by breaking one of its rules:

  • The prepared piano. Wedge bolts, coins and rubber between the strings, as John Cage did from the late 1930s, and the hammer's clean blow becomes a buzz, a thunk, a gong. It is a live demonstration of Chapter 3: you are hacking the string's mass and its overtones by hand.
  • The toy piano. No strings at all — the "hammers" strike tuned metal rods. It keeps the keyboard and the action idea, and throws away everything else, which is why it sounds like a music box.
  • The digital piano. The most radical cousin: it keeps only the weighted keyboard and the escapement's feel, and replaces string, hammer, soundboard and tuning with a loudspeaker and either a library of recorded samples or a real-time physical model of everything in this guide. When a modelled digital piano computes inharmonicity and sympathetic resonance, it is running Chapters 1–3 as software.

Which is a fitting place to end. Every one of these instruments — the fragile Florentine original, the iron-framed monster, the pocket digital — is a different answer to the same four questions we started with: how to fix a pitch, how to strike it freely, how to colour the tone, and how to tune the whole. Turn to the lexicon for the vocabulary, the constants, and every one of the 88 frequencies, set down in one place.
