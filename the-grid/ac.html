Chapter 2 · Three-Phase AC

Why Everything Comes in Threes

The grid's operating system is alternating current in three matched phases. It is not an accident of history — it is the cheapest way physics allows to move mechanical power down a wire, and it hands out free gifts: motors that start themselves, currents that cancel, and arcs that put themselves out.

§ 2.1The case for AC

The 1880s “war of the currents” was settled by one machine: the transformer. It can step AC up to voltages where transmission losses almost vanish (Chapter 3 does the arithmetic) and back down to voltages that won't kill the customer — and it only works on a changing current. DC got its revenge a century later in submarine cables (Chapter 6), but the everyday grid has been AC ever since.

AC brings a second, less obvious gift. Fifty times a second the current passes through zero — and at that instant an electric arc has no energy feeding it. Every circuit breaker in Chapter 4 is built around exploiting that fleeting moment. DC has no zeros, which is precisely why DC breakers remain hard engineering to this day.

The waveform itself is a sine — the only shape that survives differentiation intact, so inductors and capacitors merely shift it rather than distort it. Its size is quoted as RMS (root-mean-square): the DC voltage that would deliver the same heating. Your 230 V socket actually swings to ±325 V, 50 times a second.

§ 2.2Why three phases?

Take one alternator winding and its power delivery pulses: voltage and current pass through zero together, 100 times a second, so the power flow throbs from double-the-average down to nothing. A single-phase machine of any size shakes itself and the grid like an unbalanced spin-dryer.

Now add windings. With three of them, 120° apart, the three power pulses interleave so perfectly that their sum is ruler-flat: constant mechanical torque on the shaft, constant power down the line. Better still, the three currents sum to zero at every instant — so the three return wires can be merged and then thrown away. Three wires do the work of six. Two-phase systems (Niagara tried it) also smooth the power but waste copper on four wires; four or more phases smooth nothing further and multiply conductors, insulators and switchgear. Three is where the mathematics stops paying.

There is a third gift. Feed three coils, physically 120° apart, with the three phases, and their combined magnetic field doesn't pulse — it rotates, smoothly, at synchronous speed, though nothing mechanical moves. Drop a lump of conductive metal in the middle and the spinning field drags it round: that is the induction motor, the machine that runs the industrial world, self-starting with no brushes, no commutator, no electronics.

Field note

Why 50 cycles and not 5 or 500? Low frequencies make lamps flicker visibly and transformers enormous (the flux has longer to build each half-cycle, so the iron must be bigger); high frequencies multiply losses in line reactance and machine iron. The industry converged on 50 Hz (Europe, via AEG) and 60 Hz (America, via Westinghouse) — close cousins either side of the sweet spot. Older railways picked 16⅔ Hz because early series-wound traction motors commutated badly at 50.

§ 2.3The skin effect — why fat wires waste their middles

At DC, current fills a conductor evenly. At AC it does not: the alternating current's own magnetic field induces eddy voltages inside the conductor that push the current outward, crowding it into a shell at the surface. The shell's characteristic thickness — the skin depth — shrinks with frequency and with magnetic materials. At 50 Hz it is about 9 mm in copper and 12 mm in aluminium.

So a conductor much fatter than a couple of skin depths is carrying dead metal at its core. This single fact shapes the hardware of Chapter 5: transmission conductors are built as ACSR — a steel core for strength wrapped in aluminium that does the conducting (steel's skin depth is barely a millimetre, so it carries almost no current anyway) — and beyond a certain size it pays to stop growing one conductor and instead hang bundles of two or four smaller ones per phase.

§ 2.4Real power, reactive power, and the VAr

When the load is a heater, current marches in step with voltage and every watt flows one way: toward the toast. But motors and transformers must magnetise iron, and cables must charge their capacitance, and that borrowed energy sloshes back and forth twice a cycle rather than being consumed. The bookkeeping splits the flow into real power (watts — does work) and reactive power (VArs, volt-amp-reactive — sustains fields). The generator and every wire in between must carry the whole combined current, which is why utilities meter big customers' power factor and why the grid runs a whole parallel market in VArs (Chapter 6) — and why reactive starvation can collapse a grid's voltage (Chapter 7, twice).

§ 2.5Pushing power through a wire that's already live

One last piece of AC physics underpins everything in Chapters 6 and 7. Between two live busbars joined by a mostly-inductive line, power flows according to P = (V₁V₂ / X) · sin δ, where δ — the load angle — is how far the sending end's voltage wave leads the receiving end's. Generators don't “push harder” with voltage; they edge fractionally ahead in phase, like a spring twisted between two flywheels. The catch: sin δ peaks at 90°. Ask for more than the line's maximum and the spring lets go — the machines slip poles and the tie collapses. Every cascading blackout in Chapter 7 ends with exactly this.

Three phases, then — generated in threes, moved in threes. The next problem is changing its voltage a dozen times between the machine hall and your kitchen, which brings us to the quietly perfect machine: the transformer.
