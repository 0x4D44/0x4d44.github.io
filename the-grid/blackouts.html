Chapter 7 · When It Fails

The Nights the Lights Went Out

A great blackout is never one failure. It is a chain: a small event lands on a stressed system, the safeguards act exactly as designed against a problem nobody designed for, and in minutes — sometimes seconds — a machine the size of a country tears itself apart. Every case below made the grid you have today.

§ 7.1Anatomy of a cascade

Grids are planned to the N−1 rule: the system must survive the loss of any single element — any line, any generator, any transformer — without overloading anything else. The trouble begins when reality spends the margin: a hot day, a maintenance outage, a forecast miss, and the system is quietly operating closer to its limits than the studies assumed. Then the initiating event arrives — a tree, a relay, a lightning storm — and the physics of Chapter 2 takes over: power doesn't ask permission; it redistributes instantly over every remaining path. If one of those paths overloads and trips, the flow redistributes again, more steeply. Three or four steps in, the cascade is faster than any human being can think.

Before the case studies, break one yourself. The network below is honest physics — a real DC load-flow solved live in your browser. Amber squares generate, teal circles consume; the north generates more than it uses, the south consumes more than it makes, so the middle corridors run heavy. Click a line to trip it.

§ 7.29 November 1965 — the relay that darkened New York

TriggerOne backup relay, set too low two years earlier
Lost~20,000 MW · 30 million people · Ontario, New York, New England
Dark forUp to ~13 hours (New York City through the night)

At 5:16 p.m., at the height of the evening rush, a protective relay on one of five 230 kV lines running north from the Sir Adam Beck No. 2 hydro plant at Niagara operated exactly as instructed — and instructed wrongly. Set in 1963 with a threshold that made sense for 1963's loads, it had never been revisited as Toronto's demand grew. On this cold evening the line's perfectly normal loading crossed the stale setting, and the relay, believing itself to be clearing a fault, opened the line. Its flow crashed onto the other four; they tripped in 2.7 seconds; and 1,800 MW of Niagara power that had been flowing north to Toronto reversed and hurled itself south and east onto the American network, which buckled corridor by corridor into New England and down to New York. The Northeast shattered into islands; most collapsed. In Manhattan the subway stopped between stations with 800,000 riders aboard.

§ 7.314 August 2003 — the tree, the bug, and the blind control room

TriggerHot sagging lines into untrimmed trees + a frozen alarm system
Lost~62,000 MW · 50 million people · US Midwest/Northeast + Ontario
Dark forHours to 4 days

Thirty-eight years later, almost the same map. A hot Ohio afternoon; FirstEnergy's system short of reactive power and running warm. At 14:14 a race-condition bug froze the control room's alarm processor — the operators' screens stayed calm while the system beneath them was not (the software failed silent, the cruellest way). Between 15:05 and 15:41, three 345 kV lines, heavily loaded and sagging in the heat (Chapter 5's physics, to the letter), each found a tree that should have been trimmed. Nobody at FirstEnergy knew. The flows squeezed onto the 138 kV network, which failed line by line; then at 16:05 the Sammis–Star 345 kV relay — a Zone 3 distance element (Chapter 4) staring at enormous current and depressed voltage — concluded “remote fault” and tripped its healthy line. That was the cascade's starting gun: in the next eight minutes power surges swept round Lake Erie, 508 generating units at 265 plants shut down, and the Northeast went dark again — 62 GW gone.

What it changed

Mandatory, fine-backed reliability standards (goodbye voluntary guidelines); ruthless vegetation-management rules; a purge of over-reaching Zone 3 settings; and wide-area monitoring so no control room need ever be blind again. The investigators' phrase for the pattern — a hidden failure in protection, harmless until the one day it isn't — is now a term of art.

§ 7.44 November 2006 — Europe splits in three over a cruise ship

TriggerA planned line switch-off for a ship's passage, mis-calculated
Lost~14,000 MW shed · ~15 million households flickered dark · one synchronous grid became three
Dark for~2 hours — a triumph, all things considered

To let the newly-launched Norwegian Pearl pass down the River Ems, E.ON Netz switched off a 380 kV double circuit crossing the river — routine, except the N−1 arithmetic for the new configuration was done late, partly by rule-of-thumb, and a last-minute schedule change was never re-checked with the neighbours. When a coupling action shifted flows, the Landesbergen–Wehrendorf line overloaded and tripped, and the cascade ripped diagonally across Europe in fourteen seconds — splitting the continental synchronous area (Lisbon to the Black Sea, one machine, as Chapter 2 insists) into three asynchronous islands. The West woke up 9 GW short and fell to 49 Hz — automatic load shedding (Chapter 6) cut fifteen million households within seconds and held it. The North-East found itself with a 10 GW surplus and swung high. Two hours of careful resynchronisation — Chapter 1's synchroscope discipline, applied to half a continent — stitched Europe back together. The defence plan had worked; the coordination that made the split possible was overhauled anyway, seeding what is now ENTSO-E's joint operational planning.

§ 7.528 September 2016 — South Australia and the ride-through counters

TriggerTornadoes felling towers → six voltage dips in two minutes
LostThe state: ~1.83 GW of demand, 850,000 customers
Dark forMost restored in ~8 hours; full weeks for the last towers

A savage cold front — at least two tornadoes — brought down transmission towers in the state's mid-north, and each fault flickered the whole network's voltage. Individually survivable; cumulatively not, because of a setting almost nobody had thought about: many wind farms' inverters carried a counter limiting how many low-voltage ride-through events they would tolerate in a short window. The sixth dip exhausted the counters, and 456 MW of wind quietly withdrew in under seven seconds. The state's umbilical to Victoria — the Heywood interconnector — tried to make up the difference, overloaded, and tripped on loss-of-synchronism protection. Islanded with little inertia (Chapter 6) and a frequency falling at 6 Hz per second, load shedding never stood a chance: South Australia was black in moments. The postscript is constructive: the settings were changed within weeks, grid codes worldwide got serious about ride-through withstand, and the state commissioned the world's then-largest grid battery — the Hornsdale Power Reserve — whose split-second frequency response has been schooling traditional plant ever since.

§ 7.69 August 2019 — Britain's forty minutes

TriggerRoutine lightning strike → two independent-looking plants fail together
Lost~1,878 MW of generation · ~1.1 million customers shed by LFDD (~931 MW)
Dark forSupplies back in ~40 minutes — the railway took the rest of the day

At 16:52 lightning struck the Eaton Socon–Wymondley 400 kV circuit north of London. The protection cleared it in the textbook 70 ms and auto-reclosed twenty seconds later (Chapter 4 working perfectly). But the voltage disturbance rippled outward, and two things that were never supposed to fail together did: the brand-new Hornsea One offshore wind farm's control system reacted unstably and de-loaded by 737 MW, and the steam turbine at Little Barford CCGT tripped on a speed-measurement discrepancy — 244 MW more. Several hundred MW of small distributed generators dropped off too, on loss-of-mains protection. The combined loss outran the largest-infeed reserve the operator was carrying, and at 16:53:49 the frequency touched 48.8 Hz: LFDD did its blunt duty and cut about a million customers. Frequency recovered within minutes — but the disconnections had caught Newcastle airport, a hospital's supply arrangement, and, most famously, the railway: a fleet of new EMUs had protection that shut the trains down below 49 Hz and required a fitter with a laptop to restart them. Half of Thameslink stood dark across the network for hours. The grid event lasted forty minutes; the inquiry into who else's equipment secretly depends on 50.00 Hz lasted years, and GB now procures much faster reserve and more of it.

§ 7.728 April 2025 — Iberia, the first blackout of the solar age

TriggerOscillations, then a self-feeding overvoltage cascade on a solar-heavy afternoon
LostThe entire Iberian Peninsula — Spain, Portugal, Andorra; ~55 million people
Dark forMostly restored overnight (~10+ hours); full normality next day

A sunny spring midday: solar was supplying more than half of Spain's demand, few big synchronous machines were running, and — the detail the inquiries would circle — the amount of plant actively regulating voltage was thin (Chapter 6's other dial). Walk through the half hour:

Restoration was the textbook run in reverse: hydro stations black-started (Chapter 6), the links from France and Morocco energised dead corridors from the edges, and islands of supply grew and were knitted together through the night — Chapter 1's synchronising discipline, performed a hundred times before dawn. The findings have teeth for every grid that is swapping spinning steel for silicon: inverters must be asked (and paid) to regulate voltage and damp oscillations, not just pour out energy — because on 28 April the energy was never the problem.

§ 7.8What stands between you and the dark

Six blackouts, six inheritances
EventThe flawWhat your grid now carries because of it
1965 NortheastA stale relay setting nobody owned Reliability coordination, setting reviews, black start plans
2003 NortheastTrees, a silent alarm bug, over-eager Zone 3 Mandatory standards, vegetation rules, wide-area monitoring
2006 EuropeA one-off switching study done by habit Continent-wide joint operational planning & a proven split defence
2016 South AustraliaHidden ride-through counters, low inertia Ride-through grid codes, grid batteries, inertia as a product
2019 Great BritainTwo “independent” failures with one cause; brittle demand Bigger/faster reserve, LFDD review, ride-through audits of everything
2025 IberiaVoltage control thinner than the rules noticed Voltage & oscillation duties for inverter fleets — the solar age's grid code

Notice what is not on the list: a shortage of energy. Not one of these systems ran out of power — every collapse was a failure of control: settings, visibility, coordination, dynamics. That is the real moral of the whole almanac. The grid is not a warehouse of electricity; it is a performance, given continuously by thousands of machines that must agree with each other fifty times a second — from a Francis runner spinning under Ben Vorlich to the inverter on a rooftop in Seville. When the performance is good, nobody claps. The kettle just boils.
