Chapter 5 · Lines & Towers

Steel in the Landscape

A transmission line looks simple — wire, held up. But every detail is an argument won against physics: the towers know whether they pull or merely carry, the conductors are named like foxhounds and hollow with mathematics, a sacrificial wire rides on top to catch the lightning, and on a hot July afternoon the whole line quietly sits down toward the barley.

§ 5.1Reading a tower: suspension or tension?

Once you see it, you can never unsee it. Most towers on a line — perhaps four in five — are suspension towers: the insulator strings hang straight down, and the tower merely carries the weight; the conductor runs over it in one continuous catenary. But wherever the line must turn a corner, cross something important, or simply anchor a long straight section against a cascade failure, you meet a tension tower (a strain or angle tower): the conductors dead-end onto it through insulator strings pulled out horizontal, one set each side, with a slack loop of jumper conductor carrying the circuit round the steelwork between them. Tension towers are stockier, heavier, more expensive — they resist the full mechanical pull of the conductors, which is measured in tonnes.

§ 5.2Insulators: porcelain by the metre

Between live conductor and earthed steel hangs the insulator string — a chain of identical cap-and-pin discs, glass or porcelain, each good for roughly 11–15 kV of working voltage. The elegance of the design is that it is a chain: need more insulation, add more discs. A 33 kV line hangs three; a 400 kV suspension string is over twenty discs and three metres long (and doubled or quadrupled in parallel where the mechanical load demands). Each disc's underside is a maze of ribs — creepage distance — so that rain, salt and Highland peat smoke can't paint a conductive path across the surface; polluted coastal routes get extra discs or fog-profile ones.

At each end of the string you'll often see small horns or rings of plain metal: arcing horns (and on the biggest strings, grading rings). When lightning flashes the string over, the arc burns between these sacrificial horns instead of tracking down the porcelain — the string survives to insulate another day, and the breakers' auto-reclose (Chapter 4) restores the line seconds later.

§ 5.3Conductors: a kennel of aluminium hounds

The wire itself is almost never copper — too heavy, too dear. Overhead lines standardised on ACSR: aluminium conductor, steel reinforced. A core of galvanised steel strands carries the mechanical tension; layers of aluminium strands around it carry the current — a division of labour the skin effect endorses (Chapter 2: at 50 Hz the current hugs the outer layers anyway, and steel's tiny skin depth means the core barely conducts). British practice named each standard size after an animal — Dog and Lynx on the 33–132 kV networks, Zebra as the classic 400 kV conductor — so a design office could say “twin Lynx” and everyone knew the ampacity, weight and sag tables by heart.

§ 5.4Bundles, corona, and the wet-weather crackle

Above about 220 kV a single conductor per phase runs into a wall — not of current, but of electric field. If the field at the conductor surface exceeds roughly 30 kV per centimetre, the air itself begins to break down in a halo of micro-discharges: corona. It wastes power, gnaws at fittings with ozone, and hisses — famously loudest in rain, when every water droplet becomes a tiny field-concentrating spike. The cure is geometric: hang two or four conductors per phase in a bundle, held apart by spacers. The bundle behaves like one enormous fat conductor (larger effective radius → gentler surface field) while its parts stay light enough to handle — and as a bonus the phase's inductance drops, letting the line carry more power. That is why a 400 kV line's phases visibly come in pairs or quads, and why you can stand under one in drizzle and hear the grid frying quietly overhead.

§ 5.5Conductor rotation: the barrel roll

The three phases of a line do not see the world equally: the middle position couples differently to the outer ones, and to the earth beneath. Over a long route that asymmetry unbalances the phase impedances — currents and voltages drift apart, negative-sequence current heats generator rotors, and parallel telephone lines once hummed with the induced difference. The classical cure is transposition: twice along the route, at special towers, the phases rotate positions — a barrel roll in slow motion — so that over the whole length each phase spends a third of its journey in each position and the asymmetry cancels. (GB's relatively short, meshed circuits often skip full transposition and accept the small imbalance; long continental and HVDC-adjacent routes take it seriously.)

§ 5.6The wire that wants to be struck

Look at the very top of any tower: one (sometimes two) thinner wires ride above the phases, connected straight to the steel — no insulators. This is the earth wire (shield wire), and its job is to be hit. Sitting highest, it casts a protective shadow — the shielding angle — over the phase conductors below; a descending lightning leader inside that cone attaches to the earth wire instead of the phases, and the stroke's tens of kiloamps pour down the steel lattice and into the ground through the tower's buried footing.

Two things can still go wrong, and both are playable below. If the leader sneaks in at a shallow angle outside the cone — a shielding failure — it tags a phase directly and flashes the insulators over. And even a clean catch can misfire: the surge must get to earth through the tower footing resistance, and if the footing is poor (dry rock, thin Highland soil over granite) the whole tower's voltage rockets for a microsecond — high enough to flash over backwards, from steel to phase. That is the back-flashover, and it is why linesmen bury radial counterpoise wires and rods to chase footing resistance down to a few ohms. Modern earth wires earn their keep twice over: most contain optical fibres (OPGW), so the grid's protection signalling and a surprising share of the nation's internet ride the top of the pylons.

§ 5.7Sag: why a heatwave de-rates the grid

A conductor is hung, not stretched taut — it hangs in a catenary, and its sag is a thermometer. Warm the metal (July sun, plus the I²R heat of the current itself) and it lengthens; the extra length has nowhere to go but down. Statutory ground clearance is sacred, so every line carries a maximum design temperature — and therefore a maximum current, lower on a hot still day than a cold windy one. This is why ratings are seasonal, why control rooms watch the weather, and why a heatwave squeezes the grid from both ends at once: more air-conditioning demand, less line capacity. (It is also Chapter 7's opening act: the 2003 North-East blackout began with hot, heavily-loaded lines sagging into untrimmed trees.)

Steel, glass, aluminium and geometry — the grid's body. What remains is its nervous system: the control rooms, markets and interconnectors that keep the whole machine in balance every second. On to the control room.
