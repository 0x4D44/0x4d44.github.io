Chapter 1 · Generation

The Spinning Magnet

Nearly every watt on the grid begins the same way: a magnet is swept past a coil of wire, and a voltage appears. Everything else — boilers, dams, reactors, gearboxes — exists to keep that magnet turning at exactly the right speed.

§ 1.1Faraday's machine, grown enormous

In 1831 Michael Faraday showed that moving a magnet through a loop of wire induces a voltage in the loop — the faster the change in magnetic flux, the bigger the voltage. A power-station alternator (a synchronous generator) is that experiment scaled up to several hundred megawatts. The rotating part — the rotor — is an electromagnet, fed with DC through slip rings or a shaft-mounted exciter. The stationary part — the stator — carries three separate windings, spaced 120° apart around the bore. As the rotor's field sweeps past each winding in turn, each produces a sine-wave voltage, one third of a cycle behind its neighbour: three-phase AC (the subject of Chapter 2).

The frequency of that sine wave is set by geometry and speed alone: f = (pole pairs) × (rev/s). A two-pole rotor — one north, one south — must spin at 3,000 rpm to make 50 Hz, which is what a steam turbine likes: fast, compact, cylindrical, running in a hydrogen-cooled casing. A water turbine is the opposite creature: huge torque, modest speed. So hydro alternators carry many poles on a great flywheel-like rim — a dozen, two dozen, sometimes more — and turn at a few hundred rpm to make exactly the same 50 Hz. Drive the machine below and watch the bargain being struck.

Two details complete the machine. The rotor's DC field comes from an excitation system; vary the field current and you vary the voltage the machine pushes at its terminals — modern sets do this automatically tens of times a second with an AVR (automatic voltage regulator), which is how generators regulate grid voltage (a job we return to in Chapter 6). And the stator windings of a large set produce their power at a deliberately modest voltage — typically 11 to 23.5 kV, the practical limit of winding insulation — which is why every station has a step-up transformer at its fence (Chapter 3).

Field note

A 500 MW steam set's rotor is a single steel forging spinning at 3,000 rpm — its rim moves at around 200 metres per second, and the machine is filled with hydrogen because it's eight times less dense than air (less windage loss) and carries heat away better. A hydro rotor at Cruachan is the opposite: a great spider of poles metres across, turning at a stately few hundred rpm, so heavy that its inertia alone steadies the grid — a virtue we'll price up properly in Chapter 6.

§ 1.2Governors, droop, and how machines share

Spin the magnet faster and the frequency rises; load the machine harder and — unless more steam or water arrives — it slows. The governor closes that loop: it watches shaft speed and trims the steam valve or guide vanes to hold it. But here is the subtlety: you cannot put two machines on the same grid and tell both “hold exactly 50.00 Hz” — they would fight, one winding up against the other. Instead every governor is given a deliberate, gentle slope called droop: as system frequency falls 1%, the machine raises its output by a fixed fraction of its rating (a typical setting is 4% droop — a 4% frequency fall commands the full output range). Because every machine slopes the same way, they share load changes automatically, in proportion to their size, with no communication at all. Frequency itself is the signalling system.

§ 1.3Water into watts

Hydro power is gravity bookkeeping: P = ρ·g·Q·H·η — density of water × gravity × flow × head × efficiency. A tonne of water falling 277 metres (the gross head at Sloy) surrenders about 0.75 kWh; a turbine catches roughly ninety per cent of it. The shape of the turbine follows the ratio of head to flow:

  • Pelton — a wheel of split buckets struck by high-velocity jets. The choice for very high heads and modest flows: several hundred metres of mountainside behind a nozzle.
  • Francis — water spirals inward through a snail-shell casing and falls out through the runner's core. The workhorse of medium heads, and by far the most common machine in the Highlands (Sloy's four machines are Francis sets).
  • Kaplan — a ship's propeller with adjustable blades, swallowing huge flows at low head: river barrages and tidal-range schemes.
Board lore

The Hydro Board's constructional schemes were argued glen by glen through the 1940s and 50s — 78 dams and some 300 km of tunnel by the end — under the Board's founding promise of “social clause” electrification: revenue from selling bulk power south paid to string lines to crofts that could never repay the wire. Tom Johnston's engineers, led by the formidable Sir Edward MacColl, learned to hide whole power stations: Fasnakyle's hall in Glen Affric is faced in golden Tarradale stone, and the biggest machine of all was buried a kilometre inside a mountain.

§ 1.4Pumped storage — the hollow mountain

A conventional hydro scheme spends whatever the rain deposits. Pumped storage runs the ledger both ways: when power is cheap and plentiful the machines run as motors, pumping water uphill; when the evening peak bites they reverse within seconds and pour it back through the same runners. The round trip loses about a quarter of the energy — you buy four units to sell three — but you buy them cheap and sell them dear, and you buy something subtler too: hundreds of megawatts that can appear from a standing start faster than any boiler can flex.

Cruachan, above Loch Awe, was conceived by MacColl and opened by the Queen in October 1965 — one of the world's first reversible pump-turbine stations at scale, its four machines (440 MW today) standing in a cathedral of a hall blasted out of the granite a kilometre inside Ben Cruachan. It began life under the Hydro Board and was later transferred to the South of Scotland Electricity Board — the two Scottish boards trading assets as the nuclear programme grew — and today it earns much of its keep on exactly the balancing duties Chapter 6 describes. The Board answered with Foyers on Loch Ness (300 MW, 1974), reusing the site of the 1896 British Aluminium works that had been Scotland's first big hydro plant.

§ 1.5The generation family

Everything on the system ultimately divides into two tribes: machines that make their AC by spinning in synchronism — steam, gas, hydro, nuclear — and sources that make DC or variable-frequency power and reach the grid through electronic inverters — solar, batteries, and most modern wind. The distinction sounds like plumbing but turns out to govern how the whole system behaves in its worst moments, as Chapters 6 and 7 will show: synchronous machines carry stored rotational energy (inertia) and feed faults with muscle; inverters follow software.

The main types, GB perspective
TypeHow it worksCharacter
Hydro Water falls through a turbine; multi-pole alternator at a few hundred rpm. Fast to start and flex; limited by rain and geography. The Highlands' speciality since 1943.
Pumped storage Reversible pump-turbines shuttle water between two reservoirs. A grid-scale battery with a 60-year design life; also the classic black-start and fast-reserve plant.
Combined-cycle gas (CCGT) A jet-engine-derived gas turbine, its exhaust raising steam for a second turbine on the same or a twin shaft. ~55–60% efficient, flexible, and for most of the 2000s the price-setter in the GB market (Chapter 6).
Coal / oil steam Boiler → superheated steam → multi-cylinder turbine at 3,000 rpm. The 20th-century backbone — 500 MW sets at Longannet, Cockenzie, Peterhead (oil/gas) — now gone from GB entirely; the last coal set closed in 2024.
Nuclear Reactor heat raises steam; otherwise a steam station. GB's AGRs drive 3,000 rpm sets; many PWRs abroad use half-speed, four-pole machines. Steady baseload; happiest run flat out. Hunterston and Torness fed the Scottish grid for decades — and were the very reason Cruachan's pumps had cheap night-time energy to drink.
Wind Blades at 5–15 rpm through a gearbox (or direct-drive) to a generator; nearly all modern turbines connect through a power-electronic converter. Cheap energy, no fuel, but output follows weather, and the converter contributes no natural inertia unless asked to synthesise it.
Solar PV Semiconductor junctions make DC; inverters make the AC. No moving parts at all — the first generation technology in history with literally zero spin. Remember that for Chapter 7 and 28 April 2025.

§ 1.6Joining the club: synchronising

A generator cannot simply be switched onto a live grid. At the instant the breaker closes, the machine's voltage wave and the grid's must match in magnitude, frequency and phase — four conditions counting phase rotation. Get it right and the closure is a non-event: the machine slides into lockstep, and the operator loads it up by opening the governor. Get it wrong and the grid — effectively infinite — yanks the rotor into step in a fraction of a second: a torque blow that can shear couplings and rip stator bars from their slots.

The classic instrument is the synchroscope: a pointer driven by the phase difference between the incoming machine and the running busbar. Too slow and the pointer sweeps anticlockwise; too fast, clockwise. The drill every operator learned: bring the machine a shade fast, watch the pointer creep clockwise, and close the breaker just before twelve o'clock so it latches in at zero. Before synchroscopes there were the “three dark lamps” — bulbs wired across the breaker that go dark together when the two systems agree.

The machine is spinning, synchronised and loaded. But why three windings — why is the whole world wired in threes? That is Chapter 2.
