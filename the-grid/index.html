The Grid

How electricity is generated, transformed, switched, carried over the hills on steel towers, and held — second by second, day and night — at exactly fifty cycles a second. Told from the country of the North of Scotland Hydro-Electric Board, and ending with the handful of nights when it all went dark.

In 1943, with the war still on, Parliament passed the Hydro-Electric Development (Scotland) Act and created the North of Scotland Hydro-Electric Board — Tom Johnston's great project to bring “power from the glens” to crofts that had never seen an electric light. Within a generation the Board had driven tunnels through Ben Vorlich and Ben Cruachan, thrown dams across the Tummel and the Affric, and strung wire to farms the private companies had sworn could never pay. By the 1960s, nine houses in ten in the north of Scotland were connected.

This almanac is about the machine the Board plugged those crofts into — the same machine, grown vaster, that is humming outside your window right now. It is arguably the largest and most tightly-coupled machine human beings have ever built: every alternator on the system spins in lockstep with every other, from a 440 MW machine hall hollowed out of a mountain above Loch Awe to the smallest run-of-river set in a Highland glen. Push anywhere, and the whole thing feels it everywhere, at very nearly the speed of light.

Each chapter below stands on its own, but they are written in the order the electricity itself travels: made in a power station, shaped by the physics of three-phase alternating current, stepped up by transformers, guarded by switchgear, carried on towers over the moors, balanced minute-by-minute in a control room — and, once in a very long while, lost.

Board lore

The Hydro Board's first great post-war scheme was Loch Sloy, opened by the Queen Mother in October 1950: a dam across the Sloy glen, a two-mile tunnel through Ben Vorlich, and four Francis machines at Inveruglas on Loch Lomond side working under a gross head of 277 metres. Sloy could reach full output from a standing start in minutes — which is why, three-quarters of a century later, it is still what the control room reaches for when the evening peak bites.

CHAPTER 1

Generation

Why a spinning magnet makes a voltage, why hydro sets turn slowly and steam sets turn at 3,000 rpm, Pelton v Francis v Kaplan, governors and droop, and how you synchronise a machine onto a live grid without wrecking it.

 CHAPTER 2

Three-Phase AC

The case for alternating current, why three phases and not two or four, the skin effect that hollows out thick conductors, RMS volts, and the real-versus-reactive power that engineers argue about in VArs.

 CHAPTER 3

Transformers

The quietly perfect machine with no moving parts: turns ratios, why transmission runs at 400,000 volts, star and delta windings and the Dyn11 vector group, tap changers, Buchholz relays and the 100 Hz hum.

 CHAPTER 4

Switchgear & Protection

How to interrupt 50,000 amps without dying: arcs and current zeros, bulk-oil, air-blast, SF₆ and vacuum breakers, and the relays — overcurrent, differential, distance — that find a fault in forty milliseconds.

 CHAPTER 5

Lines & Towers

Suspension and tension towers, insulator strings, ACSR conductors with animal names, bundles and corona, phase transposition, the earth wire that takes the lightning, and why sag on a hot day sets the rating of a line.

 CHAPTER 6

Running the Grid

Fifty hertz as a national bargain: inertia, TV pickup, the merit order, reserve and response, reactive power and voltage, HVDC interconnectors under the sea, and what black start actually involves.

 CHAPTER 7

When It Fails

Six great blackouts, dissected: the 1965 relay that darkened New York, the 2003 tree in Ohio, Europe split in three in 2006, South Australia 2016, Britain's 9 August 2019, and the Iberian collapse of April 2025 — plus a cascade simulator of your own to break.
How to read this

Every diagram with a control strip underneath is live — drag the sliders, press the buttons, break things. Nothing here is a video; it is all computed in your browser as you watch, which is rather the point: the grid itself is not a picture either.
