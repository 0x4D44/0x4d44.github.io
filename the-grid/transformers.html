Chapter 3 · Transformers

The Quietly Perfect Machine

No moving parts, efficiency touching 99.8%, a working life of half a century — the transformer is the reason the grid exists at all. It also hums, breathes, changes its own taps under load, and announces its internal illnesses through a jar of gas invented in 1921.

§ 3.1Two coils and a ring of iron

Wind two coils around the same iron core. AC in the first coil drives an alternating magnetic flux round the core; the changing flux threads the second coil and — Faraday again — induces a voltage in it. Each turn of wire, on either side, sees the same volts-per-turn, so the voltages stand in the ratio of the turn counts: V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁. Power in must equal power out (minus a whisper of loss), so the currents run in the inverse ratio: step the voltage up ten times and the current falls to a tenth. That inverse is the whole grid in one line.

§ 3.2Why 400,000 volts?

A wire wastes power as heat at a rate of I²R — current squared. Halve the current and you quarter the loss. Since power is volts × amps, the way to halve the current while moving the same power is to double the voltage. That square law is merciless in both directions, and the interactive below lets you feel it: try pushing a power station's output down a hundred miles of line at generator voltage.

Field note

Transformer efficiency is so good it is quoted in losses, and the losses come in two flavours an engineer can hear and feel: iron loss (magnetising the core — present the moment it's energised, load or no load, and the source of the 100 Hz hum as the steel laminations magnetostrict twice per cycle) and copper loss (I²R in the windings, rising with the square of load). A big unit wastes perhaps 0.2–0.5% at full load — but 0.3% of 800 MVA is still a couple of megawatts of heat, hence the radiator banks and pumped-oil cooling (ONAN → ONAF → OFAF as the fans and pumps cut in).

§ 3.3Star, delta, and the eleven o'clock transformer

A three-phase transformer has three primary windings and three secondary windings, and each set can be connected in one of two shapes. In star (Y), one end of each winding meets at a common neutral point — which can be earthed, giving the system its reference to ground, and brought out as a fourth wire. Each winding only sees line voltage ÷ √3, which is kind to insulation at the top end of the network. In delta (D, or “mesh”), the three windings join end-to-end in a triangle: no neutral at all, each winding sees full line voltage — and, crucially, the triangle forms a closed loop where the awkward triplen harmonics of the magnetising current can circulate harmlessly instead of escaping into the network.

So practical transformers mix the shapes, and the mixture twists the phase angle: connect one side in delta and the other in star and the secondary voltages emerge shifted by 30°. Engineers name the combinations like a clock face — the vector group. The classic British distribution transformer is Dyn11: Delta HV, star (“y”) LV with the neutral brought out, LV phasor pointing at 11 o'clock — 30° ahead of the HV. Generator transformers are usually the mirror image (star HV with its neutral earthed, delta LV facing the machine). None of it is pedantry: parallel two transformers from different clock hours and the 30° disagreement drives a dead short between them.

Field note

Between 400 kV and 275 kV the grid mostly doesn't use two-winding transformers at all but autotransformers — one tapped winding shared by both sides, star connected, saving iron and copper where the ratio is modest — usually with a small delta “tertiary” winding buried inside to give the triplens their loop (and often to hang reactive-power kit from, Chapter 6). And where a delta network needs an earth that doesn't exist, a zig-zag earthing transformer manufactures a neutral out of pure geometry.

§ 3.5Holding the volts: tap changers

Grid voltage sags at teatime and swells at 3 a.m., but your socket is promised 230 V (+10%/−6%). The correction happens inside transformers: extra turns at one end of the HV winding, selected by a motorised on-load tap changer that can step the ratio ±10-ish% in small bites without interrupting the load — a diverter switch shuffles the current between taps through a resistor in a few dozen milliseconds, tens of thousands of times a year. Listen outside a primary substation and you can hear it: a periodic mechanical chunk as the network breathes.
