Chapter 6 · Running the Grid

The Fifty-Hertz Bargain

Electricity is the only commodity manufactured, delivered and consumed in the same instant. Somewhere, right now, a control room is holding national supply equal to national demand to within a rounding error — and the proof is a single number, trembling around 50.00.

§ 6.1Frequency is the fuel gauge

Every synchronous machine on the grid spins in lockstep (Chapter 1), so the whole system has one shared speed: the frequency. And frequency obeys a beautifully blunt law. When demand exceeds generation, the deficit is paid — instantly, automatically — out of the kinetic energy of every spinning rotor in the country, and they all slow down together. Generation exceeds demand, they speed up. Frequency is the imbalance, made visible.

How fast it moves depends on how much spinning steel is connected — the system inertia. A grid of 500-tonne steam and hydro rotors rides out the loss of a power station like a flywheel; a grid of inverter-fed solar and wind (Chapter 2's other tribe) has almost no stored rotation, and the same loss drops the frequency several times faster — leaving less time for reserves to catch it before the automatic load-shedding relays (LFDD, at 48.8 Hz in GB) start disconnecting towns to save the rest. Board lore

The most British of all grid phenomena: TV pickup. The credits roll on a big broadcast, a million kettles go on within ninety seconds, and demand leaps by hundreds — occasionally thousands — of megawatts. The all-time record remains the 1990 World Cup semi-final penalty shoot-out (England v West Germany): about 2,800 MW, close to three power stations' worth of hot beverage. Control rooms schedule for it with the Radio Times open on the desk, and the classic counter is pumped-storage hydro — machines at Cruachan, Foyers and Dinorwig spinning ready to catch the surge in seconds. The habit is fading with streaming, but a cup final penalty shoot-out still writes its signature on the frequency chart.

§ 6.2Who runs tonight? The merit order

Frequency response handles seconds; someone still has to decide which stations run each half-hour. The organising idea is the merit order: rank everything by the marginal cost of one more megawatt-hour, dispatch from the cheapest up until demand is met, and let the last unit needed set the price for all. Nuclear won't flex, so it runs first. Wind and solar burn nothing, so when the weather delivers, they push everything else up the stack — and on a wild night in the Minch there can be more wind than the wires south can carry, at which point the operator pays wind farms to stop (constraint costs: the price of a grid built for the old geography of coal, and the economic case for the new lines and links below).

§ 6.3The other dial: volts and VArs

Frequency is one system-wide number; voltage is thousands of local ones, and it is managed with Chapter 2's other currency — reactive power. Generators' AVRs push and absorb VArs; transformer tap changers (Chapter 3) trim each layer; shunt capacitors prop up heavy-load evenings, shunt reactors tame lightly-loaded nights (a long unloaded line generates VArs and its voltage balloons — the operator's 3 a.m. problem). Because VArs travel badly, this support must be scattered across the map, not parked in one corner: a region that runs short of reactive support sees its voltage sag, then collapse — the slow-motion fate of several blackouts in Chapter 7.

Field note — new machines for old virtues

As the coal fleet's great rotors left the system, they took their free inertia, fault current and VArs with them. The replacements are purpose-built: synchronous condensers — a synchronous machine spinning unloaded, all services and no fuel — sometimes with a flywheel bolted on. Fittingly for this almanac, one of GB's first “stability parks” of this kind opened at Keith, in Moray — deep in the old Hydro Board country, where the wind fleet is thickest and the spinning steel is now scarcest. The pumped-storage machines at Cruachan and Foyers moonlight in the same trade: spinning-in-air, providing inertia and VArs while pumping nothing at all.

§ 6.4Direct current's revenge: interconnectors

Why do the cables under the sea run on DC, a century after AC won? Because a long AC cable defeats itself. A cable is a rolled-up capacitor, and at 50 Hz that capacitance must be charged and discharged a hundred times a second — a charging current that flows whether or not any customer takes a single watt. Stretch the cable and the charging current grows until it fills the cable's entire rating: beyond roughly 50–80 km, a solid-insulation transmission cable can carry nothing but itself. DC charges the cable once, at energisation, and every amp thereafter is freight.

GB is now laced with these links — to its neighbours, and increasingly to itself, because the cheapest route for Highland and offshore wind to reach English demand is often a DC cable down the seabed, bypassing the crowded onshore grid:

§ 6.5The layered defence (and the darkest drill)

Put the chapter together and balancing is a relay race across timescales:

  • 0–2 seconds — inertia and battery response: physics and power electronics, no decisions involved.
  • 2–30 seconds — primary response: governors feel the droop (Chapter 1) and open their valves; pumped storage catches the surge.
  • 30 seconds–30 minutes — the control room re-dispatches: reserve plant loads up, interconnectors re-schedule, frequency is walked back to 50.00.
  • Hours–years — markets, maintenance seasons, and building the kit: new links, new storage, new stability parks.

And beneath it all sits the drill nobody wants to run: black start. If the grid ever goes fully dark, most power stations cannot restart — they need grid power for their own pumps, fans and excitation. The plan begins with the stations that can wake themselves: hydro above all (a battery bank, a small diesel, open the guide vanes and the glen does the rest). A station like Cruachan or Sloy energises a dead transmission corridor, picks up a block of load, and other stations synchronise to the growing island; islands are then knitted together, carefully, exactly as Chapter 1's synchroscope demands. The full national restoration is measured in days — which is the best argument there is for never needing it, and the subject of the final chapter.
