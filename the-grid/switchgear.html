Chapter 4 · Switchgear & Protection

Interrupting Fifty Thousand Amps

When a line comes down or a cable fails, the grid drives thousands of amps into the fault — and something must open the circuit while that current is still flowing. You cannot simply pull the contacts apart: the current refuses to stop, and burns on across the gap as an arc hotter than the surface of the sun. Everything in a switchyard is designed around taming that arc.

§ 4.1The arc, and the gift of the current zero

Part two contacts carrying current and the current does not stop — it ionises the gap and keeps flowing as a plasma arc at 10,000–20,000 K. The arc is a conductor; while it burns, the circuit is still closed. Stretch it, cool it, blast it — a DC arc will fight you for every millimetre, which is why DC switchgear is genuinely hard. But AC hands the breaker a gift fifty times a second: the current zero (Chapter 2). For an instant, no current flows and the arc goes out by itself.

The battle is what happens in the next few microseconds. The circuit slams voltage back across the open gap — the transient recovery voltage — trying to re-light the arc. Meanwhile the still-hot gap is furiously de-ionising, recovering its insulating strength. If insulation wins the race, the circuit is broken; if the voltage wins, the arc restrikes and the breaker must wait, arcing, for the next zero. Every breaker technology of the last century is a different way of loading the dice: fresh oil, blasted air, SF₆ gas, or nothing at all.

Board lore

Anyone who worked a 1960s–70s switchyard remembers the air-blast era: banks of compressors charging receivers to 20-odd atmospheres, and an operation that fired the stored air through the interrupter heads with a report like naval gunfire — routine switching that rattled windows a mile off. SF₆ conquered the transmission system from the 1970s partly because it is a superb quencher, and partly because it is quiet. The price: SF₆ is the most potent greenhouse gas ever assessed (over 20,000× CO₂, century-for-century), so today's engineers meter every gram, and the newest switchgear is going back to clean air and vacuum at ever higher voltages.

§ 4.3Things that switch but must never break

A switchyard is full of switch-shaped devices that are not circuit breakers. An isolator (disconnector) provides an air gap you can see — the point of it is safety of the working party, not interruption — and it can only be operated once the breaker has already stopped the current. An earth switch then clamps the dead conductor to ground, so that induced voltage from the live parallel circuits (or a switching error a mile away) cannot bite the linesmen. § 4.4Protection: finding the fault in forty milliseconds

A breaker is only as good as the brain that orders it to trip. That brain is protection: relays fed by current transformers (CTs) that scale thousands of amps down to a measurable 1 or 5 A, and voltage transformers (VTs) that do the same for volts. The relay's job description is brutal: detect any fault, trip only the faulted element, leave everything else running, and do it in a few cycles — for a big grid fault, from short-circuit to open contacts in under a tenth of a second.

  • Overcurrent (IDMT) — the veteran. Trips faster the bigger the current, with deliberately graded delays so the breaker nearest the fault goes first and upstream devices stand back. Runs the distribution system.
  • Differential (unit) protection — Kirchhoff with an attitude: measure current entering and leaving a transformer, busbar or cable; any difference is a fault inside, trip instantly. Absolutely selective, needs CTs (or a fibre link) at both ends. The transformer's Buchholz relay (Chapter 3) is its mechanical cousin.
  • Distance protection — the transmission workhorse. From volts and amps at one end, the relay computes the impedance to the fault — which, on a uniform line, is the distance. No communications needed: just geometry, in zones.

§ 4.5Second chances and spare busbars

Most overhead-line faults are transient — a lightning flashover, wind clashing two conductors, a swan, a mylar balloon. The fault arc needs the grid to feed it; once the breakers cut the power for half a second, the air de-ionises and the line is perfectly healthy again. So transmission breakers practise auto-reclose: trip, wait a beat, and quietly close again. Perhaps four faults in five never trouble the customer at all. If the fault is still there — a fallen tree, a broken insulator — the protection trips again and locks out for the repair crew. (Cables get no second chances: a cable fault is always permanent, so reclosing onto it would just hammer the damage.)

The other quiet redundancy is the busbar arrangement. A big grid substation is not one bar but two (or a ring, or a mesh): every circuit can be switched to either bar through its isolators, a bus coupler breaker ties the bars together, and a section of busbar can be emptied, isolated and worked on while the station carries on around it. When you hear that a substation is “running split” or a circuit has been “transferred to reserve bar”, this is the machinery being played like an instrument.

Field note

Grading protection is an art form with a cruel constraint: every backup layer must be slower than the layer below it, so delays stack up toward the source — yet the biggest faults, nearest the generators, are exactly where slow clearance does the most damage. Watch this tension snap in Chapter 7: a mis-set backup relay with no timer darkened the whole American North-East in 1965, and an over-eager Zone 3, seeing heavy load as a distant fault, helped it happen again in 2003.
