# 2026.07.26 — Iron Vertex, a procedural rollercoaster

Arthur saw a rollercoaster demo built with Opus 5 in a YouTube comparison
video and asked for the same thing here. I could not get at the video —
WebFetch only returns YouTube's shell, transcript services 403, and the
Chrome extension was not connected — so I asked, and he sent four
screenshots. Those were the spec: "IRON VERTEX / procedural coaster lab",
RIDE / NEW TRACK / CAM buttons, a track nameplate, and SPEED / G-FORCE /
HEIGHT / LENGTH tiles over a low-poly park.

## Shape

`iron-vertex/track.js` is pure ESM — no Three.js, no DOM — so everything
that must be *correct* runs under `node --test`. `index.html` only draws
it. That split paid for itself immediately: every defect below was found
by a Node test in milliseconds, not by squinting at a 3D scene.

## What the tests caught

Almost nothing survived first contact. In order:

- **A parameterisation bug.** Region roles (station / lift / free / brake)
  were derived from index fractions, but splicing the loop re-indexes the
  circuit. The sim thought the train was free-running while it was still
  on the chain. Fix: assign roles once and carry them through every
  remap, never re-derive.
- **The lift released early.** The plan curve is sampled uniformly in
  *angle*, not arc length, so the profile's crest does not land where the
  fraction says. The train was let go while still climbing and stalled a
  metre short. Fix: hold the chain until the track genuinely falls —
  read the boundary off the geometry.
- **An S-shaped crest.** Three asymmetric keyframes around the apex made
  Catmull-Rom overshoot into a second summit.
- **A 30g pull-out.** The drop's length was a *fraction* of the circuit,
  so on a short circuit it was steeper than vertical and the valley had a
  3 m radius. Fixed twice over: size the lift and drop in metres, then
  relax the whole profile against an explicit g budget until every
  vertical transition has a rideable radius.
- **A creased loop.** The loop's forward axis was snapped to the nearest
  base sample, putting a step in the geometry at each one.
- **A stretched loop.** Worse: the forward excursion used the raw 3D
  tangent, so on climbing track the base gradient poured into the loop
  and pinched its top to a 2 m radius. A vertical loop belongs in the
  *world* vertical plane.
- **A 140-degree snap roll.** Banking was computed pointwise, which says
  nothing about how fast track may roll. Where a circuit reverses its
  turn the ideal bank flips ±70 degrees in three metres. Rate-limited.
- **The one that poisoned everything else.** Profile relaxation moves the
  heights of already-resampled points, so the samples stopped being
  evenly spaced — and curvature is estimated by dividing by the *nominal*
  spacing. Every g figure in unevenly-spaced stretches was overstated by
  up to 3x, which is why loops kept being rejected as too violent. One
  re-resample fixed the spacing test, the g figures and the loop rate.

## Judgement calls

- **Verify, don't assume.** Generation simulates the finished track and
  retries with lower hills until the train demonstrably completes the
  circuit; the plan curve is damped until its tightest turn is rideable.
  Heuristics propose, the simulation disposes.
- **Loop placement is a search, not a fixed point.** A loop's size is
  dictated by entry speed, so one fixed entry made it a lottery — 4/120
  circuits got one. Ranking every free-running sample by flatness and
  straightness and taking the best that yields a valid loop pushed it
  past a third.
- **Vendored three.js** (following `northern-line-1987`) rather than a
  CDN: works offline, immune to someone else's outage, and testable
  headlessly.
