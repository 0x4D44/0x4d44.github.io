# 2026.07.31 — Iron Vertex: sound, a park, passengers, and a loop that stopped going through itself

Arthur asked for the lot: sound, better graphics, collision detection "so
that loop the loop track doesn't intersect itself", more buildings, richer
trees, passengers in the cars. Most of that is decoration. One item was a
real bug, and it turned out to be universal.

## Every looping circuit was passing through itself

The clue was in the request, so the first thing was to measure it rather
than believe it. A self-clearance scan over the 120 test seeds on the
code as it stood:

```
OLD: loops 99   collisions (<3.4m) 99   worst gap 0.12 m
```

Not "some seeds". **Every** loop the generator had ever drawn ran through
its own steel, and the closest approach was twelve centimetres. It had
never been noticed because nothing measured it: the profile relaxer, the
frame builder and the energy check are all one-dimensional — they walk
*along* the track and have no opinion about two stretches of it sharing a
cubic metre.

It is also not an accident of tuning. It is forced. Along a loop the
train's position advances as `advance·θ/2π + a·sin θ`, whose derivative
has to go negative over the top — that reversal is precisely what swings
the track's heading through a full turn and puts the rider upside down.
Reversal means the ascending and descending legs cross, and because the
two legs sit at *identical heights* at θ and 2π−θ by construction, a loop
drawn in a single vertical plane must pass exactly through itself near
the bottom. There is no size of loop that avoids it. I checked the
obvious escape — stretch the loop forward until the along-track motion is
monotonic — and it does avoid the crossing, by ceasing to be a loop: the
tangent never passes vertical and the rider never inverts.

Real coasters solve it the way this one now does. The legs **splay**:
`−splay·cos(θ/2)` runs from −2.6 m at entry, through zero at the top, to
+2.6 m at exit, with zero slope at both ends so the join stays straight,
and the base path either side is eased across by the same amount over 34 m
of approach with a smootherstep (zero first *and* second derivative, so
there is no curvature step where it joins). Head-on the loop now looks
like a real one — legs apart at the bottom, converging at the top.

That fixes the loop against itself. It does nothing about a 25 m loop
thrown up through the airtime hill next door, which depends on where the
plan curve happens to fold back and is only knowable after the splice. So
`selfClearance()` (spatial hash, linear in the sample count) measures the
finished circuit, and loop placement is now *gated* on it: a candidate
that collides is discarded and the next one tried. A circuit with no loop
is a better outcome than one made of intersecting steel.

Result over the same 120 seeds: minimum clearance **4.61 m**, and the loop
rate essentially unchanged (98 circuits with a loop, against 99 before).
The closest approach on a looping circuit is always the loop's own legs,
at the same height (≤1.31 m apart vertically) and cleared **sideways** by
at least 4.45 m — which is the exact assertion the new test makes, because
if the clearance ever came from a height difference the loop would be
planar again.

Three tests carry it: `selfClearance` against a figure-of-eight, a stadium
and a circle; every seed clear of `MIN_SELF_CLEARANCE`; and the "legs pass
beside each other, not through" check above. The figure is also on the
page — the ride card reads `… · 5.1 m clearance`, and the browser test
asserts it is a real number and a safe one.

## The rest of it

- **Sound** is synthesised, not sampled — nothing is fetched. Filtered
  noise for wheels and wind, a scheduled tick for the chain dogs whose
  rate *is* the speed, a hiss on the fin brakes, and a small chorus of
  detuned voices when the riders get airtime or a heavy bottom. The
  mixing decisions are a pure function, `rideMix()`, with no reference to
  Web Audio at all, so seven `node --test` cases cover the part worth
  covering. The AudioContext is only opened inside a gesture, and the
  static check enforces that.
- **The park.** Ground with drawn-in-code grass, a lake with a wave
  shader, a gravel midway with stalls, marquees, lamps and pennants, a
  turning Ferris wheel, a carousel, a drop tower, a bandstand on the
  infield, 200 little people with an idle sway, a town on the horizon,
  hills behind it, drifting cloud and three circling flocks. Four tree
  species instead of one.
- **Passengers**, four to a car, with their own shirts, skin, hair and
  nerve: the brave ones put their hands up on the airtime and everyone
  gets pressed into the seat at the bottom.
- Each seed now picks a **livery**, and the station carries a board with
  the track's name painted on it.

## What the performance work taught me

The browser test runs under SwiftShader, which is a software rasteriser,
and it is brutally honest about the things a real GPU hides. The first
complete version ran at **1.37 fps** against the old page's 4.31 — and
because the frame loop clamps `dt`, a slow renderer does not merely look
bad, it runs the *ride* in slow motion, so the test's "did the train
climb?" poll would have timed out. Measuring by hiding one thing at a
time: ground 157 ms, trees 111 ms, track 91 ms, train 44 ms per frame.

The interesting one was the train. Four cars of sixteen passengers was
about 120 tiny meshes, which is 120 draw calls plus another 120 in the
shadow pass, for objects a few centimetres across. Merging each car into
one geometry and instancing the passengers as four meshes (torsos, heads,
hair, arms) took the whole train to **six** draws, with every rider still
individually coloured and every arm still free to go up. That is better
engineering on real hardware too; it was just invisible there.

The rest is an **adaptive quality ladder**: watch the smoothed frame time
and, below about eighteen frames a second, give up the shadow resolution,
then the shadows, then the resolution the scene is drawn at. It only ever
steps down — hunting between two levels would be worse than either. A
real GPU never leaves the top rung. Under SwiftShader it settles at
**4.27 fps**, level with the old page, on a scene carrying perhaps thirty
times as much.

One small trap worth remembering: `half` is a reserved word in GLSL, and
a raw `ShaderMaterial` needs `#include <tonemapping_fragment>` and
`<colorspace_fragment>` at the end of its fragment shader or it will not
match the tone-mapped scene around it.
