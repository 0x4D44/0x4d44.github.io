# 2026.08.04 — Iron Vertex: dials, and a city to put them in

Arthur asked for four things: generation controls (length, height, speed,
buildings), an option to run the coaster through a busy city, a denser
and more realistic city modelled on Chicago, and — quoting — "really go
to town with the 3d modelling".

Two of those are one job and two are another, so this is two write-ups.

---

## Part one: the dials

### Speed is not a knob you can just add

Length and height are honest dials: they name a thing and the generator
builds it. Speed is not, and pretending otherwise would have been the
easy lie. **On a gravity coaster the speed at the bottom of the first
drop IS the height of the drop.** A "speed" slider that quietly did
nothing but scale the lift would be a height slider with a different
label.

So it is implemented as a target, and the generator meets it the two ways
a real designer would:

- **Asked for more than the lift can give** → a magnetic launch out of
  the station makes up the difference. Ask for 145 km/h and the circuit
  comes back launched, with a low top hat, because that is what a
  low-and-fast coaster is.
- **Asked for less than the drop will produce** → the drop is *trimmed*:
  it levels out higher up, so less of the apex is converted. Also real.

And where the two dials genuinely conflict — a 70m lift and a 90 km/h top
speed cannot coexist — the readout shows **what was built, not what was
asked for**. The slider says 132 km/h after you drag it to 140. That is
the honest answer and it took no more code than the dishonest one.

### The three bugs the dials exposed

Each of these was already in the generator. None could fire until a dial
could ask for a circuit it would never have drawn for itself.

**1. A floor is not a floor unless the hills stand on it.**

The speed ceiling works by refusing to let the track go more than v²/2g
below the summit feeding it. I applied it to the valleys and left the
peaks measured from platform level — so when the retry loop flattened the
hills, their summits went *below their own valleys*. The profile
inverted, the spline dived through the floor to reach them, and the
ceiling was exceeded by 40%. Hills have to be built up from the floor,
not merely clipped to it.

**2. A shoulder in fixed units on a feature sized in metres.**

The keyframes either side of a summit sat at a flat ±0.04 of the
circuit. Fine while every circuit is about 700m. On a 2.4km circuit the
first drop occupies 0.048 of the track and the shoulder lands 19 metres
before the bottom of it — so the spline drops 71 metres in 19, and the
pull-out is a 9m radius at 131 km/h.

**Fourteen g.** Measured, on `{length: 2400, height: 78, speed: 145}`.

The shoulder is now a fraction of the feature it shoulders, with the
constant chosen so every circuit the generator used to draw still gets
exactly 0.04.

**3. The retry loop could not reach the shape that was wrong.**

Eight attempts flatten the hills and then remove the loop. They never
touched the top hat or the first drop — so a launched circuit whose only
problem was the pull-in off the launch track failed all eight attempts
with the same 6.3g. Both are stretched on retry now.

### Determinism, and why it cost extra

Seeds are shareable links. Somebody may have sent one. So every change
above was checked against a captured baseline of 40 catalogue seeds, and
two of them had to be walked back:

- Making the "does this circuit want a second lift?" draw unconditional
  advanced the rng stream on **launched** circuits, because the original
  `!launched && rng() < 0.6` short-circuits and never calls `rng()`.
  Fifteen of forty seeds reshuffled. The short-circuit is now load-bearing
  and commented as such.
- The retry stretch, applied unconditionally, reshaped **29 of 40** —
  one by 12% of its length. It is gated on whether any dial has been
  touched, which is also the correct scope: the faults it cures only
  exist because the dials can ask for combinations the generator never
  drew.

Net: **one** catalogue seed changed, by 0.2% of its length, and that one
is the shoulder fix doing its job on a long circuit.

### Long, low and slow needs another lift

`{length: 2400, height: 22, speed: 60}` stalled on the far side. Of
course it did: 2.4km loses 27m of height to rolling friction and drag,
and a 22m lift has 19m to give. The generator now works out the
shortfall and covers it — five mid-circuit lifts on that combination
instead of the one it fancied. "Then it needs another lift" is what a
designer would say, so that is what the generator says.

## Part two: Chicago

`city.js`, ~950 lines, six building typologies plus two named landmarks.
Chicago because it is the city that invented this shape: a lake plain, a
river through it, a rectilinear grid over the lot, and the tall stuff
clustered where the land is dearest.

The typologies are the six things Chicago actually builds — greystone
walk-up with a stoop and a bay window; brick mid-rise with a bracketed
cornice and a fire escape; open-deck parking garage; 1920s setback
wedding cake, stepped as the zoning ordinance said, with a crown;
Miesian curtain wall on a recessed plaza; and, one of each, hand-sited,
a nine-tube bundled tower terminating at four heights and a tapered
X-braced one. Plus the L on steel bents with a train running it, the
river with bascule bridges, roof water tanks, and traffic.

### The three things that went wrong, all of them geometry

**The core box hides everything inside it.** I built each typology as a
full-size box with the floor bands inset into it. Which is exactly
backwards: the box is opaque, so the only thing you ever see is the box.
A forty-storey glass tower rendered as a featureless black slab with
every one of its carefully modelled floors sealed inside. The shell is
now under size and the bands stand proud of it.

**A per-instance colour multiplies; it does not replace.** Setting the
instance tint to the typology's own palette colour squares it. Brick
survived. Glass — already 0x39434d — went to near black. The tint stays
near white now and only varies.

**Seven hundred buildings is half a million triangles.** Measured under
SwiftShader: 3.5fps with no buildings, 0.7 with them all, against 2.1 for
the park. Three fixes, in order of value:

1. **Level of detail.** Past 430m from the origin a building is a plain
   massing block: 24 triangles against 600. The ring is anchored on the
   origin rather than the camera — worse use of the budget, but it never
   pops, and the camera never goes far from the coaster anyway.
2. **Buildings cast no shadow.** The sun's shadow camera is 460m across
   and the grid runs to 2300m, so a cast pass re-renders the whole city
   to light a strip in the middle of it. The coaster and the train still
   cast onto the streets, which is the shadow that tells you where the
   ride is.
3. **Fewer bands.** A glass tower at 13 floors reads the same as at 26.

City and park now measure the same, within the noise of a loaded box.

### The street network costs one quad

The roads are not geometry. They are a 2048² canvas painted from the
same `gridLines()` the buildings are placed on — asphalt, kerbs, dashed
centre lines on the avenues only, crossings at every junction, and the
river straight over the top of it. One texture, one plane, one draw call
for two kilometres of city, and because both consumers read the same
function there is no alignment to keep in step.

### Where the skyline goes

Neither cluster is centred on the origin, and that took one screenshot to
work out. The coaster is built at the origin and the corridor it cuts
deletes whatever it passes through — so a downtown centred there gets a
bite taken out of the middle of it. Offset, the coaster runs along the
*edge* of downtown: towers on one side, mid-rise on the other. Both
landmarks sit beyond 470m, further out than the longest circuit the
length dial can draw, because a landmark that vanishes on some seeds is
not a landmark.

## Postscript: the flicker, and a diagnosis that was wrong

Arthur reported the windows flickering badly, and guessed aliasing. I
had a better-sounding theory to hand: I had built each floor band at
*exactly* the core shell's width, so the glass and the shell were
coplanar, and coplanar surfaces z-fight. Certain, mechanical, my own
bug from the same session — obviously the cause.

It was not the cause. Measured, by grabbing two frames with a static
camera and counting pixels that changed *in isolation* (z-fighting is
speckle; clouds and traffic move in blobs):

```
coplanar   speckle 646, 608
separated  speckle 625, 633
```

Identical. The coplanarity is real and still worth fixing — it is
guaranteed to fight at some depth range — but it was not what anybody
could see.

Arthur's guess was right. A modelled floor band is about two metres
tall; at three hundred metres that is a pixel and a half of bright glass
against a pixel of dark spandrel, and **geometry cannot be filtered**.
Every frame the sample lands somewhere slightly different and the whole
facade crawls. Multisampling does not touch it, because the aliasing is
not on the silhouette — it is across the face.

The fix is to stop making distant facades out of geometry. Past 190m a
building is now a plain box wearing a **mipmapped facade texture**, with
its UVs scaled per instance so a window bay is 6.4m and a storey 3.55m
whatever size the building is. At three hundred metres the hardware
reads a mip level where those bands have already been averaged into a
flat tone, and it is perfectly steady.

Measured, as the mean high-frequency contrast across the band of the
frame where the mid-distance city sits:

```
geometric bands to 430m   hard edges 6.39% of adjacent pixel pairs
textured boxes past 190m  hard edges 3.65%
```

Two lessons, and the second is the one worth keeping.

**A confident mechanical explanation is still a hypothesis.** Coplanar
surfaces *do* z-fight. Everything about the reasoning was sound except
whether it was happening here, and that is exactly the part that needs
measuring rather than deriving.

**And the user's guess deserved the same weight as mine.** "An aliasing
problem perhaps?" was a one-line hunch against my detailed mechanism,
and it was right. I nearly spent the session polishing the wrong fix.

## What to take from this

The dials did not add features so much as **apply pressure to what was
already there**. Every one of the three generator bugs had been sitting
in the code the whole time, unreachable because the generator's own
choices never wandered into the corner where they lived. A control
surface is a fuzzer with a nicer name.

And the recurring shape from the last two sessions held again: the city's
worst bug was the same class as the tunnel's — **two things describing
one piece of geometry**, here a core box and the bands that were supposed
to sit on it, with the wrong one winning.
