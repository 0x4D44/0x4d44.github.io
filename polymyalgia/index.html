01 / Start with the tissues

The name says “muscle pain”.
The anatomy tells a richer story.

Imagine waking up and finding that putting on a jumper, turning in bed or getting out of a chair has become painfully difficult. PMR often produces a recognisable combination: aching around both shoulders, hip-girdle discomfort and prolonged stiffness after rest. It usually begins in later life and can develop over days or weeks. [1]

The important distinction is between a painful movement system and a muscle that has lost its ability to generate force. Bursae are small fluid-lined cushions that let neighbouring structures glide. Tendon sheaths, tissue around tendons and joint linings can also become inflamed. Ultrasound and MRI studies show these periarticular and sometimes extracapsular patterns in PMR; the disease is not simply widespread destruction of muscle fibres. [8][9]

PMR is uncommon before age 50 and is much more typical after 65. Women are affected more often than men. Fatigue, reduced appetite, low mood, weight loss or a mild fever may accompany the stiffness. These systemic symptoms are real, but also occur in other illnesses: the overall pattern matters more than any single item. [1]

Go deeper · Stiffness, pain and weakness are not interchangeable

“I cannot raise my arms” may mean that raising them hurts, that the joints do not move normally, or that the muscles are genuinely weak. An examination separates these possibilities. Objective weakness, neurological deficits or a substantially raised creatine kinase can direct attention towards muscle or nerve disease. Later in treatment, deconditioning or steroid-related muscle weakness can coexist with controlled PMR. A familiar diagnosis must not become the explanation for every new symptom. [2][6][23]

02 / Causes and uncertainty

We know part of the machinery.
We do not know the first domino.

PMR is an immune-mediated inflammatory disease. That is a useful description of what is happening—not a complete explanation of why one particular person develops it. No single infection, food, gene or life event has been established as its universal cause. [1][11]

Established pattern

Older age, characteristic regional inflammation and a systemic inflammatory response are central observations. Treatment directed at immune signalling can reduce disease activity. [1][12]

Supported contributors

Genetic association studies implicate immune-related regions, including HLA and the ANKRD55–IL6ST region. These are susceptibility signals, not a genetic diagnosis or a prediction that relatives will become ill. [11]

Still unresolved

The initiating trigger, the reason inflammation favours particular tissues, and the boundary between PMR and related conditions remain incompletely explained. Observations after an infection do not by themselves establish causation. [1][11]

Think of susceptibility as a landscape, not a switch

Several small influences can make an inflammatory state more likely without determining it. A genetic association tells us that a biological neighbourhood deserves investigation. It does not identify the exact causal variant, prove which cell matters, or show that altering a related lifestyle factor will treat established disease. This distinction is especially important when interpreting Mendelian-randomisation work: it is evidence about potential causal pathways under assumptions, not a randomised treatment experiment. [11]

“Autoimmune” is sometimes used as shorthand. A more cautious phrase is immune-mediated: PMR does not have a single established disease-defining autoantibody comparable to some other rheumatic conditions. Research implicates both innate inflammatory responses and adaptive immune pathways. Successful blockade of one cytokine shows that pathway is therapeutically useful; it does not show that it is the only pathway involved. [28][19]

Association is not blame. A person did not “cause their PMR” by failing to exercise enough, eating the wrong breakfast or being stressed. Lifestyle can support health during recovery without being a demonstrated explanation for the disease or a substitute for anti-inflammatory treatment. [1][6]

03 / From a molecule to a morning

An inflammatory signal can have effects far from its source.

Immune cells communicate using molecules called cytokines. Interleukin-6, or IL-6, is one important participant in PMR. It can act through membrane-bound or soluble IL-6 receptors and a signalling partner called gp130. Intracellular signalling, including JAK–STAT pathways, changes which genes a responding cell expresses. The liver can then make acute-phase proteins, including C-reactive protein (CRP). [13][28]

CRP and ESR are instruments, not verdicts

CRP is a concentration of a particular protein. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) is a laboratory measure of how quickly red blood cells settle; it is influenced by plasma proteins and other factors. Both can support the presence of inflammation. Neither identifies its cause, and neither is a direct meter of how much a shoulder hurts. Infection and other diseases may also raise them. [2]

Turning down a warning signal is not the same as proving the whole system is safe.

This becomes particularly important with IL-6 receptor inhibitors. They directly suppress the acute-phase response. A low CRP during such treatment must therefore be interpreted alongside symptoms, examination and the clinical context—not used alone to exclude active disease or infection. [6][13]

Go deeper · Why two effective medicines need not do the same thing

Glucocorticoids bind an intracellular receptor and alter broad programmes of gene regulation. IL-6 receptor antibodies interrupt a much more specific extracellular signalling step. Methotrexate has slower immunomodulatory effects rather than acting as an immediate painkiller. These differences explain why onset, monitoring, interactions and failure modes differ; “steroid-sparing” describes a useful clinical outcome, not a single molecular mechanism. [3][13][25][29]

04 / A disease with a daily rhythm

Why does getting up feel worse than staying up?

The immune system and the hormone system both have daily rhythms. In a small controlled study of untreated PMR, pain and stiffness were greatest between roughly 04:00 and 08:00 and lowest around 16:00. Cytokines and cortisol also varied during the day. This is evidence for a rhythm, not a timetable every patient must follow. [10]

It is tempting to reduce this to “too little cortisol”. The study actually found higher cortisol concentrations in PMR than in controls. Timing, inflammatory demand and hormone responsiveness are more complicated than a simple deficiency story. Likewise, discomfort after inactivity does not mean that joints have physically “rusted overnight”. It is an inflammatory and functional phenomenon. [10]

Morning stiffness that lasts more than 45 minutes is one useful feature in research classification. It is neither a magic threshold nor proof of PMR: 44 minutes does not rule it out, and 46 minutes does not rule it in. [8]

05 / Clinical reasoning, not a single test

A diagnosis is assembled.
It is not read from one blood result.

Clinicians combine the symptom pattern, examination, inflammatory markers and the absence of a better explanation. A convincing diagnosis also includes a plan to revisit it if the course stops fitting. [2]

What the work-up may include

CRP and ESR assess inflammatory activity. A blood count, kidney and liver tests help look for alternatives and establish a treatment baseline. Depending on the presentation, clinicians may check creatine kinase, thyroid function, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies. Testing is directed by the history and examination rather than a universal shopping list. Normal inflammatory markers make typical active PMR less likely, but a number alone should not overrule a concerning clinical assessment. [2][7]

Ultrasound can reveal bursitis, tendon-sheath inflammation and joint inflammation. MRI can show deeper pelvic or extracapsular patterns. PET imaging can sometimes help specialists investigate difficult cases, occult large-vessel inflammation or alternative explanations. These tests have different costs, availability and limitations. Steroids can reduce imaging signs of inflammation, so testing strategy and timing should be planned—but suspected GCA is a different urgency and treatment must not be delayed. [6][9][5]

Go deeper · Classification criteria are not diagnostic criteria

The 2012 EULAR/ACR criteria were developed to classify research participants in a prospective cohort. They combine an entry pattern with features such as prolonged morning stiffness, hip involvement, negative rheumatoid serology and optional ultrasound. Their purpose was to create more comparable study groups, not to replace individual clinical judgement. A person can have clinically diagnosed PMR without satisfying a research score, or satisfy a score while another condition remains the better explanation. [8]

Go deeper · Why a dramatic steroid response is not proof

Improvement after prednisolone can strongly support the working diagnosis, but steroids suppress inflammation in many disorders. They can also temporarily obscure important alternatives. Poor or incomplete response should prompt reassessment of dose, adherence, coexisting pain, the diagnosis and possible GCA—not an automatic assumption that ever more steroid is the answer. [3][7]

06 / A related disease, a different danger

PMR hurts around joints.
GCA can threaten blood supply.

Giant-cell arteritis (GCA) is inflammation of arteries. It overlaps with PMR, but neither diagnosis automatically implies the other. Inflamed arterial walls can compromise blood reaching vulnerable tissues, including structures involved in vision. That is why a new headache or visual symptom changes the urgency of the conversation. [4][5]

Do not wait for the perfect test

New visual loss or double vision: emergency assessment now. New headache, scalp tenderness or pain in the jaw with chewing: urgent same-day assessment. A normal blood result, an existing PMR diagnosis or already taking a modest steroid dose does not make these symptoms safe to ignore. Clinicians may start treatment for strongly suspected GCA before confirmatory ultrasound or biopsy because delaying treatment can risk irreversible harm. [4][5]

GCA is not managed by simply continuing a routine PMR taper. It has its own diagnostic pathway, substantially different initial steroid treatment and specialist decisions about additional therapy. This guide deliberately does not provide an emergency self-dosing instruction. [5]

07 / Two clocks, not one countdown

First control inflammation.
Then earn each reduction.

The aim is sustained disease control with the least treatment harm—not merely a normal CRP, and not reaching zero tablets by an arbitrary anniversary. Decisions combine symptoms, function, examination, blood tests and side effects. [3][7]

Which guidance is current?

The recommendations named “2025 EULAR recommendations” were published online on 21 July 2026. Their public abstract gives an initial PMR range of 15–25 mg/day prednisone-equivalent, individualised by the treating clinician. This is not an instruction to start treatment. Older 2015 EULAR/ACR guidance used a 12.5–25 mg range, and local protocols may differ. Prednisone-equivalent is a way of comparing glucocorticoid doses; in UK practice the prescribed medicine is commonly prednisolone. [5][7][3]

Symptoms may improve quickly while the tendency to relapse persists. Reducing treatment too quickly can uncover ongoing disease; continuing more treatment than necessary increases cumulative exposure. A taper is therefore an adaptive clinical process with agreed review points and a plan for troublesome symptoms. Guidelines offer starting frameworks, but the evidence does not justify a universal calendar that every patient should follow. [6][7]

Three reasons someone may feel worse during reduction

Relapse means inflammatory disease has become active again. Glucocorticoid withdrawal symptoms can include aching and fatigue during reduction without necessarily demonstrating recurrent PMR. Adrenal insufficiency means the body cannot produce enough cortisol for its needs. The symptoms overlap, and unrelated illness or mechanical pain adds further possibilities. Sorting them out may require assessment and sometimes endocrine testing, not guessing from the tablet count. [21][6]

Never abruptly stop prolonged steroid treatment. Ask for a written taper, a clear route to contact the team if symptoms recur, and a personalised plan for intercurrent illness. Do not repeatedly adjust the dose yourself unless following an explicit plan from your clinician. [3][21]

08 / Different tools, different trade-offs

“Steroid-sparing” is a goal.
It is not a promise of no side effects.

Medication selection considers how convincingly PMR has been established, the number of relapses, accumulated steroid exposure, other illnesses, infection risk, monitoring capacity and the person's preferences. The following explains the roles of medicines; it is not a prescribing menu. [5][7]

Prednisolone / prednisone: broad and often rapid

Glucocorticoids are central to initial treatment. Their receptor-mediated effects suppress many inflammatory processes at once. They can restore function quickly, but the same broad reach affects metabolism, bone, immunity, sleep, mood and the body's cortisol regulation. “Low dose” means lower exposure, not zero risk. Total exposure and individual susceptibility both matter. [3][23][29]

Methotrexate: a slower steroid-sparing option

Methotrexate is used in selected patients to improve control and reduce glucocorticoid requirements. It works over weeks rather than providing immediate relief. Evidence from older low-dose trials was mixed; a newer randomised study using 25 mg/week in recently diagnosed PMR reported a clearer benefit. That study dose is not automatically the right dose for an older person with kidney disease, interacting medicines or other vulnerabilities. [18][7]

For inflammatory disease, methotrexate is normally taken once weekly, not daily. Accidental daily dosing has caused fatal toxicity. The prescription needs an unambiguous weekly day, monitoring of blood counts and liver/kidney function, and the clinician's folic-acid instructions. A medication review matters before new prescriptions or over-the-counter medicines. Fever, unusual bruising, significant mouth ulcers or new breathlessness warrant prompt medical advice. [24][25]

Sarilumab: targeting the IL-6 receptor

The UK product information includes adults with PMR who have responded inadequately to corticosteroids or relapsed during tapering. Its labelled regimen is a specialist-supervised subcutaneous injection every two weeks alongside a steroid taper, with possible continuation alone. Its advantages must be weighed against infection risk and laboratory abnormalities; screening and monitoring include blood counts, liver enzymes, lipids and tuberculosis assessment. Prior diverticulitis is relevant because intestinal perforation is a rare concern. [13]

A licence is not the same as local NHS funding. Sarilumab's UK PMR indication is documented in the SmPC. Scottish Medicines Consortium advice SMC2810, dated 12 May 2025, is “not recommended” following a manufacturer non-submission. This is not a completed negative efficacy appraisal. That published Scottish position remained the one found on 15 September 2026; individual access and other UK pathways require a current local check. [13][14]

Tocilizumab: the same receptor family, distinct evidence and access

Tocilizumab also inhibits IL-6 receptor signalling. PMR-specific randomised evidence includes the small PMR-SPARE trial, described below. Do not transfer an indication or funding decision from GCA to isolated PMR, or assume that two drugs sharing a target are interchangeable in every patient. Specialist decisions need the exact indication, current local policy and the relevant product safety information. [16][5]

The frontier: IL-17 and intracellular signalling

The 2026 REPLENISH trial investigated the IL-17A antibody secukinumab in relapsing PMR. It is important new phase-3 evidence, not a reason to assume routine PMR availability from a trial result alone. Early randomised work has also investigated the JAK inhibitor baricitinib. Different targets broaden what we can learn about the disease, but efficacy, long-term safety, regulatory status and funding are separate questions. [19][20]

Go deeper · Why “newest” is not a treatment-selection rule

A trial may enrol relapsing patients rather than newly diagnosed patients, exclude substantial comorbidity, use an accelerated steroid taper or follow people for only a year. Its result answers that particular question. A clinician must then decide how much of the result transfers to a different person. The most appropriate treatment can be an older, well-monitored option rather than the most molecularly specific one.

09 / Read the experiment, not just the percentage

Four trials.
Four different questions.

The unit of comparison here is within a trial. Changing the population, endpoint, follow-up or steroid taper changes the experiment. The bars do not rank these medicines against each other.

Three habits that make a trial easier to read

Ask who was studied. New-onset, relapsing and steroid-dependent disease are not interchangeable. Ask what counted as success. Low disease activity, being off steroids at one visit and sustained remission over many months differ. Ask what came with the drug. A taper is part of the intervention, not background decoration.

A relative improvement can sound very large when the comparator event rate is small. The absolute difference and its uncertainty are often more useful. Small trials can reveal a real treatment signal while remaining poor guides to uncommon adverse effects. A non-significant safety difference does not prove two treatments equally safe. These are general principles of interpreting experiments, not special criticisms of any one PMR study.

10 / Treat the disease; protect the person

Monitoring is part of treatment.
Not an optional extra.

The benefit of controlling inflammation is substantial. The task is to preserve that benefit while actively managing the predictable costs of treatment. The monitoring plan should evolve as medicines, doses and health circumstances change. [7]

Bone and falls

Glucocorticoids can increase fracture risk. Assessment may include fracture history, falls risk, FRAX and bone-density measurement, with preventive medication where indicated. Calcium and vitamin D adequacy matter, but supplements alone are not an adequate response to high fracture risk. [22]

Metabolism and circulation

Review blood pressure, glucose, weight and existing cardiovascular risks. Steroids can worsen glucose control; IL-6 inhibitors can alter lipid measurements. A “normal” starting result does not remove the need for follow-up. [23][13]

Infection and vaccination

Discuss vaccination and infection precautions before immunosuppressive treatment where possible. Live-vaccine decisions depend on the medicine and dose. Fever, new cough, breathlessness or significant abdominal pain needs clinical attention; muted inflammatory markers can be misleading. [13][23]

Eyes, sleep, mood and strength

Longer steroid exposure can affect cataract/glaucoma risk, sleep, mood and muscle strength. Ask about these explicitly rather than assuming they are inevitable features of ageing or PMR. Severe mood or behavioural changes require prompt advice. [23]

Bone protection is a risk decision

Fracture prevention depends on age, prior fractures, steroid exposure and other clinical factors, not just whether a scan is labelled “osteoporosis”. NOGG guidance provides a framework for clinicians, including when bone-protective treatment should begin promptly. Some people need an anti-resorptive medicine such as a bisphosphonate; others need a different approach because of kidney function, oesophageal problems or other factors. This is an individual prescribing decision. [22]

Adrenal safety deserves its own conversation

Prolonged exposure to glucocorticoids can suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. Susceptibility and recovery vary. Ask whether you need a steroid emergency card and obtain a personalised sick-day plan, including what to do during significant illness, surgery or vomiting. Carrying a card is useful only when its implications are understood. [21]

Vomiting, collapse or severe illness while steroid-dependent

If you cannot keep steroid medication down, or develop marked weakness with fainting, confusion or collapse, seek urgent emergency advice and tell the team about current or recent steroid treatment. Follow an existing clinician-issued emergency plan; this page cannot calculate stress doses or determine adrenal reserve. [21]

Go deeper · Why “more pain” should not automatically mean “more steroid”

Mechanical shoulder problems, spinal disease, osteoarthritis, neuropathy, deconditioning and withdrawal symptoms can continue after inflammatory activity is controlled. Steroids can also cause weakness. Escalation without reassessment may increase harm without addressing the source of the symptom. Conversely, dismissing a true relapse as withdrawal can leave disease undertreated. Review is how those errors are separated. [2][21][23]

11 / Recovery is a distribution

Many people recover.
Not everyone follows a two-year script.

Rapid improvement is possible; durable remission may take much longer. Relapses are common and are not a personal failure. Historical real-world cohorts show substantial numbers still receiving glucocorticoids several years after diagnosis. Treatment duration is not identical to the duration of active disease. [26]

The same review estimated that 43% experienced at least one relapse during the first year. Estimates vary with the population, definition and treatment strategy, and consistent individual predictors remain elusive. A population average should guide expectations, not create a deadline or a prediction of failure. [26]

What about life expectancy?

A population-based Norwegian cohort followed for up to 38 years did not find increased overall mortality associated with PMR compared with matched controls. That is reassuring, but it does not mean the disease or its treatment is harmless, and it cannot resolve prognosis for every population or person with coexisting GCA and other illnesses. Pain, function, treatment toxicity and quality of life still matter greatly. [27]

Rebuilding function is its own project

Once inflammation is being treated, suitable movement can help reverse the loss of confidence and conditioning that accompanies painful inactivity. Exercise should match symptoms, frailty, balance and fracture risk; physiotherapy can help adapt it. A bad day is not automatically damage, and a good day is not a reason to make up months of lost activity in one afternoon. [6]

Keep the goals concrete: dressing more easily, walking a manageable route, sleeping better, rising from a chair and returning to valued activities. Balanced nutrition and adequate protein support general recovery; no special “anti-inflammatory” diet has been established here as a replacement for PMR treatment. Bring changes in function to appointments alongside pain and stiffness, not just laboratory numbers. [3][6]

Control is not the same as cure. Remission can mean little or no detectable disease activity while on treatment; glucocorticoid-free remission is a more specific outcome. State which one is meant. “Off steroids” is important, but being well, mobile and safely monitored is the larger goal.

Use this as a question list, not a self-management protocol. Tick questions to focus the conversation. The page does not save or transmit your selections.

Useful observations to bring

Describe the distribution and timing of pain, the length of morning stiffness, activities that have become difficult, new symptoms, medication changes and side effects. Bring an accurate medicine list. A brief factual record is more useful than trying to infer an inflammatory-marker result from how a single morning felt.

Avoid three common misunderstandings

“My CRP is normal, so everything is fine.” A biomarker is one part of assessment, especially on IL-6 blockade. “I felt better after two days, so the disease has gone.” Symptom suppression is not proof of durable remission. “A relapse means I have failed.” Relapse is an observed feature of the disease course; it is a reason to reassess the plan, not blame the person. Appendix A / Translate the jargon

A small working vocabulary.

Bursa / bursitis
A small fluid-lined gliding cushion / inflammation of that cushion.
Synovium / synovitis
The lining of certain joints and related structures / inflammation of that lining.
Periarticular / extracapsular
Around a joint / outside its capsule.
Cytokine
A signalling protein used by cells, including immune cells.
IL-6 / IL-17A
Two distinct cytokines investigated as treatment targets in PMR.
CRP / ESR
Two different laboratory measures that can support assessment of inflammation, but do not identify its cause.
Glucocorticoid
A steroid medicine, such as prednisolone, with anti-inflammatory and wider hormonal effects.
Steroid-sparing
Reducing the glucocorticoid treatment needed to maintain disease control.
HPA axis
The hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal signalling system regulating cortisol production.
Relapse / remission
Return of disease activity / a state of low or absent activity under a specified definition.
Absolute difference
The subtraction of two outcome rates. 28% minus 10% is 18 percentage points.
Composite endpoint
A study outcome requiring several components. Its definition matters as much as its headline percentage.
