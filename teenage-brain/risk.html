Chapter 4

Risk & Reward

Ask why teenagers do dangerous things and the honest first answer is: because it feels fantastic. The teenage brain's reward system doesn't just work — it works harder than it ever will again, before or after. That extra intensity is not a defect to be medicated away. It is the engine of everything a young person is supposed to do at that age: explore, seek novelty, take the leap, and leave the nest.

The neurochemical at the centre of this is dopamine — not the "pleasure molecule" of pop science, but the brain's signal for wanting, motivation, and "that was better than expected, do it again."

In 2006 Adriana Galván and colleagues put children, teenagers and adults in a scanner and gave them rewards. When a reward arrived, one region lit up far brighter in the teenagers than in either the children or the adults: the ventral striatum (its core is the nucleus accumbens), the hub of the brain's reward circuit. The adolescent reward response was, quite literally, turned up — an inverted-U across the lifespan that peaks in the teenage years.

Put that beside Chapter 1 and the picture sharpens. The reward hub matures early; the prefrontal cortex that would temper it matures late. So for a few years the brain feels rewards with an adult's intensity — arguably more than an adult's — while weighing consequences with a system that isn't finished. Sweep the age below and watch the reward response rise and fall.

§4.1 Why evolution built it this way

It's tempting to see all this as a design fault. It isn't — and the clearest way to see why is to ask what adolescence is for. Across social species, adolescence is when the young must leave the safety of home, find their own food, territory and mates, and learn to survive without their parents. That is a fundamentally risky assignment, and a brain optimised for caution would fail it. Heightened reward-sensitivity and novelty-seeking are the push that gets a young animal out of the den and into the world.

~200% the often-cited rise in morbidity and mortality from childhood to late adolescence — despite these being physically the healthiest years of life. Almost none of it is disease; the danger is overwhelmingly behavioural, the price of the same drive to explore that makes the years so formative (Dahl, 2004).

This reframes "risk-taking" itself. We notice the reckless version — the speeding, the substances — but the same reward drive powers the good risks that define a life: auditioning, asking someone out, moving to a new city, standing up in class, trying the thing you might fail at. A teenager willing to risk humiliation for reward is not malfunctioning. They are running exactly the program that turns a dependent child into an independent adult.

Dash, a spark of dopamine

"People call me trouble. Rude. I'm the reason you'll ever try anything new — the first song, the first stage, the first time you left home. I burn brightest in the teenage years because that's when you most need a reason to be brave. Aim me well."

§4.2 The catch: a system this plastic is easy to hijack

There is a shadow side, and it deserves plain statement. A reward system this responsive and this plastic is also unusually easy to capture. Drugs, alcohol, nicotine and the engineered rewards of gambling and some apps all work by hijacking exactly this dopamine circuitry — and they get a bigger grip on a system that is both more reactive and still being wired. That is why a substance sampled at fifteen can hook far harder than the same substance sampled at twenty-five, a fact we return to in Chapter 6.

Two true things at once

The teenage reward system is both the source of adolescence's glorious appetite for life and its greatest vulnerability. The same sensitivity that makes a first success feel incandescent makes a first hit of an addictive drug bite deeper. You cannot keep one without the other; they are the same circuit. The task of the adults around a teenager is not to dull the engine but to steer it toward rewards worth having.
