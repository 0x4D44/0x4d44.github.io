Chapter 7

The Lexicon

Everything in one place: the working vocabulary A–Z, a short history of how we learned all this, a table of the real numbers, and the primary sources — the actual papers — behind every claim in the guide. If you want to check our working, this is where the receipts live.

§7.1 The vocabulary, A–Z

Amygdala "almond," pl. amygdalae
A pair of almond-shaped structures deep in the temporal lobes; the brain's rapid emotional and threat-detection hub. Early-maturing and rich in sex-hormone receptors, which is why emotion runs hot in adolescence before the prefrontal cortex can regulate it.
Axon
The long output fibre of a neuron, carrying signals to other cells. Wrapping it in myelin is what speeds the signal up during adolescence.
Cognitive control executive function
The prefrontal-cortex functions of planning, holding back impulses, weighing consequences and directing attention. The "brakes" of the dual-systems model; matures last.
Crystallised intelligence
Accumulated knowledge, vocabulary and expertise. Unlike fluid intelligence it keeps rising for decades, well past adolescence.
Dendritic spine
A tiny protrusion on a neuron where a synapse forms. Spine density in childhood runs two to three times adult levels; the excess is pruned through the teens.
Dopamine
A neurotransmitter central to motivation, wanting and reward-learning ("that was better than expected — do it again"). Its signalling peaks in adolescence, underwriting both the teenage appetite for reward and its vulnerability to addiction.
Dual-systems model maturational imbalance model
The leading framework for teenage risk-taking: an early-maturing reward/socio-emotional system paired with a late-maturing cognitive-control system, with the gap between them peaking in mid-adolescence.
Fluid intelligence
On-the-spot reasoning, pattern-finding and working memory — the "raw processing" side of intelligence. Near its lifetime peak in the late teens and early twenties.
Grey matter
The neuron cell bodies and synapses — the brain's "processors." Its volume follows an inverted-U through childhood and adolescence, rising then falling as connections are pruned.
Hebbian plasticity "fire together, wire together"
Donald Hebb's 1949 principle that connections which are used together are strengthened, and unused ones weaken. The selection rule behind synaptic pruning.
HPG axis hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal
The hormonal loop that drives puberty: hypothalamus → pituitary → gonads, producing testosterone and oestradiol, which in turn remodel hormone-sensitive brain circuits.
Melatonin & circadian phase delay
Melatonin is the hormone that signals "time to sleep." At puberty its nightly release shifts one to three hours later, pushing the whole sleep–wake clock back — the teenage "night owl" shift, and the reason early school starts hurt.
Metacognition
Thinking about one's own thinking — monitoring, planning and reflecting on mental processes. Emerges strongly in adolescence.
Myelin & myelination
Myelin is the fatty insulation wrapped around axons by glial cells; myelination is the process of laying it down. It raises signal speed from around 1 m/s to as much as 100 m/s and continues through the teens into the thirties, building the brain's fast long-range network (white matter).
Nucleus accumbens core of the ventral striatum
A key node of the reward circuit whose response to reward is exaggerated in the teenage brain relative to children and adults.
Prefrontal cortex PFC
The cortex just behind the forehead; seat of planning, judgement, impulse control and abstract reasoning. The last region to mature, not structurally complete until the mid-twenties.
Pruning synaptic pruning
The removal of unused synaptic connections — up to ~40% in some regions — that sculpts a sharper, more efficient adult brain from an over-connected child's one. Carried out physically by immune cells called microglia.
Sensitive period
A window when the brain is unusually shaped by experience. Long known for infancy; adolescence is now recognised as a second such window for higher cognition and social learning.
Synapse
The junction where one neuron signals another — the brain's basic connection. Adolescence is largely about editing the vast childhood surplus of these.
Ventral striatum
A reward-processing region (containing the nucleus accumbens) that lights up more strongly to reward in adolescents — the neural signature of heightened teenage reward sensitivity.
White matter
The myelinated axon tracts connecting brain regions — the "network cabling." Its volume rises steadily through adolescence and into adulthood as myelination proceeds.

§7.2 How we learned all this

A short timeline of adolescent brain science
YearMilestone
1904G. Stanley Hall's Adolescence casts the teenage years as inevitable "storm and stress" — hormones, no brain science.
1949Donald Hebb states "fire together, wire together" — the rule that will later explain pruning.
1979–97Peter Huttenlocher counts synapses in human cortex post-mortem, revealing childhood overproduction and later pruning.
1982Irwin Feinberg proposes that schizophrenia may stem from adolescent synaptic pruning gone too far.
1999Jay Giedd's team publishes the first longitudinal MRI study — the teenage brain is caught mid-rebuild.
2000Linda Spear reframes adolescent behaviour as adaptive, not defective.
2004Nitin Gogtay turns repeat scans into a time-lapse: the cortex matures back-to-front.
2005–06Gardner & Steinberg quantify the peer effect; Galván finds the teenage reward system runs hot.
2008The dual-systems / imbalance model (Steinberg; Casey) and Blakemore's "social brain in adolescence" are laid out.
2011Ramsden et al. show teenage IQ — and the brain tissue behind it — can still rise or fall.
2015Fuhrmann, Knoll & Blakemore make the case for adolescence as a second sensitive period.
2022Solmi et al. pool 192 studies: peak age of mental-illness onset is 14.5.

A note on the interactives

The live diagrams in this guide are schematic: they faithfully reproduce the shape of published findings — the inverted-U of reward-seeking, the back-to-front maturation wave, the cumulative onset curve through its published anchor points — rather than re-plotting raw study data. They are built to convey the pattern honestly, not to stand in for the papers, which are linked above.
