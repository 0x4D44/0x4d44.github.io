Chapter 6

When Storms Hit

Everything that makes the teenage brain wonderful — its plasticity, its intensity, its openness to the world — is the same thing that makes it vulnerable. A system this open to being shaped can be shaped the wrong way. This is the hard chapter: why so much lifelong mental illness begins in adolescence, why an addictive drug bites harder at fifteen, and why the teenage clock is at war with the school bell. None of it is destiny.

Adolescence is not a disorder, and the overwhelming majority of teenagers come through it well. But it is, unmistakably, the age when the mind is most likely to first run into trouble.

§6.1 The most important graph in adolescent psychiatry

In 2022 Marco Solmi and colleagues pooled 192 studies covering more than 700,000 people to ask a simple question: when, across a whole life, do mental-health conditions first begin? The answer is stark. The single most common age of onset — the peak — is 14.5 years. By age 18, roughly half of all the mental-health conditions a person will ever experience have already made their first appearance. Drag the age across the curve and read it off.

Why should one narrow window carry so much? Because it is the same window this whole guide has been describing. A brain in the middle of its largest rebuild, running on an off-balance pair of systems (Chapter 2), flooded with hormones (Chapter 3), maximally sensitive to reward and to stress (Chapters 4–5), navigating the highest-stakes social world of a lifetime — that is a system with many ways to be knocked off course. Some conditions are, in part, the rebuild itself going slightly awry: one leading hypothesis links schizophrenia, which often first appears in the late teens and early twenties, to synaptic pruning that goes too far.

Read this the right way

This is not "adolescence causes mental illness," and it is certainly not a reason to treat every struggling teenager as a patient. It means two useful things. First, most first episodes are treatable, and catching them early — during the plastic window — changes outcomes dramatically. Second, ordinary adolescent turbulence and an emerging disorder can look alike from outside, so persistent, function-wrecking distress deserves to be taken seriously rather than waved off as "just a phase."

§6.2 Why fifteen is a dangerous age to start

Chapter 4 showed a reward system at its most sensitive and most plastic. Now the cost. Every major addictive substance works by hijacking that same dopamine circuitry — and it gets a firmer grip on a system that is both more reactive and still being wired in. The epidemiology is blunt: people who begin drinking in early adolescence are several times more likely to develop alcohol dependence than those who wait until they are adults. The drug is the same. The brain it lands in is not.

~4× the roughly four-fold higher rate of later alcohol dependence among people who begin drinking before age 15 compared with those who wait until 21 — a signature of the teenage reward system's heightened vulnerability to being captured (Grant & Dawson, 1997).

§6.3 The clock and the school bell

One of the cruellest mismatches in modern life is invisible and purely biological. At puberty the brain's internal clock shifts later. The sleep hormone melatonin, which tells the body it's time to wind down, starts being released one to three hours later than it did in childhood — so a teenager genuinely cannot fall asleep at nine, no matter how early they went to bed. Their biological night has moved. And then a 7 a.m. alarm for an early school start drags them out of bed in what is, for their body, the dead of night. Drag the age and watch the sleep window drift straight into the school day.

The hopeful part

Vulnerability and plasticity are the same coin. A brain this open to harm is equally open to help: good relationships, sleep, exercise, treatment and time land with outsized effect in adolescence precisely because the tissue is still being shaped. The teenage brain that can be knocked off course by a bad year can be set right by a good one. That is not wishful thinking; it is the direct corollary of everything in Chapter 5.

Melo, the sleep signal

"I show up three hours later than I used to — that's just the schedule now, not defiance. Wake them at six and you're fighting my chemistry, not their character. Let the clock be, protect the sleep, and watch how much of the 'moody teenager' quietly disappears."

If you take one thing from this chapter, take this: the fragility is real, but it is the price of an openness that is mostly a gift. Knowing where the storms come from is the first step to weathering them — and to being, for the teenager in your life or the one you once were, the good year rather than the bad one.

If you or a young person you know is struggling, this guide is not a substitute for real help — talk to a GP, a school counsellor, or a local mental-health service. In an emergency, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line.
