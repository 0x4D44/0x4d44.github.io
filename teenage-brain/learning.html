Chapter 5

The Learning Window

We are used to thinking of the "critical period" for learning as babyhood — the time to soak up a first language, to wire up vision. But the teenage brain has a superpower that is only now being properly measured: a second window of heightened plasticity. Adolescence is not just something to get through on the way to a capable adult brain. It may be the best time, after infancy, to become one.

The very features that make adolescence turbulent — a brain mid-rebuild, hungry for stimulation, exquisitely sensitive to experience — are the features that make it a learning machine.

In 2015 Delia Fuhrmann, Lisa Knoll and Sarah-Jayne Blakemore gathered the evidence that adolescence functions as a sensitive period — a stretch when the brain is unusually shaped by experience. Their argument: the same plasticity that lets a teenager learn a language, an instrument or a sport with startling speed also makes them unusually susceptible — to social stress, to the effects of drugs, to whatever environment they are placed in. Heightened plasticity is a double-edged gift: more upside and more downside, for the same reason.

§5.1 Two windows, not one

Below is plasticity across a lifetime. The towering first window is infancy. But notice the second rise in the teenage years — smaller than infancy, but far above the adult baseline, and tuned to different things: not basic vision or a first language, but higher cognition, memory, social understanding and identity. Pick a domain and drag the age.

§5.2 The mind gets faster — and knows itself

Alongside the plasticity, raw cognitive horsepower climbs steeply. Three gains stand out:

  • Processing speed — how fast the brain moves information — rises through the teens to a lifetime peak in the late teens and early twenties, powered by all that myelination from Chapter 1.
  • Abstract reasoning. Around early adolescence, thinking becomes able to handle the hypothetical, the counterfactual, the systematic — Piaget's "formal operations." A teenager can suddenly argue politics, spot logical contradictions, and imagine how things could be, not just how they are.
  • Metacognition — thinking about thinking. Adolescents develop the ability to monitor their own reasoning, plan strategies and reflect on their minds, a skill that keeps sharpening into adulthood.

Psychologists split intelligence into two kinds, and they move differently. Fluid intelligence — on-the-spot reasoning, pattern-finding, working memory — rises fast in adolescence and peaks early. Crystallised intelligence — accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, expertise — keeps growing for decades. The teenage years are where fluid intelligence is near its lifetime maximum: the best raw hardware you will ever have for learning something genuinely new and hard.

IQ is not carved in stone

A 2011 study by Sue Ramsden and colleagues, published in Nature, scanned teenagers' brains and tested their IQ, then did it again a few years later. Both verbal and non-verbal IQ rose in some and fell in others by as much as a full standard grade — and the changes tracked physical changes in the corresponding brain regions. The lesson is not that IQ is meaningless but that in the teenage years it is still moving, and what a teenager does with those years is written into the tissue.

§5.3 What you build now, you keep

Put the two halves of this chapter together — heightened plasticity, plus pruning that makes whatever is practised permanent — and you get the single most actionable idea in adolescent neuroscience. The teenage brain is not just able to learn fast; it is deciding what to keep forever. Skills, languages, habits, ways of thinking and even the shape of a personality are being wired in and locked down. This is the biology behind the old intuition that the teens are formative. They are formative in the most literal sense: the form is being set.

Neura, the neuron

"Here's the deal nobody tells you: right now I'm cheap to rewire and I make things permanent. Practise the guitar, the language, the kindness, the courage — I'll keep it. Practise the doomscroll, I'll keep that too. I don't judge. I just build what you use."
