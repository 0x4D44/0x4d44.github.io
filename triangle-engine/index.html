The Triangle
Engine

Every three-dimensional picture you have ever seen on a screen was made by flattening a pile of triangles and arguing, pixel by pixel, about which one you can see. This is how that argument is settled — and how, in six decades, the machinery that settles it went from one room-filling computer at Lincoln Laboratory to a chip that answers the question ten billion times a second.

Nothing here is a video. Every triangle on this page is transformed, clipped, divided by w and rasterized by a software renderer written for it, running in your browser as you scroll.

01 · Lineage

The pipeline never changed.
Only who does the work.

The sequence — transform, clip, divide, rasterize, shade, test depth, blend — was settled by about 1974 and has not been rearranged since. What moved is where each stage runs: out of software onto wired silicon, and then out of wired silicon into programs you write. Scrub the timeline and watch each row of the stage map change hands.

    03 · Transform

    Perspective is a division.
    Everything else is bookkeeping.

    Three dimensions become two by one arithmetic trick: give every point a fourth coordinate w, arrange for w to end up holding the distance from the eye, then divide. Distant things get small because you divided by a bigger number. The 4x4 matrix does not create perspective — it only sets up the division.

    Try

    • Field of view 110°, distance 2. Then 25° and 12. Same cube.
    • Tick the dolly zoom and move either slider on its own.
    • Switch to orthographic and watch the w column stop changing.

    The lens

    With this on, field of view and camera distance move together so the cube stays the same size on screen. Everything about its shape still changes — because both sliders only ever moved the divisor.

    04 · Rasterize

    Three questions,
    asked of every pixel.

    Filling a triangle looks like it should need geometry. It does not. For each of the three edges, write down a linear function that is positive on the inside and negative on the outside. A pixel is covered when all three are positive. That is the whole algorithm — Juan Pineda published it in 1988 — and because each pixel's answer is independent of every other, hardware can ask thousands of them at once.

    Try

    • Drop the resolution to 8 and count the covered cells by eye.
    • Switch to the edge functions: three lines cut the plane into seven regions.
    • Drag a corner past the opposite edge to reverse the winding.
    Move a corner

    Why the sign of the area is the winding, and the winding is free culling

    The same edge function, evaluated at the third corner, is twice the triangle's signed area. Reverse the order of the corners and the sign flips. So the test that tells you whether a triangle faces you is already computed by the time you have set up its edges — which is why backface culling is a checkbox rather than a cost. In a closed model it discards about half the triangles before a single pixel is considered.

    05 · Depth

    Sorting is not enough.
    Ask each pixel instead.

    The obvious way to draw a solid scene is to sort the polygons back to front and paint over. It fails, and not in a subtle way: three triangles can overlap in a cycle that has no correct order, and two that pass through each other have no single answer at all. Catmull's 1974 fix was to stop sorting objects and start asking pixels — keep one depth per pixel, and let each fragment argue for itself.

    Try

    • Read the two pictures against each other. Only one is right.
    • Pick the sign scene at 16-bit, then drag the near plane outwards.
    • Try the cyclic scene: no order of three objects can paint it.

    The same buffer, pointed somewhere else

    Once you have a buffer that answers "what is the nearest surface in this direction", you can point it at something other than the camera. Render the scene from the light's position, keep only the depths, and you have a record of what the light can reach. Then, shading each pixel, transform it into that view and compare: if it is further away than the recorded depth, something else got there first, and the pixel is in shadow. That is shadow mapping — Lance Williams, 1978 — and it is the same idea as the z-buffer with the camera moved. Every hard-edged shadow you have seen in a game is a depth buffer being read back.

    06 · Texture

    The floor that swims,
    and the floor that shimmers.

    This is section 03's division coming back to collect. Two artefacts define an era each. The first: interpolating texture coordinates straight across the screen, when perspective says you must divide by w — the warping, sliding floors of 1994. The second: sampling a texture more finely than the pixels can carry, which turns a distant checkerboard into a boiling mess. Both are fixed below, and you can switch each fix off.

    Try

    • Turn perspective correction off, then raise the sub-division slider.
    • Set minification to None and tick 'creep the camera forward'.
    • Show the chosen level as colour, and watch the bands march away.

    Aliasing is a motion artefact: it is the picture changing far more than the camera did. Switch minification off, then turn this on, and watch the distance boil. Nothing in the scene is moving more than a fraction of a texel.

    The pyramid

    Lance Williams, 1983. Each level is a half-size, prefiltered copy. Every level after the first adds a quarter of the one before it, so the whole chain costs the original texture plus a quarter plus a sixteenth plus a sixty-fourth — a geometric series that sums to just under one third extra, and creeps closer to a third the deeper the chain goes without ever reaching it. Under a third, whatever the texture: that is why mipmapping was affordable a decade before it was fast.

    07 · Light

    Faceted, then smooth,
    then actually made of something.

    A sphere made of flat triangles looks like a flat-triangle sphere. Gouraud's answer in 1971 was to light the corners and interpolate the colour; Phong's in 1973 was to interpolate the normal and light every pixel, which costs far more and looks far better. Fifty years later the arithmetic changed its allegiance: instead of tuning an exponent until the highlight looked right, you describe the surface — how rough it is, whether it is metal — and the reflection follows from that.

    Try

    • Select Gouraud, then drag the triangle count down to 3.
    • Use ⇄ compare to flick between Gouraud and Phong at 300 triangles.
    • Untick sRGB output on any model and watch it go chalky.

    The mistake everybody made for twenty years

    Light adds linearly; screens do not display linearly. A pixel value of 0.5 in an sRGB image is not half the light — it is about 21% of it. For two decades renderers added, multiplied and blended in the display's own crooked units, which is why so much of that era looks either washed out or muddy in the midtones, and why half-covered antialiased edges came out too dark. Turn the checkbox above off and the sphere goes chalky: that is the whole of the bug, in one control.

    08 · Parallel

    Not fast.
    Just very, very wide.

    A GPU core is slower than a CPU core and much worse at anything unpredictable. It wins by running the same instruction across a fixed group of lanes at once — 32 of them on Nvidia hardware, a 'warp' — and by keeping so many groups in flight that memory latency never stalls the machine. Both facts have a price, and both prices are visible in the two instruments below.

    Try

    • Set the slider to 0% and step to line 03.
    • Untick the quad grouping and step through it again.
    • Starve the scheduler: one resident warp, 30 cycles of latency.

    Two things happen as you push the sliders. Too few warps and the machine idles through every fetch. Enough warps and the arithmetic units never stop — at which point the shader's register count, not its instruction count, becomes the thing that limits you.

    09 · Rays

    The other way round.
    Start at the eye and ask.

    Rasterization takes each triangle and asks which pixels it covers. Ray tracing takes each pixel and asks which triangle it hits — which is slower for the first, visible surface and better at everything else: what a mirror sees, what light reaches a point, what is standing behind the camera. Both diagrams here are top-down slices, so you can see the rays themselves.

    Try

    • Drag the object count from 6 to 220 and watch the speed-up.
    • Now watch the object-test count while you do it. It barely moves.
    • Switch the hierarchy off and drag the count again.
    Arrow keys move

    Noise falls as the square root of the sample count, which is a brutal exchange rate: to halve the grain you must take four times as many samples. Real-time ray tracing cannot afford more than a handful, so it takes a noisy picture and hands it to a denoiser — and that denoiser, increasingly, is a neural network trained on the difference between the noisy image and the right answer.

    10 · Today

    The same seven jobs.
    Negotiable at last.

    Here is the claim from section 01, tested. The same seven jobs, at three moments fifty years apart. Nothing was added to the list and nothing was removed from it — the work simply changed hands, twice, and in the same direction each time: out of software, into wired silicon, and then back out into programs you write.

    The same jobs, three times

    And then it started moving back

    The tidy version of this story ends in 2006, with the pipeline dissolved into a pool of programmable cores and the fixed-function boxes reduced to a rasterizer, a texture unit and a blender. It has not stayed tidy.

    Rasterization has partly returned to software. Unreal Engine 5's Nanite rasterizes triangles smaller than a pixel in a compute shader, because the hardware rasterizer shades in 2×2 quads and a sub-pixel triangle wastes three quarters of one. The oldest fixed-function box on the chip turns out to have a size below which it is the wrong tool.

    Ray tracing does not use the boxes in this order at all. It has its own fixed-function unit, added in 2018, that answers a different question: not which pixels does this triangle cover but what does this ray hit. Section 09's diagram is not a stage of the pipeline on this page. It is a second pipeline, running beside it, and every modern frame is a negotiation between the two.

    And the last box is being questioned too. What you finally see is increasingly not what was rasterized: it is a low-resolution, noisy, temporally-accumulated estimate handed to a neural network that reconstructs the rest. The pipeline still ends in a framebuffer. It is no longer obvious that the framebuffer is the picture.

    Sixty years, four re-homings of the same seven jobs, and one thing unchanged throughout: somewhere underneath, something is still asking every pixel whether all three edge functions came out positive.

    The nine mechanisms, in one line each

    1. — ten stages, fixed in order since 1974.
    2. — perspective is a division by w; the matrix only sets it up.
    3. — three linear functions, one sign test each, per pixel.
    4. — stop sorting objects; ask each pixel instead.
    5. — divide by w again, then prefilter, or it swims and it shimmers.
    6. — interpolate the normal, not the colour; then conserve energy.
    7. — 32 lanes, one instruction pointer, and both sides of every branch.
    8. — a tree makes the nearest-surface question nearly free of scene size.

    11 · Sources

    Where each claim comes from,
    and what these models leave out.

    Everything specific on this page — a date, a number, an attribution — is listed below against the paper, specification or product document it came from. The renderer that draws the diagrams is real, but it is not a graphics card.

    What is simplified here, deliberately

    • The renderer is a model, not a simulator. It performs the same operations in the same order, but no GPU works this way internally: real hardware rasterizes hierarchically in tiles, compresses depth and colour, batches memory into cache lines, and reorders almost everything it legally can.
    • The pipeline diagram is the logical order, not the physical one. Hardware runs depth before shading whenever it can prove that is safe, keeps thousands of fragments in flight, and only guarantees that the result matches the order the specification describes.
    • One light, no shadows, no global illumination in the shading instrument. The point there is the difference between the shading models, which is easiest to see with a single light.
    • The warp diagram uses one warp of 32 lanes. AMD's wavefronts have been 64 lanes and, since RDNA, 32; Intel's are configurable. The consequence of divergence is the same in every case.
    • The ray tracing views are two-dimensional slices. A real bounding-volume hierarchy is three-dimensional, much deeper, and built with a surface-area heuristic that this page does not implement.
    • Dates are ship dates where a product is named and publication dates where a paper is. Where an idea was discovered more than once — the depth buffer, most famously — both are noted.
