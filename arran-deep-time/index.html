01 · The whole island is an outcrop

One island. Two geological countries.

Arran sits across the Highland Boundary Fault. Hard, ancient rocks and young granite build the mountainous north; younger sandstones and a volcanic centre make the broader, gentler south. It is often called “Scotland in miniature” — but the miniature is extraordinarily complete.

An interpretive map, simplified from the geological record — not a navigation map.

Palaeogene · intrusive igneous

North Arran Granite

Around 58 million years ago, a near-circular pluton rose into older Dalradian rocks. Its coarse outer granite and finer inner granite now carry Goatfell and the northern ridges.

ProcessMagma cooled slowly below ground
LandscapeHigh, jointed, glacially sharpened mountains
Look forPale granite, quartz, feldspar and mica

The dividing line. The Highland Boundary Fault cuts from north-east to south-west. It helps explain why the north feels Highland and the south Lowland — not just culturally, but in the rocks beneath your boots.

03 · Lochranza · 1787

The gap that made the Earth old.

James Hutton came looking for a boundary between kinds of rock. On Newton Shore he found an argument: steep older beds, then a broken and eroded surface, then gently inclined younger sandstone. The outcrop records events that cannot be squeezed into a short history.

Read the sequence

Mud becomes layered rock

About 520 million years ago, sediment accumulated on a deep ocean floor. Burial and metamorphism changed it into hard Dalradian metasediment.

  1. 1Deposit the older beds.
  2. 2Bury, heat, fold and tilt them.
  3. 3Raise and erode the tilted stack.
  4. 4Lay younger sediment over the cut surface.
~160 millionyears missing between the two rock packages
What the outcrop says

The lower Dalradian rocks are roughly 520 million years old; the overlying Kinnesswood sandstone is around 360 million years old. The boundary is not merely old rock touching younger rock. It is a machine for reasoning backwards: sedimentation, deep burial, deformation, uplift, erosion — and then sedimentation again.

04 · A cabinet without glass

Hold the island in six specimens.

A geological map can feel abstract. A hand specimen makes it physical: grains tell you how fast something cooled, bands preserve pressure, fossils reveal an environment, glass remembers a melt that froze too quickly to grow crystals.

05 · The latest great sculptor

Rock supplied the block. Ice cut the form.

The granite mountains existed before the Ice Age, but their famous silhouette did not. Repeated glaciers quarried fractured rock, deepened glens and used trapped debris as abrasive. When the ice left, it dumped till and the unloaded land slowly rebounded.

06 · Stone becomes tool, monument and livelihood

Geology enters human history.

People did not merely live on Arran’s geology. They selected it, exchanged it, farmed around it, cut it, mined it and built routes to move it. The same materials that record vanished worlds became the practical fabric of the island.

Selection, extraction and movement: geology became a network of choices.

c. 4000–2500 BC · Neolithic

A glassy stone travels

Arran pitchstone fractures to a razor edge. It becomes blades and flakes, then travels far beyond the island — evidence of exchange networks reaching Ireland, mainland Britain and Orkney.

08 · Arran UNESCO Global Geopark

Deep time becomes a modern responsibility.

On 17 April 2025 Arran was formally announced as the United Kingdom’s tenth UNESCO Global Geopark. The designation joins internationally significant geology to conservation, education, local culture and sustainable development.

Ground shapes habitat

Acidic granite and rhyolite support heath and peat; cliffs and rocky slopes create refuges for plants rare elsewhere. Geodiversity underpins biodiversity.

Evidence needs context

An outcrop, footprint or stone circle is more informative in place than detached. Conservation protects relationships: layer to layer, artefact to landscape, story to community.

A living economy

Geotourism turns scientific importance into year-round interpretation, walking and local enterprise — while periodic UNESCO revalidation asks the island to keep demonstrating good stewardship.

The final layer is ours

Arran’s rocks record worlds that vanished without witnesses. The modern layer is different: people can read what is happening, choose what to protect, and decide what evidence the next reader will inherit.
