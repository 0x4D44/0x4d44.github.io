Take a parabola. Feed its output back in as its input, over and over. That's the whole machine. Turn one knob and it will split, and split, and split again — faster each time — until it shatters into chaos. And hidden in the shattering is a number that shows up everywhere.

01 One rule, on repeat

A parabola, chasing its own tail

The logistic map was a toy model of a population: next year's size is this year's, times a growth rate r, times how much room is left. One line of arithmetic. In 1976 the ecologist Robert May pointed out it does something outrageous.

Pick a growth rate and iterate. The cobweb below traces the orbit: up to the curve (apply the rule), across to the diagonal (feed the answer back), repeat. For low r the orbit spirals into a single resting value — one stable population. Nudge r higher and that single point suddenly splits into a two-year boom-and-bust cycle. Higher still, four. Drag it.

02 The cascade

Every ending, at once

Instead of one r at a time, plot them all. For each growth rate across the bottom, let the orbit settle and mark where it lands. One curve becomes two, two become four, four become eight — the period-doubling cascade — and then, abruptly, a fog of chaos. The colour is the Lyapunov exponent: cyan where nearby orbits converge (order), magenta where they fly apart (chaos). Drag the line; the cobweb above follows.

03 The number in the cascade

The forks arrive by a fixed ratio

Measure where each doubling happens. Call the growth rates R₀, R₁, R₂…. The gaps between them shrink — and here's the thing — they shrink by very nearly the same factor every time. Take the ratio of one gap to the next and it settles down fast. In 1978 Mitchell Feigenbaum found it converges to a specific constant, δ ≈ 4.6692016… Reveal the doublings and watch the ratio home in.

04 The punchline

The number was never about the parabola

Here is why δ is famous. Throw the logistic map away. Use a completely different formula — a sine arch, a downward parabola in another disguise — anything with a single smooth hump. Run the same cascade, measure the same ratio. You get the same number. δ doesn't live in the equation; it lives in the shape of the route to chaos itself. Swap the map and see.

This is why physicists cared. The same δ was then measured in the lab — in dripping taps, oscillating circuits, convecting fluids, heartbeats — systems sharing no equations with a toy population model, yet doubling their way into turbulence at the very same rate.

05 What it means

Simple rules are not simple behaviour

The lesson that rearranged a field: you do not need a complicated system to get complicated, unpredictable behaviour. One parabola will do it.

Chaos here is fully deterministic — no randomness anywhere, every step exactly computed. Yet past the accumulation point the orbit is unpredictable in practice, because two starting values a hair apart diverge exponentially (that's exactly what the positive Lyapunov exponent measures). Determinism and predictability, long assumed to be the same thing, quietly part ways.

The elegance and simplicity of the model is deceptive… even simple deterministic systems can exhibit dynamics of astonishing complexity. after Robert May, "Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics", 1976

And threaded through the mess, a strange order: universality. When a system falls into chaos by period-doubling, it does so on a schedule set by δ — the same for a parabola, a faucet, and a heart. A little corner of the unpredictable turns out to be exactly, measurably lawful.
