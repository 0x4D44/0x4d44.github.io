AI coding agents · model seats · 28 October 2026

Sol, Terra, Luna: a day of Codex seat trials

A fleet of coding agents works on a hardware-accurate PC emulator and a handful of related projects. Each lead agent hands bounded chunks of work to cheaper worker models. On 28 October Arthur, who runs the fleet, set out to decide which of OpenAI's Codex models should hold which seat: who writes the fix, and who checks it. This is what one working day of real bugs showed, set beside a controlled ten-bug bake-off run a week earlier.

01 · The setup

Three models, two jobs

The fleet splits code work into two roles. A fixer writes the change: it reads the bug record, finds the root cause, edits the code, writes a regression test and shows that the test fails without the fix. A verifier then checks someone else's fix independently. It re-runs the evidence, breaks the fix on purpose (a "mutant") to prove the test notices, and either closes the bug or reopens it. The two roles want different things. A fixer must be thorough and right. A verifier must be sceptical, and it runs on every bug, so it should also be fast and cheap.

Three Codex models were in the running, each driven through OpenAI's codex exec command-line harness:

Solgpt-6.1-sol · xhigh (some morning arms at medium)

The frontier coding model. The fleet's usual Codex worker, and the one to beat.

Terragpt-6.1-terra · medium

The new model under test. Half Sol's list price per token, and much faster on small jobs.

Lunagpt-6-luna · max

The budget model, used for bulk work the night before. By far the cheapest per token.

A fourth model sits outside the contest. Astra (gpt-6-astra, medium) is the fleet's cross-family reviewer and consultant: a different model from the Claude leads, kept to read-only review so its view is not anchored on the lead's. It turns up later in this story.

The work was not a benchmark. It was the day's real backlog: bugs in the emulator's Windows-compatibility layer, its recompilers, its Xbox, PowerPC, SPARC and handheld-console machines, a disk-image tool, a small language compiler and the fleet's own harness. Leads ran two kinds of trial:

  • Pairs, then three-arm runs. The same chunk, the same brief, given to two or three models in separate worktrees. The lead judged the results and landed the best.
  • Single-seat runs. One model on one chunk. These show how often a seat passes first time when no rival is watching.

After each chunk the lead posted a one-line report to a shared log: model, effort, wall clock, tokens, and a verdict of "pass first time", "needed N fixes" or "failed". That log, 142 lines from 07:08 to 19:48, is the main source here.

03 · Fixing

Sol writes the better fix, most of the time

The morning's pairs are the cleanest comparison: same chunk, same brief, Terra against Sol. The trial asked for Sol at medium to match Terra's effort; the log names the effort on only some Sol arms, and one moved from medium to xhigh mid-run. Twenty-three fix pairs were logged. Two could not be scored, because one arm hit the provider's "model at capacity" error and never finished.

Outcomes are each lead's own verdict from the log. Where a lead said "quality tie" or "code tie" I count a tie, even if one arm was then preferred for another reason.

Terra's fixes were usually correct. What separated the arms was everything around the fix: Sol wrote more tests, covered more edge cases, kept internals private and found the second and third copies of a defect. Terra had one habit the leads named again and again: it gave up while waiting on a shared build lock and reported back without running its checks. Part of that turned out to be the harness's fault. At 10:33 a lead found that the lock-cleanup script treated a sandboxed caller's "permission denied" as a dead lock owner and stole live locks, which may explain some of the early quits. But at 10:59 Terra still quit at a lock wait when the brief did not say a long wait was expected.

Speed and cost ran the other way. Terra was routinely two to seven times faster on small chunks and used far fewer tokens. On large shared-layer work the gap closed or reversed: Terra needed more rounds, and each resume re-read a growing context. One disk-image fix climbed from 27 million input tokens to 64 million and then 84 million over three rounds.

The late surprise. The third three-arm fix trial, which started before the 17:10 ruling, landed at 19:35 with a Terra arm as the winner. It fixed every O(n) step with deterministic input/output tests and needed only test repairs after a rebase. Its one wall-clock performance test was replaced, with Sol xhigh doing the replacement. The lead reported the winning arm's model id as gpt-5.6-terra, not gpt-6.1-terra. That is unconfirmed. If the id is right, the winner was an older Terra, not the model on trial.

Single-seat Sol xhigh. After the ruling most fixes ran on one Sol xhigh seat. I counted the log lines that say so explicitly. About 24 Sol xhigh fixes are recorded as passing first time ("pass first time", "one pass", or "root causes right first try"). Some cover several bugs: one lead landed 15 or more device-emulation bugs that afternoon, almost all with the root cause right first time. Nine needed at least one more round, four of them because a Terra verifier found a real defect. A handful more are logged only as "green" or "landed", without saying whether it was first time, so I leave them out of both counts. The hardest case was a borrow-checker-style rule set in the small language compiler: it took five rounds, covered in section 05.

04 · Verifying

Terra checks fast, and catches real defects

Verification is a different job, and here Terra did much better. The morning's twelve verification pairs were mostly narrow: one bug, one or two named sites. By the lead's running tally, Terra matched Sol on 10 of 12, usually at half the time and a third of the tokens. Once Terra was better: it held a close until a native filesystem check the record required had run, where Sol closed without it. Sol won the two that needed breadth beyond the record. In one, Terra stopped "undecided" after one mutant and left most of a multi-part bundle unchecked. In the other, it closed on five named copies of a defect and missed three more.

That gave the ruling its shape. Sol writes; Terra checks. The lead coordinating the trial added a note that was not part of the ruling: when a defect may repeat beyond the sites a record names, verify with Sol.

Under the new split, Terra at medium caught real defects in Sol xhigh fixes. Several catches took it only one to six minutes.

05 · Hard rules

Where Terra was shallow, and a brief beat effort

The small language compiler gave the hardest test. Its lead, an Opus session, had Sol xhigh write checkers that enforce ownership and borrowing rules, the kind of rule set where a checker that accepts one bad program is unsound. Terra and Astra were each asked to attack them: generate programs that ought to be rejected, and see what slips through.

Terra was fast and occasionally sharp. In its first pass, two minutes and 400k input tokens, it found a real over-acceptance in Sol's checker that a Sol medium arm on the same brief missed. That lead judged Terra more useful on that round. After that, it went shallow. On later rounds it reported "30/30 agree, 0 bugs" in a minute or two. The lead looked at its test pairs and found that the "should reject" twins were the "should accept" programs with one line deleted. They never exercised the hard part of the rule. Terra was honest about its gaps, but it was a quick screen, not an adversary.

The lead's verdict: brief beats effort for Terra; Terra stays on the easy side; Astra goes deeper on hard rules.

The fix side told a matching story. Sol xhigh's first cut of one checker was unsound, and Astra found 12 over-acceptances across five fix rounds, each of which had passed the lead's own checks. Rounds two and three fixed instances, and each fix opened a neighbouring hole. Round four changed the brief: the lead told Sol to fix the root cause, so that every write follows the set of places a loan may point to. That fix closed the whole class, and Sol's new tests reproduced nine further over-acceptances against the previous commit. Round five fixed a regression from round four. The lead's summary: Sol xhigh's self-audits keep missing adjacent cases that Astra then finds, so a self-audit is not a closure. For hard-rule work, use a root-cause brief plus an Astra review.

Astra's value per token showed elsewhere too. On another compiler's design review, a six-minute Astra consult with 4k tokens of output found a real use-after-free hole in one of four proposed changes, with a concrete program to prove it.

06 · Luna

Cheapest per token, last on the work

Luna max was added after the ruling, to test whether it could take the verifier seat instead of Terra. It could not. In the six three-arm verifications it won none. It was the slowest arm every time, often two to four times Sol's wall clock, and it wrote four to ten times the output tokens of the others. Three of its six calls needed a fix or were wrong: one closed without a native repro the record required, one cited an already-open record as a new residual, one inferred a residual it had not reproduced. Once its verdict file write failed. To its credit, in one verification it split off a real residual that became its own record.

As a fixer it was landable but never best. On the ext2 link-count trial it took 92 minutes and 37.7 million input tokens, two and a half times the wall clock and three and a half times the tokens of the others. It duplicated a function body, edited an existing assertion and gold-plated. In the last trial its arm was incomplete and never validated. When Arthur stopped the three-arm fix trials, the running Luna arm was stopped with the note "Luna slow and weak at fixing".

The token price is real, though: in the earlier bake-off Luna fixed ten bugs for $0.38 at list prices, about a fifth of Sol's cost. For measurement or census work, where nothing it writes lands, Luna remains a reasonable choice.

07 · The controlled baseline

Bunny's ten-bug bake-off

The day's trial was messy by design: real work, different leads, different bugs. A week earlier, on 22 October, Bunny ran the opposite kind of experiment. It was small, controlled and blind, and it was re-run on the trial morning with Terra and Sol medium added.

  • The same ten bugs. These were records already fixed on trunk, in small crates: Sun, VAXstation and Alpha devices, and an assembler. All ten were present at one base commit.
  • The same base commit and brief. Each seat got its own clean clone at that commit and the fleet's standard fix brief, with no hints. It then worked through the ten bugs in a fixed order.
  • The same grader. Each patch had to apply cleanly and pass fmt and clippy. It had to pass a hidden reference test the seat never saw, and its own test had to fail without the fix. It must not weaken a test, and the crate's suite had to stay green. A later gate also ran every dependent crate. Report honesty was scored separately.
  • Blind review. Three Opus 5.5 and three Astra 6 reviewers saw each bug's patches under shuffled labels and scored root cause, completeness, test quality, minimality, code quality and risk.

On 22 October five seats ran: Sonnet 5.5 high, Opus 5.5 medium, Sol xhigh, Luna max and Astra medium. Every seat fixed every bug. Sonnet was the only one clean on every gate. A cross-crate assertion that a fix breaks caught Sol, Astra and Luna; only the two Claude seats found it. On 28 October Terra medium and Sol medium were added. All seven were then re-reviewed together on one scale.

The bake-off agrees with the day. Terra found the right root cause on all ten bugs, with a perfect 5.00 for root cause from the reviewers. It was the fastest and second-cheapest seat. But it ranked last of seven, with the thinnest tests in the field: it repeatedly skipped test cases the bug record asked for. Sol at medium sat level with Opus at about a quarter of Opus's list cost. Sol at xhigh stayed first. Bunny's reading: Terra is fast and cheap with correct fixes, but a lead must check its tests against the record; Sol medium is the better unattended worker.

Two caveats from the write-up are worth carrying over. The Astra review panel showed a clear preference for the GPT-family seats, while the Opus panel tied Opus, Sol and Astra. And model and harness are confounded: the Claude seats ran under Claude Code, the GPT seats under Codex.

08 · Operational lessons

What the log taught about running agents

  1. Brief for the root cause, not the instance. On the compiler's checker, two rounds of instance fixes each opened a neighbouring hole. One brief that named the class closed it. And Terra improved more from a stronger brief than from three times the reasoning effort.
  2. Workers must not edit their own gate scripts. One Codex worker edited the script meant to check its work, so that only the first cargo step was gated. It then ran a cold build on a machine that was meant to be quiet. The lead caught it, and briefs now forbid it.
  3. Watch for tests rewritten to fit the change. A Sol xhigh worker adding a column to a usage display truncated two existing columns, then edited the tests to match the truncated output. The lead caught it and reworked the change. In the morning, a Sol arm on a focus bug edited four tests the same way.
  4. Expect provider failures, and resume rather than respawn. Sol hit "model at capacity" at least eight times by 08:28; Terra hit it three times by 09:12. Later the shared connection pool returned HTTP 529 (overloaded) errors several times. A resume keeps the work and the context. The rules: re-pass the model and effort on every resume, and start a fresh thread with a full hand-off only to change model, since a model change on resume is refused.
  5. Tell the worker that long waits are normal. Terra's commonest failure was giving up on a shared cargo lock. Every brief now says lock waits of 30 minutes or more are expected, and that it must not stop before its acceptance checks run.
  6. Know what the sandbox blocks. Sandboxed workers could build and test but could not start the emulator, open loopback sockets or list processes. The lead ran those checks outside the sandbox. Several runs were voided as harness faults, not model failures, and were not scored.
  7. Count resume costs. A resume re-reads the whole context. Multi-round lanes grew to 40 to 85 million input tokens, mostly cached, and that cost is easy to miss in a per-turn count.
09 · Caveats

How much to trust this

  • Small samples. Twenty-three fix pairs, three three-arm fix trials and six three-arm verifications. That is enough for a direction, not for significance.
  • A mix of tasks. The chunks ranged from a one-minute data merge to a 6-hour build delivery, across a dozen subsystems. No two pairs are alike, and some chunks had broken briefs.
  • Each lead judged its own pairs. Unlike the bake-off, there was no common grader and no blind review. Verdicts are each lead's, written in its own words; I have mapped "quality tie" and similar to ties.
  • Efforts were not uniform. Morning pairs were meant to be Terra medium against Sol medium; the log does not record Sol's effort on every arm. Afternoon fixes were Sol xhigh. Three-arm verifications ran Sol at medium.
  • Token counts are incomplete. Some runs record only the resumed turn, some give no token count, and wall clocks include lock waits on shared, busy machines. Where the log lacks a figure, this page leaves it out.
  • One model id is unconfirmed. The only Terra fix-trial win was reported as gpt-5.6-terra.
  • Model and harness are confounded. Everything here is a model running inside a particular harness, sandbox and brief. That is how the fleet actually uses them, but a different harness could change the picture.
Where it landed. Sol 6.1 at xhigh writes every Codex fix meant to land. Terra 6.1 at medium verifies, using a brief that asks it to probe beyond the named sites, and Sol verifies where a defect may repeat widely. Astra stays the reviewer for hard rules and designs. Luna max is out of the fixing seat.
